# Whooping cough (pertussis) in children — clinical management

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** diagnosis, antibiotic treatment, admission, exclusion and contact prophylaxis for pertussis in children. Vaccination is covered separately in `pertussis` (Australian Immunisation Handbook), which has no treatment content; the pertussis row of `empiric-antimicrobials-children` gives azithromycin only.

> ✅ **OPEN AND QUOTED.** 25 of 25 claims quoted verbatim from the **RCH Melbourne Clinical Practice Guideline *Whooping cough (pertussis)*** (Last updated May 2025); **40 fragments re-checked by machine. 3 quoted doses.**

> ⚠️ **Infants under 6 months: consider admission for all — they can deteriorate rapidly** (apnoea, bradycardia, cyanosis). Treat early; notify every suspected or confirmed case.

---

## Recognise it

- ⚠️ **Pertussis is notifiable in Australia — notify every suspected or confirmed case.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Infants ≤6 months are at greatest risk** of apnoea, bradycardia, severe pneumonia, seizures and encephalopathy, and are **most often infected by family members.** [S1]
- **Immunised children can get it, usually less severely.** Immunity wanes 4–12 years after the last dose; neither vaccine nor infection gives lifelong protection. [S1]
- **Incubation 4–21 days (usually 7–10).** Infectious from symptom onset until the earliest of 21 days after onset, 14 days after paroxysmal cough began, or 5 days of appropriate antibiotics. [S1]
- **Phases:** catarrhal (1–2 weeks; coryza, dry cough) → paroxysmal (1–2 weeks; paroxysms peaking after about a week) → convalescent (2–6 weeks, up to 3 months). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Infants may have apnoea, bradycardia or cyanosis with coughing spasms.** The classic whoop and post-tussive vomiting are more common in unvaccinated children. [S1]
- **Clues:** other family members coughing (over 70% of household contacts are infected); fever is uncommon and clinical signs are usually absent. [S1]

## Investigate

- **Nasopharyngeal flocked swab for PCR is the test of choice**; it is usually negative after 21 days, or 5–7 days into effective antibiotics. **Serology is not clinically useful.** [S1]
- **Culture** (the most specific test, allows susceptibility testing) for: catarrhal or early paroxysmal phase · severe cases needing admission · suspected treatment failure · public health request. [S1]
- **No routine bloods;** FBE may help as a severity marker under 3 months (thrombocytosis signals a poorer prognosis). **CXR only in unwell infants under 6 months.** [S1]

## Treat

- **Treat if:** catarrhal or early paroxysmal phase · cough under 14 days or symptoms under 21 days · admitted · severe symptoms or complications. Antibiotics started in that window reduce severity and transmission. [S1]
- **Azithromycin:** under 6 months 10 mg/kg orally daily for 5 days; 6 months and over 10 mg/kg (max 500 mg) on day 1, then 5 mg/kg (max 250 mg) daily on days 2–5. [S1]
- **Or clarithromycin** (1 month and over): 7.5 mg/kg (max 500 mg) orally bd for 7 days. [S1]
- **Macrolide contraindicated or resistant:** trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (8/40 mg/mL), over 6 weeks of age, 0.5 mL/kg (max 20 mL) orally bd for 7 days. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do not use erythromycin** — risk of pyloric stenosis in infants. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Consider admission for:** all infants under 6 months · respiratory distress · inability to feed · pneumonia · cyanosis or apnoea, with or without coughing · seizures. **Droplet precautions.** [S1]
- **Exclude cases from school, childcare and contact outside the home** (especially with infants) until the earliest of 21 days from onset, 14 days from paroxysmal cough, or 5 days of appropriate antibiotics. [S1]

## Contacts

- **Contacts get the same antibiotic regimens as cases**; the aim is to protect infants under 6 months. Immunocompromised contacts: case by case. [S1]
- **Close contacts** (exposed in the first 21 days of the case's illness): household and family · anyone who stayed overnight in the same room · face-to-face within 1 metre for over 1 hour (shorter for neonates). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Treat within 14 days of first contact (21 days for infants under 6 months):** any exposed infant under 6 months · any exposed woman in her last month of pregnancy — in any setting. [S1]
- **Also treat contacts who may pass it to an infant under 6 months:** household members where such an infant lives · healthcare staff working with infants or pregnant women · all children and staff in a childcare centre caring for such infants. [S1]
- **Unimmunised or incompletely immunised (under 3 doses) contacts under 7 years: exclude for 14 days** from last exposure, or until 5 days of appropriate antibiotics. [S1]
- **Vaccinate:** cases still complete the schedule; close contacts not up to date get DTPa or dTpa as soon as possible after exposure; adults without a pertussis vaccine in the last 10 years should be offered one. [S1]

## Escalate and discharge

- **Involve the paediatric team** for any infant ≤6 months, or severe disease with apnoea, cyanosis, pneumonia, seizures or encephalopathy. [S1]
- **Discharge when** coughing episodes pass without hypoxia or bradycardia and fluid intake is adequate — most admitted infants still have paroxysms after discharge. [S1]
