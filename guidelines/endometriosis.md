## ⚠️ The diagnostic change

> ⚠️ **Laparoscopy should no longer be considered the gold standard for diagnosis of endometriosis** — but
> **should be considered if imaging is negative and/or empirical treatment is ineffective or
> inappropriate** [S1].

> ⚠️ **A normal exam and imaging CANNOT EXCLUDE endometriosis** [S1].
>
> ⚠️ **Biopsy should be taken at the time of laparoscopy. However, a negative histological result does not
> rule out endometriosis** [S1].

⚠️ **Neither a normal scan, nor a normal laparoscopy, nor a negative biopsy excludes the diagnosis.**
**People with suspected endometriosis should be referred to a gynaecologist for further consideration of
laparoscopy** [S1].

**Biomarkers:** ⚠️ **there are no biomarkers with sufficient sensitivity or specificity to warrant use in
the workup of endometriosis** — **all three guidelines state that biomarkers should not be used** [S1].

## The delay

> **Affecting one in nine Australian women and up to 50% of women experiencing infertility** [S1].
>
> ⚠️ **The average delay in the diagnosis of endometriosis in Australian women has been reported to be
> between seven and 12 years** [S1].

**An estimated 23,400 endometriosis-related hospitalisations and a cost to the economy of $9.3 billion each
year due to loss of quality of life and reduced productivity** [S1].

⚠️ **Uncertainty and delays in diagnosis contribute to the psychological burden experienced by those with
the condition** [S1].

**Symptoms vary considerably and do not always relate to extent of disease. Patients may be asymptomatic or
have pelvic pain, menstrual changes (eg heavy bleeding), bowel symptoms, fatigue or infertility** [A1].

## Classification

**The revised American Society of Reproductive Medicine staging system is the most recognised** — ⚠️ **it
does not predict the severity of symptoms or prognosis** [S1].

**Also classified as superficial peritoneal disease, ovarian endometriosis, and deep infiltrating
endometriosis (DIE)** — **lesions invading >5 mm beyond the surface of the peritoneum**, often involving
**bladder, bowel, pelvic wall and retroperitoneal structures** [S1].

⚠️ **Studies on endometriosis confirmed laparoscopically in adolescents demonstrated that ALL STAGES can be
present** [S1] — youth is not a reason to defer.

## Treatment

**Individualise according to age, symptoms, extent of disease and pregnancy plans** [A1]. ⚠️ **Hormonal
therapies should be avoided in those wishing to conceive** [S1].

### Analgesia — weaker than it looks

| Source | On NSAIDs and paracetamol |
|---|---|
| **A1** | **NSAIDs are first line for pain relief and can be used with other treatments**, ⚠️ **although evidence is inconclusive regarding their effect on pain due to endometriosis.** **Paracetamol may be used with, or as an alternative to, NSAIDs but evidence for effectiveness is lacking** |
| **S1** | ⚠️ **the evidence is limited to ONE SMALL randomised controlled trial using naproxen, for which the results were inconclusive.** **The guidelines suggest a SHORT TRIAL** — and **if they do not provide adequate pain relief, other management options should be considered and the patient should be referred** |

⚠️ **Both sources describe the analgesic evidence as inconclusive. The instruction is to trial briefly and
move on, not to persist.**

**Neuromodulators:** ⚠️ **there are no studies evaluating the effectiveness of anti-neuropathic medications
for endometriosis-related pain**; RANZCOG **suggests they might be used as a broader chronic pain
management strategy following referral to a pain or condition-specific specialist** [S1].

### ⚠️ Hormonal therapy — with doses, from the open source

> **These hormonal therapies have been shown to lead to a clinically significant reduction in dysmenorrhoea
> and non-menstrual pelvic pain, WITHOUT ANY DEMONSTRATING CLEAR SUPERIORITY** [S1].
>
> **All have similar efficacy in reducing pain but they differ in terms of adverse effects and cost** [A1].

⚠️ **Because nothing is superior, the choice is made on adverse effects, contraception needs, cost and
patient preference — not on efficacy.**

| Drug | Route | Contraceptive? | Dose [S1] |
|---|---|---|---|
| **COC** | **oral, vaginal ring or transdermal patch** | **yes** | **cyclical or continuous**; ⚠️ **continuous further reduces dysmenorrhoea**. ⚠️ **No evidence of superiority of any one preparation** |
| **Levonorgestrel 52 mg (IUD)** | **intrauterine** | **yes** | ⚠️ **replace 5 yearly** — see *Unresolved* |
| **Medroxyprogesterone 150 mg** | **IM** | **yes** | **injection 3 monthly** |
| **Etonogestrel 68 mg** | **subdermal implant** | **yes** | **replace 3 yearly** |
| **Medroxyprogesterone** | **oral** | ⚠️ **NO** | **10 mg 3 times daily** |
| **Dienogest** | **oral** | ⚠️ **NO** | **2 mg once daily** |
| **Norethisterone** | **oral** | **yes** | **5–10 mg once daily** |
| **Drospirenone** | **oral** | **yes** | **4 mg once daily** — ⚠️ **currently off-label for endometriosis** |
| ⚠️ **Goserelin** | **subcutaneous** | ⚠️ **NO** | **3.6 mg injection 4 weekly** — ⚠️ **maximum 6 months, specialist supervision** |
| ⚠️ **Nafarelin** | **intranasal** | ⚠️ **NO** | **200 µg twice daily** — ⚠️ **maximum 6 months, specialist supervision** |

> ⚠️ **Four of these are NOT contraceptive: oral medroxyprogesterone, dienogest, goserelin and nafarelin.**
> **A1 is explicit that an effective NON-HORMONAL method of contraception must be used during treatment
> with dienogest**, and **during GnRH agonist treatment, to avoid pregnancy in the event of missed
> doses** [A1].

**First line:** **all three guidelines recommend offering the COCP or a progestogen to people with
suspected, confirmed or recurrent endometriosis** [S1] — ⚠️ **note "suspected"; treatment does not wait for
a diagnosis.**

**GnRH agonists:** ⚠️ **ESHRE considers them SECOND-LINE; RANZCOG recommends them ONLY as an adjunct to
surgery for DIE involving bowel, bladder or ureters** [S1]. **Their use requires specialist advice** [S1].

**Bone:** **hypo-estrogenic adverse effects including decreased BMD limit treatment to 6 months** [A1].
**Adding combined MHT (add-back therapy) reduces these adverse effects and protects against BMD loss while
maintaining efficacy, which may allow longer treatment** [A1]. ⚠️ **BMD usually recovers within 2 years of
discontinuation** [S1].

**Danazol:** ⚠️ **androgenic adverse effects (eg hirsutism, voice change) limit its use; other treatments
are preferred. Maximum duration of treatment is 9 months** [A1]. ⚠️ **S1's table does not include danazol at
all.**

### Surgery

**Generally performed via laparoscopy**, aiming to **remove endometrial deposits and correct distortions to
anatomy**, with **an overall improvement in most health-related quality of life domains** [S1].

⚠️ **Major surgical complications are higher when DIE is present, particularly involving the bowel** —
**refer women with DIE to a centre of expertise or a multidisciplinary team** [S1].

**Endometriomas:** **cystectomy is recommended by all three guidelines as it reduces the risk of recurrence
and pain and increases spontaneous pregnancy rates compared to ablation** — ⚠️ **but this needs to be
carefully weighed against its effect on ovarian reserve, especially in young women not immediately seeking
fertility** [S1].

> ⚠️ **Surgery is not a cure. Relapse of symptoms occurs in 40–45% of women, and up to 30% are readmitted
> for surgery within five years of their first surgery** [S1]. **Symptom recurrence following surgery or
> hormonal treatment is common** [A1].

**Afterwards:** **postoperative hormonal suppression via LNG-IUD or continuous COCP might have some benefit
for secondary prevention of pain and disease recurrence, and all three guidelines recommend considering
hormonal treatment following laparoscopy** [S1].

**Hysterectomy** **might be considered if women have completed their family and medical and surgical
interventions have been ineffective, or where there is concurrent heavy menstrual bleeding and/or
adenomyosis**, **removing all visible lesions at the same time** [S1]. ⚠️ **When considering bilateral
oophorectomy, discuss the increased risk of diminished bone density, dementia and cardiovascular
disease** [S1]. ⚠️ **Pain symptoms might persist or recur due to persisting or recurring endometriosis
and/or central sensitisation** [S1].

## ⚠️ Where the sources part: surgery for infertility

| Source | Says |
|---|---|
| ⚠️ **A1 (AMH 2026)** | ⚠️ **surgery is the treatment of choice for infertility associated with endometriosis; hormonal treatments do not improve fertility and can delay conception** |
| ⚠️ **S1 (AJGP 2024, reporting three guidelines)** | **the three guidelines agree that offering surgical management of rASRM STAGE 1 AND 2 endometriosis MIGHT improve fertility outcomes.** ⚠️ **The data for fertility outcomes in surgical management of STAGES 3 AND 4 are LESS CLEAR** |

⚠️ **A1 makes a flat recommendation; S1 reports a stage-dependent and hedged one.** ⚠️ **This guideline does
not reconcile them.** **What they agree on:** ⚠️ **hormonal suppression should NOT be offered
postoperatively when fertility is a main priority** [S1]; **hormonal treatments do not improve fertility and
can delay conception** [A1].

**Assisted reproduction** [S1]: **IUI with ovarian stimulation might be performed in stage 1 and 2, as
pregnancy rates are higher than expectant management or IUI alone. IVF is recommended if tubal function is
compromised, there is male factor infertility or other treatments have failed.** ⚠️ **ART is safe to use in
women with endometriosis — there is no difference in recurrence rates.**

⚠️ **Refer people experiencing infertility or subfertility to a specialist with an interest in
endometriosis-related infertility** [S1].

**Pregnancy:** ⚠️ **endometriosis might increase the risk of first trimester miscarriage and ectopic
pregnancy**; **pregnancy has a variable effect on lesions — they might regress or grow**; and ⚠️ **people
should NOT be advised to become pregnant for the primary purpose of treating endometriosis** [S1].

## After menopause

⚠️ **Two to five per cent of people will experience endometriosis-related symptoms after menopause**, and
⚠️ **differential diagnoses such as malignancy need to be considered when postmenopausal people present
with pelvic pain** [S1].

> ⚠️ **A systematic review found that people with a history of endometriosis might experience REACTIVATION
> when treating menopausal symptoms with MHT, as well as an increased risk of MALIGNANT TRANSFORMATION of
> the lesions — the latter found particularly with oestrogen-only preparations.** **ESHRE recommends
> combined oestrogen–progesterone formulations be used, INCLUDING IN PEOPLE WHO HAVE HAD A
> HYSTERECTOMY** [S1].

⚠️ **That reverses the usual rule.** Oestrogen-only MHT is standard after hysterectomy; a history of
endometriosis is a reason not to.

## Referral and care model

⚠️ **Refer young people aged 17 years and under to a paediatric and adolescent gynaecologist with an
interest in endometriosis, or a gynaecologist comfortable treating adolescents** [S1].

⚠️ **People with endometriosis meet the criteria for a chronic disease management plan** [S1].
**Specialist guidance is recommended** [A1].
