## ⚠️ Refer before you treat

> ⚠️ **Urgently refer any patient with a red eye who also has eye pain, photophobia or visual disturbance,
> or who wears contact lenses, to an ophthalmologist or optometrist for further assessment** [A1].

**Conjunctivitis is typically a non-sight-threatening, self-limiting condition that needs to be
distinguished from sight-threatening conditions such as gonorrhoea, chlamydia, herpes simplex virus (HSV),
uveitis and microbial keratitis** [S1].

| Emergency | Why | Action |
|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Gonococcal conjunctivitis** | ⚠️ **can lead to corneal melt and perforation** [S1]; **corneal ulceration and perforation that can lead to blindness** [A1] | ⚠️ **1 g IV or IM ceftriaxone immediately**, **1 g oral azithromycin** to cover chlamydia, **same-day ophthalmology review** [S1] |
| ⚠️ **Microbial keratitis** | **serious bacterial infection of the cornea that can be sight threatening** [S1] | ⚠️ **same-day referral to an ophthalmologist** [S1] |
| ⚠️ **HSV keratitis (dendrite on fluorescein)** | sight-threatening | ⚠️ **refer all new cases to an ophthalmologist within 24 hours** [A1]. ⚠️ **NEVER use corticosteroids for herpes simplex epithelial keratitis, proven or suspected** [A1] |
| ⚠️ **Any contact lens wearer** | ⚠️ **contact lens wear is the most common risk factor for microbial keratitis, particularly overnight wear or inadequate disinfection** [S1] | **refer to an optometrist or ophthalmologist to exclude MK or marginal keratitis** [S1] |
| **Neonatal conjunctivitis** | **gonococcal onset 2–5 days after birth; chlamydial 5–12 days** [A1] | ⚠️ **urgent referral** [A1] |

## The split

**Conjunctivitis accounts for approximately 1% of all primary care presentations**, and **in children,
66.6% of all eye-related problems that presented to the GP are related to conjunctivitis** [S1].

| | Frequency [S1] | Discriminator |
|---|---|---|
| **Viral** | **the most common cause of infectious conjunctivitis in the adult population** | **preauricular lymphadenopathy is more prevalent** [S1]; **watery or mucoid discharge** [A1] |
| **Bacterial** | **second most common in adults, responsible for the majority of paediatric cases** | ⚠️ **the triad: bilateral mattering of the eyelids, lack of itching, no previous history of conjunctivitis** [S1] |
| **Allergic** | ⚠️ **the most common non-infective cause, affecting between 15% and 40% of the population** | ⚠️ **itching is the most pathognomonic symptom because it is rare in other ocular conditions** [S1] |
| **Chlamydial** | — | **prolonged episode, typically unilateral, watery serous discharge, STI exposure** [S1] |
| **Gonococcal** | — | ⚠️ **acute hyperpurulent onset with copious purulent discharge** [S1] |

> ⚠️ **The rate of clinical accuracy in diagnosing viral conjunctivitis is less than 50%. Viral
> conjunctivitis is often misdiagnosed as bacterial conjunctivitis** [S1].

## Examination

**Use personal protective equipment** — **hand hygiene, gloves and a cotton bud to examine and touch the
eyelids**, and **thoroughly clean all equipment following examination** [S1].

**A systematic approach** [S1]:

1. **Measure visual acuity in both eyes.**
2. **Review the lids, lid margin and conjunctiva** — **lid oedema, chemosis and subconjunctival
   haemorrhage might be present.**
3. **Evert the upper and lower eyelids** (best after topical anaesthetic) and **check for a foreign body.**
4. ⚠️ **Look carefully for pseudomembranes** — **yellow–white fibrin-rich 'sheets' that layer over the
   conjunctiva**, **difficult to identify**, and **should be removed to prevent symblepharon formation.**
5. **Examine for preauricular lymphadenopathy** — **more prevalent in viral conjunctivitis.**
6. **Check the eyelid margin** — **blepharitis can frequently be a contributing factor**; look for
   **erythema and oedema of the lid margin, oily scale or greasy crusting of the lashes**.
7. ⚠️ **Instil fluorescein and examine the cornea.** ⚠️ **Exclude a dendrite — this suggests HSV
   keratitis.** **Microbial keratitis and marginal keratitis also take up fluorescein and require
   ophthalmic review.**

## ⚠️ Where the two sources disagree

Three points. **Both are Australian, both are current, and they do not say the same thing.** ⚠️ **Each
statement is attributed. Neither is presented as the answer.**

### 1 · The chloramphenicol regimen

| Source | Regimen for bacterial conjunctivitis |
|---|---|
| **S1 (AJGP 2024)** | **a broad-spectrum antibiotic (such as chloramphenicol) four times daily for five to seven days** |
| ⚠️ **A1 (AMH 2026)** | ⚠️ **1 eye drop every 2 hours while awake for 1–2 days then, if there is improvement, 1 drop 4 times daily for up to 5 days** |

⚠️ **AMH front-loads; the review does not.** ⚠️ **This is a dose disagreement between two current Australian
sources and it is not resolved here.** A1's figure sits in the **attestation queue** and is **not machine
re-checkable**; S1's **is** quoted verbatim and re-checkable.

### 2 · Whether to swab

| Source | Says |
|---|---|
| ⚠️ **A1** | ⚠️ **swabs for culture are not usually required for initial diagnosis** of bacterial conjunctivitis |
| ⚠️ **S1** | **if there is copious discharge, a bacterial swab should be sent for microscopy and culture**, and **if in doubt, take a swab and send for viral PCR to ascertain the cause** |

⚠️ **S1's position follows from its own finding that clinical accuracy for viral conjunctivitis is under
50%.** A1's follows from bacterial conjunctivitis being self-limiting. **Both are internally coherent and
they point opposite ways.**

### 3 · Route of antiviral for HSV keratitis

| Source | Says |
|---|---|
| **S1** | ⚠️ **oral antiviral treatment for 10–14 days** — **valaciclovir, famciclovir or aciclovir** |
| **A1** | ⚠️ **treat with topical aciclovir** (for **herpes simplex epithelial keratitis**) |

⚠️ **Different routes for what may be different entities.** A1 is specific to **epithelial** keratitis — the
dendrite. ⚠️ **This guideline does not assert they are the same condition, and does not pick a route.** What
both agree on: ⚠️ **specialist involvement, and A1's absolute — never start corticosteroids yourself.**

## Viral conjunctivitis

**Often caused by adenovirus (65–90% of cases)** [S1]; **mainly caused by adenoviruses** [A1]. **Highly
transmissible, commonly through contaminated swimming pools and contact with contaminated objects.
Transmission occurs in 10–50% of cases** [S1]. **Incubation and communicability ongoing for five to 14
days** [S1].

**Presentation:** **red eye, tearing, burning and sticky discharge**, **most notable on waking**; **might
transmit from one eye to the other two to three days after the first eye is involved**; **recent contact
with someone with red eyes (incubation three to five days prior) or a recent URTI** [S1].

**Treatment is symptomatic.** ⚠️ **Topical ocular antivirals or antibacterials are not indicated; ocular
lubricants and/or cool compresses may help to relieve symptoms** [A1]. **Regular cool compresses to the
eyes, as well as lubricant drops as required** [S1].

**Remove discharge with a clean cotton ball or tissue soaked with sterile water or sodium chloride 0.9%
solution** [A1].

**If there is notable pseudomembrane formation, refer to an ophthalmic specialist for removal**, and ⚠️ **if
pseudomembranes are present, regular topical steroids will need to be used — in conjunction with either an
ophthalmologist or optometrist** [S1].

### What to tell the patient

| | |
|---|---|
| **Course** | ⚠️ **self-limiting, can worsen in the first few days, usually resolves in two to three weeks** [S1] |
| ⚠️ **Contagious period** | ⚠️ **10–14 days after symptoms appear and while the eyes are symptomatic** [S1]; **highly contagious until discharge resolves** [A1] |
| **Precautions** | **regular hand washing, no sharing of linen and towels** [S1]; **avoid touching eyes, wash hands frequently, do not share towels or bedding** [A1] |
| ⚠️ **Work** | ⚠️ **a medical certificate is often required for up to 14 days** [S1] |
| **Contact lenses** | ⚠️ **discard the previous lenses; resume with new lenses once asymptomatic for one week** [S1]; **discontinue use until symptoms have completely resolved** [A1] |
| **When to come back** | ⚠️ **if symptoms worsen or do not resolve within two to three weeks** [S1]; **if symptoms are severe or do not improve within 7–10 days** [A1] |

## Bacterial conjunctivitis

**Most commonly staphylococcal species, followed by S. pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae** [S1][A1].
**More common in children** [S1][A1]. **Typically lasts seven to 10 days** [S1].

**Two varieties: hyperacute (<24 hours) onset with rapid progression; and acute or subacute onset,
moderate to severe** [S1]. **Sticky eyelids might be present in approximately 90% of affected
individuals** [S1].

### ⚠️ How much do the drops do?

> ⚠️ **There are no significant differences among the various broad-spectrum antibiotic eye drops in
> achieving a clinical cure. Antibiotics will decrease the period of infectivity, although there is limited
> benefit** [S1].

> ⚠️ **Topical ocular antibacterials may hasten recovery, prevent complications and limit the spread of
> infection to other people but their benefits are modest. They are indicated in young children or if
> symptoms are severe** [A1].

> ⚠️ **AMH records that a randomised double-blind study in children in primary care with mild acute
> infective conjunctivitis found no significant difference in clinical cure between topical chloramphenicol
> and placebo** [A1].

⚠️ **Read those three together.** **Children are the group in whom bacterial conjunctivitis is most
common** [S1], **the group A1 names as an indication for treatment** — and **the group in which the trial
A1 cites found no difference against placebo.**

### Delayed prescribing

> **As bacterial conjunctivitis is frequently self-limiting (resolving within 7 days without treatment), a
> delayed approach — where antibacterials are only started if symptoms do not improve within 2–3 days — may
> be considered in uncomplicated cases** [A1].

⚠️ **S1 does not mention delayed prescribing.** It is A1's option alone.

### Drug choice

**Chloramphenicol is preferred as it has a broad spectrum of action, has good ocular penetration, is well
tolerated and inexpensive** [A1] — ⚠️ **although it is not active against Pseudomonas** [A1].

> ⚠️ **Pseudomonas is the organism behind contact-lens-associated keratitis, and chloramphenicol does not
> cover it.** That is the mechanical reason both sources send **every contact lens wearer** for specialist
> review rather than treating them in general practice.

**Framycetin may be used if chloramphenicol is unsuitable but is associated with contact allergic
reactions. Other antibacterials (eg tobramycin) should be reserved for use by ophthalmologists and for
serious infections not responding to other treatment** [A1].

**Chloramphenicol is available without prescription for bacterial conjunctivitis**, and ⚠️ **the patient
should consult a doctor or optometrist if symptoms do not improve within 48 hours** [A1].

⚠️ **On the old fear: large population-based studies have found no association between use of
chloramphenicol eye drops and aplastic anaemia** [A1].

**Practical:** **consider whether the patient has had issues with previous eye drops including
preservatives, because these can cause ongoing ocular hyperaemia and irritation. Antibiotic ointments are
frequently preservative free** — but ⚠️ **ointments are quite viscous and can cause blurred vision
following application, which might limit the patient's use of therapy** [S1].

**Follow-up:** **most cases do not require follow-up. If symptoms last beyond one week, consider other
contributing factors and referral. If the conjunctivitis is not resolving, investigate chlamydia** [S1];
**seek advice if there is no improvement after 2 days of treatment with antibacterials** [A1].

## Chlamydial and gonococcal conjunctivitis

| | Chlamydial | Gonococcal |
|---|---|---|
| **Picture** | **prolonged, typically unilateral, watery serous discharge, STI exposure** [S1] | ⚠️ **acute hyperpurulent, copious purulent/mucopurulent discharge** [S1] |
| **Test** | **chlamydia PCR** [S1] | ⚠️ **urgent Gram stain** [S1] |
| **Treat** | ⚠️ **systemic antibiotics — azithromycin or doxycycline as per local guidelines. Topical antibiotics are NOT beneficial** [S1]. **Treat with azithromycin; in adults, doxycycline is an alternative** [A1] | ⚠️ **1 g ceftriaxone IV/IM immediately + 1 g oral azithromycin** [S1]; **a single dose of ceftriaxone or cefotaxime, with azithromycin also in adults** [A1] |
| **Neonates** | ⚠️ **onset 5–12 days after birth, urgent referral** [A1] | ⚠️ **onset 2–5 days after birth, urgent referral** [A1] |
| **Disposition** | **investigate for other STIs** [S1] | ⚠️ **same-day ophthalmology — an ocular emergency** [S1] |

**Trachoma** is separate: ⚠️ **a chronic conjunctivitis affecting rural and remote populations of Aboriginal
and Torres Strait Islander people; azithromycin is used for prevention and treatment** [A1]. ⚠️ **The
strains of *C. trachomatis* that cause inclusion conjunctivitis differ from those that cause
trachoma** [A1]. **No trachoma dose is held here — see *Unresolved*.**

## Allergic conjunctivitis

**Itchy eyes, with redness, swelling and tearing; may occur in association with allergic rhinitis. Two main
types, seasonal (hay fever) and perennial** [A1]. **A response to a transient (eg pollen) or persistent (eg
house dust mite) allergen** [S1].

**Investigations: none needed** [S1]. **Identify and avoid triggers — pollens, animal dander, house dust
mites** [A1].

**General measures:** **cold compress to eyelids as required, irrigation with sodium chloride 0.9% solution
(eg twice daily), ocular lubricants** [A1].

| Class | Position |
|---|---|
| **Topical antihistamines** | **preferred to oral antihistamines, particularly for isolated ocular symptoms, as they have a more rapid onset — however they can sting on instillation** [A1]. ⚠️ **Might take several weeks before a full effect is seen** [S1] |
| **Antihistamines with mast cell stabilising properties** | **azelastine, ketotifen and olopatadine may also prevent symptoms** [A1] |
| **Mast cell stabilisers** | ⚠️ **not useful for acute symptoms — can take up to 2 weeks to reach full effect.** Useful **started 2–4 weeks before hay fever season**, or with a topical antihistamine for initial control [A1] |
| **Oral antihistamines** | **might help alleviate symptoms** [S1] |
| **Ketorolac** | **occasionally used for seasonal allergic conjunctivitis when response to other treatments is inadequate**; ⚠️ **can cause ocular irritation** [A1] |
| ⚠️ **Corticosteroids** | ⚠️ **only under specialist supervision for severe symptoms unresponsive to other treatments; serious adverse effects requiring monitoring, eg raised intraocular pressure** [A1]. Refer **if unable to control symptoms with both topical and oral antihistamines** [S1] |
| ⚠️ **Vasoconstrictors** | ⚠️ **not recommended — benefit is doubtful and rebound hyperaemia can lead to overuse** [A1] |

⚠️ **Treatment for allergic rhinitis, eg intranasal corticosteroids, may also relieve the symptoms of
allergic conjunctivitis** [A1] — see the **allergic rhinitis** guideline.

## Blepharitis, blepharoconjunctivitis, stye and chalazion

**Blepharitis is one of the most commonly encountered ocular disorders, affecting up to 40% of the
population** [S1]. ⚠️ **Cure is usually not possible; treatment aims to control symptoms and prevent
exacerbations** [A1].

**Anterior:** **seborrhoeic — greasy scales around the eyelashes**; **staphylococcal — redness and swelling
of the lid margin, hard scales and eyelash loss or misdirection** [A1].
**Posterior:** **meibomian gland dysfunction — blocked glands and/or frothy discharge along the eyelid
margin** [A1].

**Symptoms: erythema, burning, irritation, tearing, eyelid crusting and sticking, typically worse in the
morning** [S1].

### Eyelid hygiene — the treatment

> **Recommended for all patients with blepharitis. A common regimen is twice daily while disease is active,
> then daily to prevent exacerbations** [A1]:
> - **apply a warm compress to closed eyes for 2–5 minutes and gently massage eyelids**
> - **clean the eyelids using a damp cotton pad dipped in diluted baby shampoo (eg 1 part shampoo to 10
>   parts water); proprietary eyelid cleansers are also available**

⚠️ **Because blepharitis is a chronic disease, eyelid hygiene must be performed even after an acute
exacerbation has resolved** [S1].

**Drugs, second:** ⚠️ **evidence for efficacy of antibacterials for blepharitis is limited** [A1]. If
hygiene fails: **chloramphenicol eye ointment for anterior blepharitis**; **an oral antibacterial (eg a
tetracycline) for posterior blepharitis, especially if associated with rosacea** [A1] — see the **rosacea**
guideline.

**Treat associated conditions — seborrhoeic dermatitis, rosacea or dry eyes** [A1].

**Blepharoconjunctivitis:** **inflammation of both the eyelid and conjunctiva**, **worse in the morning,
eyelids shut upon waking**; **the mainstay of management is eyelid hygiene** [S1].

| | Stye [A1] | Chalazion [A1] |
|---|---|---|
| **What** | **acute staphylococcal abscess of a sebaceous gland associated with an eyelash follicle** | **chronic granuloma of a meibomian gland** |
| **Feels** | **pain, tenderness and focal swelling** | **minimally tender focal lid mass** |
| **Do** | **hasten 'pointing' with hot water compresses 3 or 4 times daily; pulling out the associated eyelash may aid recovery** | **ocular lubricant and warm or cold compresses; most resolve over several weeks** |
| **Antibacterials** | ⚠️ **not usually required** | ⚠️ **usually not indicated** |
| **If it fails** | — | **incision and drainage may be required** |

⚠️ **Styes are often secondary to blepharitis** [A1] — treat the lid disease or it recurs.

## Infectious keratitis

⚠️ **Inflammation of the cornea caused by bacteria, viruses, fungi or protozoa. Risk factors include contact
lens wear, corneal trauma or abrasion, and immunosuppression. Refer promptly to an ophthalmologist as it is
sight-threatening** [A1].

**Bacterial keratitis:** **pain, redness, blurred vision, photophobia and tearing**; **staphylococci,
streptococci and Gram-negative bacilli such as Pseudomonas**; **frequent topical ocular antibacterials —
monotherapy with ciprofloxacin or ofloxacin, or combined therapy such as high-strength cefazolin with
gentamicin** [A1]. **Diagnosis is based on a fluorescein assessment showing a corneal ulcer, with
surrounding infiltrate and potentially cells within the anterior chamber** [S1].

**Herpes simplex epithelial keratitis:** **branching ulceration of the cornea**, **usually one eye,
bilateral more common in immunosuppression**, **foreign body sensation often mild due to associated loss of
corneal sensation, photophobia and tearing** [A1]. **On fluorescein, a classical dendrite — but it can also
present as superficial punctate keratopathy, mild conjunctival injection, ciliary flush or corneal stromal
opacification** [S1].

> ⚠️ **Never use corticosteroids for herpes simplex epithelial keratitis (proven or suspected)** [A1].
> **Refer all new cases to an ophthalmologist within 24 hours, and refer recurrent cases that fail to
> respond to treatment within 3 days or that have corneal opacity** [A1].

⚠️ **The two sources give different antiviral routes — see *Where the two sources disagree*.**

## Herpes zoster ophthalmicus

> ⚠️ **The ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve is involved in about 10–20% of herpes zoster cases
> and, without antivirals, half of these will develop eye complications — conjunctivitis, keratitis,
> uveitis or raised intraocular pressure** [A1].

**Seek specialist advice** [A1]. ⚠️ **Start oral aciclovir, famciclovir or valaciclovir as soon as possible
— within 72 hours of rash onset — to help resolve rash and to decrease pain, corneal damage and the risk of
anterior uveitis; treat for at least 7 days.** ⚠️ **Use IV aciclovir if sight is threatened** [A1].

**Regular use of ocular lubricants is recommended; systemic analgesics and cool compresses may be used for
symptom relief; uveitis and keratitis may be controlled with topical corticosteroids** [A1].

⚠️ **No oral antiviral dose is given by either source for this indication — see *Unresolved*.**
