# Delirium

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-21 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults, hospital care and transition to primary care.

> **Australian primary source.** Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care (ACSQHC).
> ***Delirium Clinical Care Standard*** [S1]. **Eight quality statements.**

**This guideline closes a cross-reference in the dementia guideline of this compendium**, which names
delirium twice as the condition to exclude — in the comprehensive assessment, and as a reason to consider
EEG or CSF examination.

## ⚠️ A whole quality statement devoted to not using a drug

> **Quality statement 7 — Avoiding use of antipsychotic medicines.**
> **Antipsychotic medicines are not recommended to treat delirium. Behavioural and psychological symptoms in a
> patient with delirium are managed using non-drug strategies** [S1].

In thirty-nine guidelines this is the only time a national standard has devoted **an entire numbered
statement** to a medicine you should not give. Not a caution inside a treatment statement — a statement whose
subject is the avoidance.

> **Evidence does not support the routine use of antipsychotics for treating delirium** [S1].

**The narrow exception**, and its conditions [S1]:

> **Short-term antipsychotic use may be considered in limited circumstances — for instance, when non-drug
> strategies are unsuccessful and there is an imminent risk of the patient harming themselves or others.**

- **Assess the potential harms and benefits and, whenever possible, discuss the use of the medicine with the
  patient and family and obtain informed consent.**
- **Use the lowest appropriate dose for the shortest possible duration**, per *Therapeutic Guidelines:
  Psychotropic*. > **A single dose is usually enough.**
- **When an antipsychotic has been used in an emergency, discuss the use with the patient and their family or
  carer afterwards, so they understand why it was used.**
- **If prescribed for more than a single dose, document the plan for the duration of therapy and the criteria
  for cessation — that is, the change in behaviour to be achieved.**
- > **Advise the patient and their family or carer that longer-term use of antipsychotics has a greater risk of
  > harm than of benefit, except in limited circumstances.**
- **Arrange psychiatry or geriatric review for a patient with delirium who has other indications for
  antipsychotic use, or who has an existing prescription for antipsychotics.**

### The other two things to avoid

> **Avoid benzodiazepines when managing delirium, as complications are common and long-acting benzodiazepines
> increase delirium** [S1].

> **Avoid using physical or mechanical restraints, as they can increase agitation, prolong delirium and
> increase the risk of injury** [S1].

**Over-sedation can have serious consequences, such as dehydration, falls, respiratory depression, pneumonia
and death** [S1]. **People with Parkinson's disease or with Lewy body dementia are at an increased risk of
severe adverse reactions from antipsychotics** [S1] — the same warning the **dementia** guideline gives, from a
different document.

## ⚠️ The risk threshold is 20 years earlier for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people

**Key risk factors** *(Quality statement 1)* [S1]:

- > **age ≥ 65 years (≥ 45 years for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people)**
- **known cognitive impairment or diagnosed dementia**
- **a previous diagnosis of delirium**
- **a severe medical illness** — a clinical condition that is deteriorating or is at risk of deterioration
- **current hip fracture**

A **twenty-year difference** in the age at which screening begins, written into the risk factor list itself.
It is the starkest equity adjustment in any guideline in this compendium, and it appears as a parenthesis
rather than a footnote.

**Conduct a delirium risk assessment in the pre-admission clinic or within 24 hours of presentation to
hospital** [S1]. **Patients with any of these risk factors should receive a validated screening test for
cognitive impairment**, which is **recognised as a significant risk factor for developing delirium** [S1].

> **Conducting cognitive screening on presentation to hospital helps identify patients who should be assessed
> for delirium and enables monitoring for delirium onset during a hospital stay by providing a baseline
> measure. This also applies to patients with known cognitive impairment** [S1].

**Even a patient with known dementia is screened — to get a current score.** Without a baseline, an acute
change cannot be detected.

**Use a validated tool that is culturally appropriate** [S1].

### Consent to the risk

> **Before any planned admission, the risk of delirium is assessed and discussed with the patient, to enable
> an informed decision about the benefits and risks** *(Quality statement 1)* [S1].

Delirium is treated as a **risk of admission to be consented to**, like any procedural risk.

## Prevention

**Quality statement 2** [S1]: a patient at risk **is offered a set of interventions to prevent delirium and is
regularly monitored for changes in behaviour, cognition and physical condition**, with interventions
determined **before a planned admission or on admission**, in discussion with the patient and their family or
carer.

- **Develop a delirium prevention plan in partnership with the patient and family or carer**, as part of a
  comprehensive care plan [S1].
- **Offer multicomponent interventions**, considering clinical risk factors and the setting [S1].
- **Encourage family or carers to be involved — for example, to orient and reassure the patient** — and **ask
  them to alert the healthcare team to any changes in the patient's mental or physical condition** [S1].
- > **Educate patients and family or carers about delirium before it occurs, to reduce distress if it does
  > occur** [S1].
- **Monitor patients regularly, at least daily, for changes in cognition and behaviour, and for clinical
  deterioration** [S1].
- **Risk of delirium is increased post-operatively** [S1].
- **Conduct medication reconciliation before patients are transferred between locations or phases of care** —
  between wards, or to another facility [S1].

**The prevention interventions also apply to patients who already have delirium** [S1].

## ⚠️ The quiet patient is the sicker one

> **Identifying hypoactive, hyperactive or mixed cases of delirium is necessary to implement appropriate
> treatment strategies. Hypoactive delirium is more common in older people, but is often missed and has a
> worse prognosis than other subtypes of delirium, including worse long-term cognition when delirium has a
> longer duration** [S1].

> **Delirium is less likely to be recognised in patients with frailty or dementia** [S1].

The delirium that gets noticed is the agitated one. The one that carries the worse prognosis is the one that
looks like a settled patient — and it is least likely to be recognised in exactly the population most at risk.

## Assessment and diagnosis

**Quality statement 4** [S1]: assess **using a validated tool, by a clinician trained to assess delirium**, in:

- **patients with cognitive impairment on presentation to hospital**
- **patients who have a sudden decline in cognitive function or change in behaviour during their hospital
  admission**

**Seek information about the patient's usual mental status** from the patient, family or carer, GP or other
primary care provider [S1]. **Ask about behavioural changes** [S1]: **confusion or worsened concentration;
agitation or restlessness; sleepiness, including altered levels of consciousness; whether the patient has been
less communicative or less responsive than usual; whether the patient has had difficulty cooperating with
reasonable requests or has had other alterations in mood.**

> **Family members or carers are often the best source of information about acute changes in a patient's
> mental status or behaviour** [S1].

> **As delirium symptoms can vary throughout the day, more than one assessment may be required to diagnose
> delirium** [S1].

**The diagnosis is determined and documented by a clinician working within their scope of practice** [S1].
**Document the diagnosis to aid in transfers of care, including in handover notes, referral and discharge
letters** [S1] — and the reason is given:

> **A history of delirium increases the risk of recurrence, and documenting an episode of delirium allows for
> preventive measures and monitoring for new delirium in subsequent healthcare encounters** [S1].

**Discuss the diagnosis with the patient and their family or carer** [S1]. **Current international clinical
guidelines include validated delirium diagnostic tools, some of which require training to use
effectively** [S1].

## Treating the cause

**Quality statement 5** [S1]: a set of interventions to treat the causes, **based on a comprehensive assessment
that includes relevant multidisciplinary consultation**. **Seek a patient summary from the patient's general
practitioner to help inform the investigation.**

A comprehensive assessment involves [S1]:

- **a medical and social history, paying close attention to the patient's medication history, including
  adverse reactions, their pain management needs, and their hydration and nutritional status**
- **a physical examination**
- **investigations, informed by the medical history and physical examination**
- **consultation with other clinicians with relevant expertise — such as a geriatrician, psychiatrist,
  dietitian — whenever possible.** **In rural and remote health services this could be facilitated by using
  telehealth when information technology systems allow.**

> **Prevention is the most effective strategy, but outcomes for patients with delirium can also be improved by
> early intervention** [S1].

## Preventing complications of care

**Quality statement 6** [S1]: care to prevent **functional decline, dehydration, malnutrition, falls and
pressure injuries**, based on risk — **assessed, monitored and documented**.

- **Falls prevention examples**: **reorientation, appropriate lighting, and ensuring that patients are using
  their eyeglasses or hearing aids** [S1].
- **To lessen the risk of functional decline, encourage mobility and self-care with assistance as
  necessary** [S1].
- **Assist patients as required throughout the day to maintain optimal nutrition and hydration.** **Ensure
  dentures are fitted correctly, particularly at mealtimes.** **For patients at risk of malnutrition, arrange
  for a dietitian to assess and manage them** [S1].

Glasses, hearing aids and properly fitted dentures appear as **clinical interventions**, not amenities.

## Information, support and de-escalation

**Quality statement 3** [S1]: the patient and their family or carer **are encouraged to be active participants
in care**, are **provided with information about delirium and its prevention in a way that they can
understand**, and **when delirium occurs, receive support to cope with the experience and its effects**.

- **Recognise when to engage an interpreting service.** > **Family or friends may not be appropriate
  interpreters because of health privacy issues** [S1].
- **If a patient develops delirium, proactively assess their distress** and inform them and their family or
  carer about the plans to treat and reduce the severity of symptoms and any distress [S1].
- **Support patients to make their own decisions and to choose a support person to be involved in
  decisions** [S1].
- > **Ask patients specifically about fears or concerns, and about hallucinations. Reassure patients and help
  > them feel safe** [S1].

### When a patient is severely disturbed

**First, look for a cause** [S1]: **conduct a comprehensive assessment including a medication review; identify
medicines known to contribute to delirium and adjust if appropriate, such as medicines with anticholinergic or
sedative properties**; and **ensure that any medical causes for distress and agitation — such as pain,
constipation, urinary retention and hypoxia — are treated.**

**Then reassure and de-escalate** — involving family or carers if possible, or **one-on-one nursing** [S1].
**Obtain information about the patient, their needs and preferences, and ways to reduce distress; if the
patient cannot provide it themselves, engage family or carers and use a structured tool such as the TOP 5
model** [S1]. **Ensure the environment is safe and noise is minimised, and that the patient is observed
without staff invading their personal space** [S1].

**Verbal and non-verbal techniques** named in the standard [S1]:

- **being respectful**
- **introducing yourself and using their title rather than their given name if the patient does not know you**
- **talking slowly and calmly**
- **not disagreeing with the patient**
- **asking questions and listening to the answers**
- **expressing empathy and concern to show that you have understood**
- **inclining your head slightly, to show you are listening and to give you a non-threatening posture**
- **acknowledging their feelings and that the situation they find themselves in is frightening or distressing**
- **providing a distraction**

A national standard specifying **head posture** and **not using a stranger's first name** is unusual, and it
is the direct alternative to the drug that statement 7 rules out.

## Transition from hospital

**Quality statement 8** [S1]: before a patient with **persistent or resolved** delirium leaves hospital, an
**individualised comprehensive care plan is developed collaboratively** with the patient and their family or
carer, describing post-discharge care needs, **strategies to help reduce the risk of delirium and related
complications, a summary of changes in medicines and any other ongoing treatment**.

> **This plan is provided to the patient and their family or carer before discharge, and to their general
> practitioner and other regular clinicians within 48 hours of discharge** [S1].

- **Include a plan for review by a specialist clinic, specialist or primary healthcare provider 10 days after
  discharge** [S1].
- **List all medicines the patient needs to take, specifying the generic drug name, dose, reason for use and
  duration for each one. Explain why any medicines have been stopped or changed** [S1].
- **Describe all ongoing treatments and follow-up needed for comorbidities; arrange outpatient rehabilitation
  when required; advise of ongoing community support services with contact details** [S1].

## Where this connects in the compendium

**Dementia** — the dementia guideline names delirium as the condition to exclude; this standard notes delirium
**is less likely to be recognised in patients with dementia**. Both warn that **antipsychotics carry increased
risk of severe adverse reactions in Lewy body dementia**.

**Opioid analgesic stewardship** — over-sedation here lists **respiratory depression** among its
consequences, and both standards warn against **benzodiazepines**.

**Low back pain** avoids benzodiazepines as ineffective; **opioid stewardship** names them as a
respiratory-depression risk; **this standard** says they **increase delirium**. Three Australian documents,
three separate reasons.

**Hip fracture** appears here as a named delirium risk factor — and has its own clinical care standard, not
yet written in this set.
