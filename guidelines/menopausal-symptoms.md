### What is disputed

> **Magraith and Jang's menopause review contains unsupported claims. Menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) is
> characterised as 'highly effective' and 'the most effective treatment' for menopausal symptoms. A list of
> symptoms includes sleep disturbance, mood changes, cognitive concerns and musculoskeletal symptoms, which
> are not actually menopausal** [S2] — Mintzes.

> **the only symptoms proven to be associated with menopause are hot flushes and vaginal dryness** [S2] —
> Fugh-Berman.

**On quantification** — ⚠️ **Neither the purported benefits nor harms of MHT are quantified, except for a
doubling or tripling of venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk. However, absolute risk of VTE is described only as
remaining low. Risks of heart attack, stroke and breast cancer, similarly, remain unquantified** [S2].

**On duration** — ⚠️ **A claim that there is no maximal duration of MHT use is similarly cavalier; based on
the UK Million Women study, 5 years use of estrogen plus progestogen leads to 6 extra breast cancers per 1000
users; 10 years use leads to 19 extra breast cancers per 1000 users** [S2].

**On cardiovascular prevention** — the review's Cochrane citation is challenged: **this review concludes there
is 'strong evidence that treatment with hormone therapy in post-menopausal women overall, for either primary
or secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease events has little if any benefit…'** [S2].

**On the guideline chain** — ⚠️ **The claim that menopause – rather than ageing per se – is associated with
increased risk of cardiovascular disease is supported not by research evidence, but by an International
Menopause Society guideline. Among the guideline's 21 authors, 14 report pharmaceutical industry
funding** [S2].

### The authors' response

**On absolute VTE risk, the number the review omitted**:

> **The baseline risk for VTE is 1 to 2 events in 1000 person-years; for women taking MHT, the risk is 2 to 3
> events in 1000 person-years, which is considered low** [S2].

**On breast cancer**: **the risk was only seen in women taking combination treatment, implicating the role of
progestogens** … ⚠️ **Until breast cancer rates have been studied for women using more modern forms of MHT, we
would argue against attributing an increased risk of breast cancer to all forms of MHT equally** [S2].

**On the Cochrane review**: **We were referring to the subgroup analysis in the Cochrane review that focused
on women starting MHT within 10 years after menopause and showed lower mortality and coronary heart disease
in this group. We have stated that MHT is not indicated for primary prevention of cardiovascular
disease** [S2].

**On route**: **Fugh-Berman states that estrogen plus progestogen combinations increased the risk of VTE, but
fails to discuss that this is associated with the route of administration of estrogen and the type of
progestogen used** [S2].

**What remains unresolved between them**: whether sleep, mood, cognitive and musculoskeletal symptoms are
menopausal; whether "no maximal duration" is defensible; and whether the cardiovascular association is
menopause or ageing. **A prescriber should know the dispute exists.**

## Definitions and epidemiology

**The average age of menopause in Australian women is 51 years** [S1].

| Term | Definition [S1] |
|---|---|
| **menopause** | **the final menstrual period or the permanent cessation of ovarian function** |
| **early menopause** | **menopause occurring at 40 to 44 years of age** |
| **premature ovarian insufficiency** | **menopause occurring before 40 years of age** |
| **perimenopause** | **from when the menstrual cycle starts changing until 12 months after menopause** |
| **postmenopause** | **from 12 months after menopause** |

**Approximately 75% of women experience symptoms during perimenopause and after menopause, with 25%
experiencing moderate to severe symptoms affecting their quality of life** [S1].

**Common symptoms** [S1] — ⚠️ *the scope of this list is what [S2] disputes*: **menstrual cycle changes**;
**vasomotor symptoms (hot flushes, night sweats)**; **mood changes**; **cognitive concerns ('brain fog')**;
**sleep disturbance**; **musculoskeletal symptoms**; **low libido**; **formication (sensation of insects
crawling under the skin)**; **genitourinary symptoms (vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, urinary urgency, urinary
frequency, recurrent urinary tract infections)**.

**Scope note from the source**: **In this article, the term 'women' only refers to cisgender women. Trans- and
gender-diverse people may also experience menopausal symptoms and may benefit from appropriately tailored
health services** [S1].

## Premature ovarian insufficiency

> **MHT is recommended for women with premature ovarian insufficiency (menopause occurring before 40 years of
> age) regardless of symptoms, unless contraindicated** [S1] — **until the usual age of menopause, to reduce
> the risks of osteoporosis and cardiovascular disease** [S1].

This is the one MHT indication in the review that does not depend on symptoms.

## MHT regimens

- **estrogen-only for women who have had a total hysterectomy**; ⚠️ **unopposed estrogen is associated with endometrial hyperplasia and potential malignancy**
- **cyclic combined MHT—continuous estrogen with a progestogen given cyclically (e.g. for 12 to 14 continuous days of a calendar month)**
- **continuous combined MHT—continuous estrogen and progestogen**
- **tibolone for women more than one year after menopause, particularly those with low libido** [S1]

**Perimenopausal vs postmenopausal**: **for perimenopausal women, cyclic MHT is used** … **for postmenopausal
women, continuous combined MHT is used**. ⚠️ **If continuous combined MHT is used by perimenopausal women,
irregular bleeding frequently occurs; cyclic MHT is recommended to minimise irregular bleeding. Once a woman
has been using cyclic MHT for 12 months, a trial of continuous combined MHT can take place** [S1].

**Estrogen**: **estradiol is preferred because it is structurally similar to 17-beta-estradiol, the commonest
naturally occurring estrogen in premenopausal women** [S1]. **Systemic estrogen should be started at a
low-to-medium dose and adjusted according to symptoms** [S1].

**Topical vaginal estrogen is only appropriate for managing genitourinary symptoms. Low-dose vaginal estrogen
(estradiol or estriol) is available as vaginal tablets, pessaries or creams and does not require the addition
of a progestogen** [S1].

**Progestogens**: **several progestogens are available** — **micronised progesterone, dydrogesterone,
drospirenone, norethisterone, medroxyprogesterone acetate**. ⚠️ **No randomised controlled trial data are
available to guide choice.** **Micronised progesterone and dydrogesterone … may have a lower breast cancer
risk than older synthetic progestogens and, for most women, are considered first line** [S1].

**The 52 mg levonorgestrel-releasing IUD, can be used for up to 5 years in combination with estrogen. It has
the added benefit of providing contraception and managing heavy menstrual bleeding** [S1].

**Women younger than 50 years of age, without contraindications, can use combined hormonal contraception such
as a combined oral contraceptive pill for MHT** [S1].

**Tibolone**: **the usual dose is 2.5 mg orally, daily**; **low-dose tibolone (e.g. 1.25 mg orally, daily) may
reduce both vertebral and nonvertebral fractures**. ⚠️ **Women with a history of breast cancer should not be
prescribed tibolone. Tibolone is associated with increased risk of stroke in women older than 60 years of
age** [S1].

## Monitoring

**Women starting on MHT must be warned about adverse effects, including nausea and breast tenderness.** **Women
should be reviewed after 6 to 12 weeks of starting MHT** … **Annual reassessment is recommended** [S1].

⚠️ **Bleeding and spotting often settles over months, but if it persists beyond 6 months, or becomes heavy or
prolonged, it should be investigated** [S1].

## ⚠️ Contraindications and cautions

**Contraindications to MHT** [S1]: **hormone-dependent cancers including breast and endometrial** ·
**undiagnosed vaginal bleeding** · **acute cardiovascular event** · **acute venous thromboembolism** ·
**porphyria cutanea tarda** · **severe liver disease**.

**Conditions where caution is recommended** [S1]: **past myocardial infarction, transient ischaemic attack or
stroke** · **high risk of venous thromboembolism** · **active liver disease** · **migraine with aura** ·
**hypertriglyceridaemia** · **hepatobiliary disease** · **high risk of breast cancer** · **age older than 65
years and no prior use of MHT**.

**Notes on the box** [S1]: **MHT is generally safe to use in patients with treated stage 1 endometrial
malignancy** · **consider transdermal estrogen for MHT if the patient is anticoagulated** · **exercise caution
with oral estrogen; transdermal estrogen is preferred** · ⚠️ **treated hypertension is not a contraindication
to MHT use**.

**Prefer transdermal** for **a history of atherosclerotic heart disease or stroke**, **a history of migraine
with aura**, **treated cardiovascular disease risk factors (e.g. hypertension, dyslipidaemia)**, **increased
risk of venous thromboembolism**, and **hepatobiliary disease** [S1].

**Why route matters**: **oral estrogen undergoes first-pass metabolism in the liver and alters the hepatic
production of coagulation factors. This is associated with a 2- to 3-fold increase in venous thromboembolism,
but the absolute risk remains low** … **Transdermal estrogen does not carry this risk** [S1].

## Nonhormonal drugs for vasomotor symptoms

⚠️ **All drugs listed in the table, except clonidine, are not registered by the Therapeutic Goods
Administration for treating vasomotor symptoms** [S1] — the entire nonhormonal option set is off-label bar one
agent that is no longer recommended.

| Drug | Dose [S1] | Adverse effects [S1] |
|---|---|---|
| **desvenlafaxine** | **25 to 150 mg orally daily** | **dizziness, nausea, sexual dysfunction** |
| **venlafaxine** | **37.5 to 150 mg orally daily** | as above |
| **citalopram** | **10 to 20 mg orally daily** | **dizziness, nausea, sexual dysfunction** |
| **escitalopram** | **5 to 20 mg orally daily** | as above |
| ⚠️ **paroxetine** | **10 to 25 mg orally daily** | **should not be co-administered with tamoxifen; co-administration can cause inhibition of cytochrome P450 2D6 and reduce the efficacy of tamoxifen** |
| ⚠️ **clonidine** | **25 to 75 micrograms orally twice daily** | **dizziness, drowsiness, constipation**; **may be used but is no longer recommended because of its adverse effects** |
| **gabapentin** | **100 to 900 mg orally daily in up to 3 divided doses** | **drowsiness, dizziness, possible withdrawal symptoms** |
| ⚠️ **oxybutynin** | **2.5 to 5 mg orally twice daily** | **dry mouth, drowsiness, blurred vision**; **may cause adverse effects, particularly cognitive decline in older people** |

**Generally, nonhormonal drugs are less effective than MHT and do not confer the bone- or
cardiovascular-protective benefits of estrogen. Most of the drugs have limited use because of their adverse
effects** [S1].

⚠️ **Unlike vasomotor symptoms, genitourinary symptoms do not improve with time** [S1].

## Not recommended

⚠️ **Complementary therapies and herbal preparations (e.g. black cohosh, phytoestrogens) have insufficient
evidence of benefit, can cause adverse effects, and are not recommended** [S1].

⚠️ **Custom-compounded, bioidentical hormone therapy is also not recommended because of limited dose
regulation and lack of safety data** [S1].

**The North American Menopause Society no longer recommends clonidine or pregabalin for treatment of
vasomotor symptoms because of their adverse effects; however, they are still used in some instances in
Australia** [S1].

## Nonpharmacological

**Lifestyle modifications such as exercise, weight loss and reducing alcohol consumption may be helpful for
some women. These measures may not reduce the severity of symptoms but may make them more manageable** [S1].
**Cognitive behavioural therapy can reduce the impact of vasomotor symptoms and alleviate sleep
disturbance** [S1]. **Nonhormonal lubricants can be helpful for vaginal dryness** [S1].

## Emerging

**Estetrol may provide some safety advantages through reduced effects on liver and breast tissue** [S1].
**A 2023 randomised placebo-controlled trial reported the neurokinin 3 receptor antagonist, fezolinetant, was
effective and well tolerated for the treatment of vasomotor symptoms** [S1].
