# Alcohol problems

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-21 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults and young people, primary care and hospital.

> **Australian primary source.** Riordan BC, Winter DT, Barrett L, et al. *New Australian guidelines for the
> treatment of alcohol problems: an overview of recommendations.* **Med J Aust** 2021; 215(7 Suppl) [S1] —
> an overview of the **Guidelines for the Treatment of Alcohol Problems**, released **April 2021**,
> **commissioned by the Commonwealth of Australia** and integrated with the updated NHMRC *Australian
> Guidelines to Reduce Health Risks from Drinking Alcohol* [S2].

**Periodically updated over the past 25 years** [S1]. Recommendations carry **Levels A–D** or **GPP** (good
practice point); the grading scheme's definitions were not in the retrieved overview and are recorded under
Unresolved.

## ⚠️ The clinician is named as a barrier

No other guideline in thirty-seven addresses the treating clinician's own conduct as an obstacle to care.
This one does, with examples.

> **People who experience alcohol-related problems often experience stigma, as these are frequently viewed as
> personal moral failings that are undeserving of sympathy** [S1].

> **Stigma is, unfortunately, common across health care settings for people experiencing problems with alcohol
> and it is a significant barrier to accessing health and other services. Research has found examples of
> clinicians denying appropriate care to patients experiencing problems (eg, regarding them as less deserving
> of liver transplants). Patients have reported being offered advice based on clinicians' own opinions rather
> than evidence, and being talked down to, scolded and blamed for the problems they are experiencing with
> alcohol; such experiences deter future and further help-seeking** [S1].

**Three steps individual clinicians can take** [S1]:

- **use person-centred practice that treats patients with respect and compassion and includes the patient in
  decision making about their treatment**
- **use language consistent with guides produced by peer support organisations**, to prevent prejudicial or
  stigmatising language
- **take part in anti-stigma training, especially training that is peer-led or has had substantial peer input
  into its development**

The source also notes the limit of individual effort: **stigma should be addressed at the structural and
organisational level, as individual clinicians can struggle to make behavioural changes where this is not
supported by the broader workplace** [S1].

## ⚠️ No beneficial health effects, no risk-free threshold

> **Patients should be advised that alcohol use has no beneficial health effects. There is no clear risk-free
> threshold for alcohol intake. The safe dose for alcohol intake is dependent on many factors such as
> underlying liver disease, comorbidities, age and sex** *(Level A)* [S1].

The overview states its own editorial position: it **will not recommend consuming alcohol for health
benefits**, because the latest research indicates **there are no net health benefits from alcohol use**, there
is a clear link between alcohol use and harm, and **there is a risk that a non-drinker will develop an alcohol
use disorder if drinking is recommended** [S1].

### NHMRC drinking guidelines [S2, reproduced in S1]

| Guideline | Content |
|---|---|
| **Adults** | **Healthy men and women should drink no more than 10 standard drinks a week and no more than 4 standard drinks on any one day.** **"The less you drink, the lower your risk of harm from alcohol."** |
| **Children and people under 18** | **Should not drink alcohol** |
| **Pregnancy and breastfeeding** | **Women who are pregnant or planning a pregnancy should not drink alcohol. For women who are breastfeeding, not drinking alcohol is safest for their baby** |

## The burden

- **5552 deaths attributed to alcohol in 2017** [S1].
- **Alcohol consumption is linked to over 200 diseases**, including alcohol-related injuries, cancers,
  cardiovascular diseases and liver disease — **4.6% of Australia's overall burden of disease** [S1].
- **About 150 000 alcohol-related hospitalisations in 2012–13.** **Despite declines in consumption,
  alcohol-attributable emergency department presentations, hospitalisations and ambulance attendances have
  remained stable or increased** [S1].
- **Conservative estimates in 2010 suggest a $14.4 billion cost to the economy, more than double Australia's
  alcohol-related tax revenue** [S1].
- **In 2017, per capita consumption was at its lowest in 50 years, at 9.5 L of pure alcohol annually, yet
  alcohol is the sixth highest risk factor contributing to the burden of disease in Australia** [S1].

Consumption is falling; harm is not. That disconnect is stated plainly and is the reason treatment, not only
prevention, carries this guideline.

## Screening and assessment

- **Screening for unhealthy alcohol use and appropriate interventions should be implemented in general
  practice *(Level A)*, hospitals *(Level B)*, emergency departments and community health and welfare settings
  *(Level C)*** [S1].
- > **The Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) is the most effective screening tool and is
  > recommended for use in primary care and hospital settings. For screening in the general community, the
  > AUDIT-C is a suitable alternative** *(Level A)* [S1].
- **Quantity–frequency measures can detect consumption that exceeds levels in the current Australian
  guidelines** *(Level B)* [S1].
- **Indirect biological markers should be used as an adjunct to screening** *(Level A)*; **direct measures of
  alcohol in breath and/or blood can be useful markers of recent use** *(Level B)* [S1].

**Assessment** should include **evaluation of alcohol use and its effects, physical examination, clinical
investigations and collateral history taking** *(Level C)*; and **assessment for alcohol-related physical
problems, mental health problems and social support should be undertaken routinely** *(GPP)* [S1].

**Where there are concerns regarding the safety of the patient or others, specialist consultation is
recommended** *(Level C)* [S1]. **Assessment should lead to a clear, mutually acceptable treatment plan which
specifies interventions to meet the patient's needs** *(Level D)* [S1].

> **Sustained abstinence is the optimal outcome for most patients with alcohol dependence** *(Level C)* [S1].

## Withdrawal

**Risk factors for withdrawal**: **regular daily consumption of more than 80 grams of alcohol per day,
previous withdrawal, and intercurrent illness including cessation of other drugs** [S1].

> **Withdrawal symptoms generally start 6–24 hours after the last drink, peak at 24–48 hours and subside within
> 2–4 days, but may last a week; cravings, mood changes and sleep disturbances may persist for several
> weeks** [S1].

**Symptoms range from trivial to severe life-threatening complications (e.g. seizures and delirium). However,
mortality is low with good clinical care** [S1].

> **Most cases of withdrawal can be managed in an ambulatory setting with appropriate support** *(Level B)*.
> **Tapering diazepam regimens** *(Level A)* **with daily staged supply from a pharmacy or clinic are
> recommended** *(GPP)* [S1].

**Benzodiazepines are the preferred medication, supported by Level A evidence** [S1]. And the supply controls
are as specific as the drug:

> **Diazepam access should be managed with daily pickup from a pharmacy or clinic, or supervised by a
> responsible adult, and diazepam use should be limited to a duration of 5 days. If the patient continues to
> drink or is sedated, doses should be withheld** [S1].

**Medications for symptom management can be administered as required — e.g. paracetamol, metoclopramide,
hyoscine and loperamide** [S1].

**History before withdrawal** [S1]: motivation and a realistic plan for abstinence — **if not, other treatment
options should be considered**; alcohol and other drug use including quantity, frequency, duration and time of
last use; **previous withdrawal attempts and any complications (seizures, delirium and hallucinations)**; home
environment and social supports; medical and psychiatric conditions. **Examination**: vital signs, hydration,
mental state, evidence of intoxication, withdrawal signs (tremor, anxiety, sweating, tachycardia), and **signs
of liver disease and other complications** [S1].

**Support during ambulatory withdrawal** should focus on **strategies for coping with withdrawal symptoms,
maintaining motivation and reassurance — daily if possible** — and **linking the patient to a 24-hour telephone
counselling or crisis service** [S1].

### ⚠️ Thiamine

> **Nutrition advice should include drinking plenty of fluids (2 litres per day minimum) and avoiding heavy
> meals. Thiamine supplements (300 mg per day for 7 days) should be provided orally if the patient is well or
> by intramuscular injection if they are nutritionally depleted. For suspected Wernicke encephalopathy, higher
> thiamine doses are recommended initially (500 mg total dissolved solids by intravenous infusion) with ongoing
> treatment guided by specialist advice** [S1].

The route is decided by nutritional state, not by convenience — **oral only if the patient is well**.

## Pharmacotherapy for alcohol dependence

| Medicine | Recommendation [S1] | Level |
|---|---|---|
| **Acamprosate** | **Recommended to help maintain abstinence from alcohol** | **A** |
| **Naltrexone** | **Recommended for prevention of relapse to heavy drinking** | **A** |
| **Disulfiram** | **Only recommended in close supervision settings where patients are motivated for abstinence** | **A** |
| **Baclofen, topiramate** | **Some evidence exists, but their side effect profiles are complex and neither should be a first-line medication** — **off-label** | **B** |

**Acamprosate and naltrexone have different targets** — *maintain abstinence* versus *prevent relapse to heavy
drinking* — and the distinction is the source's own, not a paraphrase.

## Psychosocial treatment

- > **Cognitive behaviour therapy should be a first-line psychosocial intervention for alcohol dependence. Its
  > clinical benefit is enhanced when it is combined with pharmacotherapy for alcohol dependence or an
  > additional psychosocial intervention (eg, motivational interviewing)** *(Level A)* [S1].
- **Motivational interviewing is effective in the short term and in patients with less severe alcohol
  dependence** *(Level A)* [S1].
- **Brief motivational interviewing interventions are more effective than no treatment for people who consume
  alcohol at risky levels** *(Level A)*, and **are most effective in primary care settings** *(Level A)* [S1].
- **Residential rehabilitation may be of benefit to patients who have moderate-to-severe alcohol dependence and
  require a structured residential treatment setting** *(Level D)* [S1].
- > **Peer-led support programs such as Alcoholics Anonymous and SMART Recovery are effective at maintaining
  > abstinence or reductions in drinking** *(Level A)* [S1].

**Relapse prevention and aftercare** [S1]: **return to problematic drinking is common and aftercare should
focus on addressing factors that contribute to relapse** *(GPP)*. And:

> **A harm-minimisation approach should be considered for patients who are unable to reduce their drinking**
> *(GPP)* [S1].

## Comorbidity

### ⚠️ Alcohol and opioids

> **Active alcohol use disorder, including dependence, significantly increases the risk of overdose associated
> with the administration of opioid drugs. Specialist advice is recommended before treatment of people
> dependent on both alcohol and opioid drugs** *(GPP)* [S1].

This lands directly on the **opioid analgesic stewardship** guideline in this compendium, which lists
**alcohol among the CNS depressants** raising the risk of sedation and respiratory depression. Two Australian
sources, opposite starting points, same warning.

**Older patients requiring management of alcohol withdrawal should have their use of pharmaceutical medications
reviewed, given the prevalence of polypharmacy in this age group** *(GPP)* [S1].

**Smoking cessation can be undertaken in patients with alcohol dependence and/or polydrug use problems; some
evidence suggests varenicline may help support reduction of both tobacco and alcohol consumption**
*(Level C)* [S1].

### Mental health

- **More intensive interventions are needed for people with comorbid conditions, as this population tends to
  have more severe problems and carries a worse prognosis than those with single pathology** *(GPP)* [S1].
- **The Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10 or K6) is recommended for screening for comorbid mental
  disorders in people presenting for alcohol use disorders** *(Level A)* [S1].
- **People with alcohol use disorder and comorbid mental disorders should be offered treatment for both
  disorders; care should be taken to coordinate intervention** *(Level C)* [S1].

### Physical

> **In patients with alcohol use disorder, early recognition of the risk for liver cirrhosis is critical.
> Patients with cirrhosis should abstain from alcohol and should be offered referral to a hepatologist for
> liver disease management **and** to an addiction physician for management of alcohol use disorder**
> *(Level A)* [S1].

**Two referrals, not one** — the liver and the dependence are managed by different people, and the guideline
insists on both.

> **Alcohol abstinence reduces the risk of cancer and improves outcomes after a diagnosis of cancer**
> *(Level A)* [S1].

**Cardiovascular** [S1]: **have a high index of suspicion for hypertension and dysrhythmia (supraventricular
tachycardia and atrial fibrillation)**; **these disorders increase stroke risk**; **an abstinence-focused
treatment plan is recommended for patients with cardiac disease to improve clinical outcomes**.

That connects to the **atrial fibrillation** guideline here, which names **alcohol excess** among the
comorbidities whose management is an important component of AF treatment — and to **stroke and TIA**.

**Neurological** [S1]: **Wernicke encephalopathy, cerebellar dysfunction, subdural haematoma, and
alcohol-related dementia should be considered as differential diagnoses in patients with altered level of
consciousness, falls, cognitive or motor decline, memory loss and alcohol use disorder.** **Alcohol
abstinence, analgesia, a balanced diet, high dose thiamine and a multivitamin supplement containing other B
vitamins are recommended.**

## Specific populations

| Population | Recommendations [S1] |
|---|---|
| **Gender** | **Screen women and men for domestic abuse** *(Level C)*. **Consider child protection assessments for caregivers with alcohol use disorder** *(GPP)*. **Explore contraceptive options with women of reproductive age who regularly consume alcohol** *(Level B)* |
| **Pregnancy and breastfeeding** | **Advise that there is no safe level of alcohol consumption** *(Level B)*. **Pregnant women who are alcohol dependent should be admitted to hospital for treatment in an appropriate maternity unit that has an addiction specialist** *(GPP)* |
| **Young people** | **Perform a comprehensive HEEADSSS assessment** *(Level B)*. **Treatment should focus on tangible benefits of reducing drinking through psychotherapy and engagement of family and peer networks** *(Level B)* |
| **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples** | **Collaborate with Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander health workers, organisations and communities, and seek guidance on patient engagement approaches** *(GPP)*. **Use validated screening tools and consider integrated mainstream and Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander-specific approaches to care** *(Level B)* |
| **Culturally and linguistically diverse groups** | **Use an appropriate method, such as the "teach-back" technique, to assess the need for language and health literacy support** *(Level C)*. **Engage with culture-specific agencies as this can improve treatment access and success** *(Level C)* |
| **Sexually diverse and gender diverse populations** | **Be mindful that these populations experience lower levels of satisfaction, connection and treatment completion** *(Level C)*. **Seek to incorporate LGBTQ-specific treatment and agencies** *(Level C)* |
| **Older people** | **All new patients aged over 50 years should be screened for harmful alcohol use** *(Level D)*. **Consider alcohol as a possible cause for older patients presenting with unexplained physical or psychological symptoms** *(Level D)*. **Consider shorter acting benzodiazepines for withdrawal management** *(Level D)* |
| **Cognitive impairment** | **Cognitive impairment may impair engagement with treatment** *(Level A)*. **Perform cognitive screening for patients who have alcohol problems and refer them for neuropsychological assessment if significant impairment is suspected** *(Level A)* |

The cognitive-impairment pair is worth holding: it is **Level A that impairment obstructs treatment**, and
**Level A that you should screen for it** — the strongest evidence on the page attaches to a barrier, not a
therapy.

## Practical supports named

For patients with severe, ongoing problems [S1]: **empower family and close friends to reduce availability of
alcohol and encourage clinical engagement**; **consider medicolegal and ethical obligations relating to driving
assessments, child protection, welfare, guardianship and employment**; **provide thiamine supplements to
prevent further central nervous system damage**; **attend to advanced liver disease and other organ damage**;
and **avoid interactions between medications for alcohol use disorders and other medications — e.g.
paracetamol, benzodiazepines, anticoagulants and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs**.
