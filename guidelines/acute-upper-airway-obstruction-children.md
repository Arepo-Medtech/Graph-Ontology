# Acute upper airway obstruction in children

> ⚠️ **Do not upset, sedate or reposition a child with moderate to severe obstruction. Defer investigations until the airway is secure.** Decompensation can be rapid.

---

## Key points

- ⚠️ **Let the child adopt a position of their choice and avoid distress.** Decompensation can be rapid and needs emergency airway management. [S1]
- **Infants are most at risk**, and a child with a pre-existing narrow airway may fully obstruct with otherwise minor swelling. [S1]
- **Croup is the most common cause, but always consider others.** [S1]

## Assess

- **Let the child settle quietly on a parent's lap**, and observe closely with minimal examination. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Hypoxia is a late sign. Total obstruction rapidly progresses to unconsciousness and cardiorespiratory arrest.** [S1]

## Differential diagnosis

- **Presentations often overlap, particularly the bacterial causes.** **Croup:** young child (rare under 3 months), sudden harsh barking cough, hoarse voice, stridor; may be febrile but systemically well. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Epiglottitis:** inadequate Hib immunisation or immunocompromise; high fever, systemically unwell; muffled voice, neck hyperextension, drooling; **absent cough**; low-pitched expiratory stridor or stertor. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Bacterial tracheitis:** systemically unwell, more severe and rapidly progressive, after a recent URTI; markedly tender trachea; thick secretions. [S1]
- **Inhaled foreign body:** young child, very sudden onset, a coughing or choking episode (may be unwitnessed), possibly unilateral chest signs or wheeze. [S1]
- **Anaphylaxis:** facial and tongue swelling, wheeze, urticaria, allergen exposure, haemodynamic compromise. [S1]
- **Retropharyngeal abscess:** sore throat, fever, neck pain and stiffness or torticollis, a full red posterior pharyngeal wall, dysphagia and drooling. [S1]
- **Quinsy:** severe, often unilateral sore throat, hot-potato voice, trismus, medially displaced tonsil and deviated uvula. [S1]
- **Ludwig angina:** swollen, tender floor of mouth, submandibular swelling, with a facial laceration or dental abscess. **Airway burns:** facial burns, singed nasal hairs, sooty sputum. [S1]

## Investigations

- ⚠️ **Moderate to severe obstruction: at high risk of complete obstruction if upset, sedated or repositioned. Defer investigations until the airway is secure.** [S1]
- **If IV access is needed, use analgesia and distraction.** **Croup needs no investigations.** X-ray (chest and/or soft-tissue neck) or CT may help locate the obstruction. [S1]

## Treat

- ⚠️ **Emergency airway management here is a difficult airway: involve the most senior clinician available**, with anaesthetic or ENT support. [S1]
- **Oxygen may improve saturations but does not relieve the obstruction.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Consider nebulised adrenaline for temporary relief in any life-threatening obstruction**, while awaiting definitive measures. [S1]
- **Consider dexamethasone 0.6 mg/kg (max 16 mg) IM, IV or oral** to reduce swelling if present. [S1]
- **For intubation, use a tube ½ to 1 size smaller than usual for age.** Cuffed tubes allow inflation if a leak develops as the obstruction resolves; gaseous induction is preferred. [S1]
- **Treat the specific cause**; for bacterial infections follow local antimicrobial guidelines. [S1]

## Escalation and discharge

- **Moderate obstruction: involve the paediatric team. Severe obstruction or a known difficult airway: anaesthetics and/or ENT.** [S1]
- **Transfer** if the child may deteriorate and need airway management beyond local capability. [S1]
- **Discharge when** the cause is identified, signs have resolved or are mild and improving, and follow-up is in place. [S1]
