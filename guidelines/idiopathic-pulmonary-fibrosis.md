# Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and progressive pulmonary fibrosis

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-anchored; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) or non-IPF progressive pulmonary fibrosis (PPF): anti-fibrotic choice, starting, switching and stopping, adverse-effect management, acute exacerbations, key comorbidities, rehabilitation and transplant referral. Diagnosis of ILD is outside this statement. PBS access for each drug is in `docs/no-guideline-pbs-listings.md`.

> ⚠️ **The PBS criteria for nintedanib in progressive fibrosing ILD are not the same as the international PPF definition.** The PBS allows progression observed over **24 months** (taken from the INBUILD trial); ATS/ERS/JRS/ALAT PPF uses 12 months. Pirfenidone is **not** approved in Australia for PPF.

## Anti-fibrotic therapy in IPF

- **Nintedanib and pirfenidone both slow lung-function decline in IPF**, roughly halving the rate of FVC decline, and are licensed in Australia for mild to moderate disease. Efficacy looks similar for the two. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Consider an anti-fibrotic for every patient at IPF diagnosis.** The decision to start or stop is individual: disease stage, preferences, comorbidities, toxicity risk and funding rules. Australia has no upper FVC limit for mild disease (New Zealand does, at FVC above 90%). [S1]
- **Survival:** pooled trials showed pirfenidone cut all-cause death at 1 year by 48% (HR 0.52); for nintedanib the combined 12-month mortality HR was 0.69 (95%CI 0.44–1.07). Robust trial proof of long-term survival benefit is still lacking. [S1]
- **Do not withhold treatment on age alone.** Nintedanib slowed FVC decline similarly in patients over 75 years and with multiple comorbidities; discontinuation was more common, but most tolerated it. [S1]
- **Severe disease:** post hoc data show benefit irrespective of baseline severity (including FVC <50% and/or DLCO <35%). Subsidised access may not cover severe impairment at baseline; whether to continue should weigh expected benefit against side effects and quality of life. [S1]
- **Switch for toxicity, not for progression.** Funding permits switching between agents for intolerance, and many tolerate the alternative; there is no evidence switching helps disease progression, and it costs time off therapy. [S1]
- **Combination nintedanib plus pirfenidone is not recommended** outside clinical trials and is not subsidised in Australia or New Zealand. [S1]

## Adverse effects and monitoring

- **Pirfenidone:** nausea (37.6%), diarrhoea (28.1%), dyspepsia, vomiting and photosensitive rash (25%) are the common effects. Counsel every patient on avoiding direct sun: broad-brimmed hat, long sleeves and 50+ sunscreen. [S1]
- **Nintedanib:** diarrhoea is the main effect (about 60% vs 18% on placebo) but led to stopping in <5%. Manage with a low-fibre diet, avoiding spicy food, and loperamide. Take both drugs with meals. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Monitor liver function after starting.** Serious ALT/AST rises occurred in 3.7% on pirfenidone (usually within 6 months) and 5% on nintedanib (mostly within 3 months), and reversed with dose reduction or interruption. Both are contraindicated in severe hepatic impairment; intolerance of one does not predict intolerance of the other. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Check interactions:** pirfenidone is metabolised by CYP1A2, so avoid inhibitors such as ciprofloxacin and fluvoxamine (or reduce the dose substantially and monitor). Nintedanib levels can be affected by strong P-glycoprotein inducers or inhibitors. [S1]
- **Dose reductions or interruptions help manage side effects,** and lower doses still appear to slow FVC decline, though the minimal effective dose is unknown. [S1]

## Progressive pulmonary fibrosis (PPF)

- **PPF is non-IPF ILD that progresses despite optimal treatment:** radiological fibrosis plus at least two of three over 12 months: worse symptoms, FVC decline ≥5% or DLCO ≥10%, or radiological progression. ⚠️ Australian PBS access to nintedanib also allows progression over 24 months (INBUILD criteria). [S1]
- **Progression should prompt diagnostic reassessment.** Some progressive ILDs are still best treated with immunosuppression (e.g. CTD-ILD) or antigen avoidance (fibrotic hypersensitivity pneumonitis), possibly with nintedanib added. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Nintedanib should be considered in PPF.** In INBUILD (non-IPF fibrosis >10% of lung on CT, progressing within 24 months) FVC fell 80.8 mL/year on nintedanib vs 187.8 mL/year on placebo, consistently across subgroups; it is approved in Australia for PPF. [S1]
- **Pirfenidone has no local approval or guideline recommendation for PPF;** only low-quality evidence suggests benefit. [S1]

## Acute exacerbations and comorbidities

- ⚠️ **Acute exacerbation of IPF has no proven therapy** and a poor prognosis: supportive care, oxygen, and cover for possible infection. High-dose corticosteroids are common but unproven, and anti-fibrotics have no proven role for starting in the acute setting. [S1]
- **Reflux treatment does not improve IPF respiratory outcomes;** treat symptomatic reflux as for the general population. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Ambrisentan, bosentan and riociguat are contraindicated in pulmonary hypertension with IPF.** [S1]
- **Shortened telomeres raise the risk of harm from immunosuppression;** use it cautiously in these patients. [S1]

## Rehabilitation, transplant and trials

- **Refer every ILD patient early for pulmonary rehabilitation,** and consider repeating it if symptoms progress. [S1]
- **Refer early for transplant assessment**, since the disease course is hard to predict. Continue the anti-fibrotic while waiting, if the transplant centre agrees. [S1]
- **Offer every patient with IPF or PPF the option of a clinical trial.** [S1]
