## Key points

- **May be an asymptomatic laboratory finding. Stop any oral or IV phosphate-containing products.** ⚠️ **Assess for acute or chronic renal impairment if severe; seek specialist advice if symptomatic.** [S1]
- **Pseudo-hyperphosphataemia** can follow ex vivo haemolysis, e.g. a difficult draw or tourniquet. [S1]

## Assess

- **Check renal function (urine output, UEC), calcium and albumin.** ⚠️ **Severe hyperphosphataemia can cause symptomatic hypocalcaemia.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Check the sample:** timing against recent IV phosphate and whether it was drawn from the infusion's IV access; **repeat if haemolysis is suspected.** [S1]
- **Ask about phosphate-containing medicines and supplements, including enemas.** [S1]
- **Often asymptomatic in isolation.** Features: anorexia, nausea, vomiting · kidney failure · tachycardia, arrhythmia, ECG changes · weakness or spasms, hyperreflexia, soft-tissue and vascular calcification · secondary hyperparathyroidism. [S1]
- **Monitor phosphate at a frequency set by the degree of abnormality and symptoms.** [S1]

## Treat

- **Treat the cause, aiming for the normal range;** monitor clinically and with phosphate and calcium. **Stop phosphate-containing medicines or IV solutions.** [S1]
- **Significant acute: promote renal excretion with IV sodium chloride 0.9% volume expansion or a loop diuretic.** ⚠️ **Caution with renal impairment — discuss with nephrology or PICU.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Severe or symptomatic, particularly with kidney injury, may need dialysis — discuss with nephrology or PICU.** [S1]
- **Chronic: dietary phosphorus restriction and/or phosphate binders such as calcium carbonate.** [S1]

## Escalation

- **Paediatric team** if symptomatic or significantly abnormal; **renal team** if renal dysfunction; **consider transfer** if symptomatic or needing monitoring your centre cannot provide. [S1]
