# Acute uncomplicated urinary tract infection (cystitis) in non-pregnant women

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-21 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** non-pregnant, non-catheterised adult women, no urological abnormality, not immunocompromised, no
systemic illness or pyelonephritis [S1]

> Every clinical sentence carries a source tag. Claims that no retrieved source supports are listed under
> *Unresolved* rather than written. Not a substitute for the current product information.

## Treatment aims

Resolve symptoms quickly and avoid progression to pyelonephritis, using the narrowest agent local
susceptibility supports.

Symptom resolution — not bacteriological cure — is the primary endpoint in the trials that determine
first-line choice [S2][S3]. In an otherwise healthy non-pregnant woman the risk of complications is low
enough that symptomatic treatment with NSAIDs and delayed antibiotics is a legitimate option [S1], which
makes shortest effective exposure an aim in its own right: a course of antibiotics raises the odds of a
resistant isolate at the next episode for up to six months (adults, primary care: OR 4.97, 95% CI
2.04–12.06 [S5]; children: OR 13.23, 95% CI 7.84–22.31 [S6]).

## Diagnosis

In women, typical symptoms — frequency, urgency, dysuria, nocturia, suprapubic pain — **without vaginal
discharge** are accurate enough to diagnose without further testing [S1].

Urine culture and susceptibility testing are reserved for recurrent infection, treatment failure, a history
of resistant isolates, or atypical presentation [S1].

## Non-drug treatment

NSAIDs are an option for symptomatic treatment, alone or with delayed antibiotics, on the basis that
complication risk is low [S1].

For **prevention of recurrence**: increased fluids, cranberry products and methenamine hippurate [S1].
Antibiotic prophylaxis is also effective but carries adverse effects and resistance risk [S1].

No retrieved source supports non-drug treatment as a substitute for antibiotics in an acute symptomatic
episode.

## Drug treatment

### First line — nitrofurantoin, five days

Best supported in the retrieved evidence, and the support has strengthened.

Two randomised trials compared it with fosfomycin. Nitrofurantoin 100 mg three times daily for five days
achieved clinical resolution in 70% versus 58% for single-dose fosfomycin 3 g through 28 days (difference
12%, 95% CI 4–21, P = .004); microbiological resolution 74% versus 63% [S2]. The 2026 SCOUT trial (n = 768,
four regimens) again placed nitrofurantoin highest at 74% and single-dose fosfomycin lowest at 59%
(difference 15.5 percentage points, 95% CI 5.9–25.1, p = 0.0168), with pivmecillinam 70% and two-dose
fosfomycin 67%; its authors conclude fosfomycin's first-line role should be re-evaluated [S3].

Its resistance position is also the most favourable oral option. In the Australian series retrieved,
five-year nitrofurantoin resistance in urinary *E. coli* was 2.7%, against trimethoprim 20.7% and ampicillin
41.9% [S4].

**Renal function.** Contraindicated below creatinine clearance or eGFR 60 mL/min/1.73m² [R1]. The basis is
efficacy rather than demonstrated toxicity, and the two should not be conflated — see *Practice points*.

### Alternatives

**Trimethoprim, three days** appears first-line in the reviewed guidance [S1], but Australian resistance
makes it a weaker empirical choice: 20.7% in the retrieved Australian series, whose authors concluded
ampicillin and trimethoprim "should be reconsidered for empirical treatment" in their population [S4].

**Fosfomycin** and **trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole** are listed first-line in the reviewed guidance [S1];
both carry Australian caveats — see *Australian context*.

Where allergy constrains choice, the pattern matters: of 375 US women with uncomplicated UTI, 24.0%
reported one antibiotic allergy and 6.7% two or more, most commonly to trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole
(15.7%) — the agent most prescribed in that cohort [S8].

## Practice points

- **Ask about recent antibiotics before choosing empirically.** Prior exposure is the strongest modifiable
  predictor of resistance and persists up to six months [S5][S6].
- **Interpret local antibiograms with care.** Laboratory data may *overestimate* resistance in primary care,
  because samples reaching the laboratory are enriched for failure and complexity. In a Swedish primary-care
  series, 80% of *E. coli* isolates were susceptible to everything tested, and ciprofloxacin resistance was
  lower than the regional laboratory reported [S5].
- **The nitrofurantoin renal threshold rests on efficacy, not proven harm.** Clinical failure of five-day
  nitrofurantoin rises as eGFR falls (aOR 1.05 per 10 mL/min decrease, 95% CI 1.01–1.09), and the ranking
  inverts across 60: above it nitrofurantoin is best (14.6% failure vs fosfomycin 20.7%, trimethoprim
  20.8%); below it fosfomycin outperforms nitrofurantoin (16.0% vs 23.3%, aOR 0.61, 95% CI 0.39–0.95) [S10].
  Reduced GFR lowers urinary concentration while raising hepatic exposure two- to three-fold [S11].
  Against that, in 795,484 patients matched at eGFR <60, nitrofurantoin carried *lower* odds of
  hospitalisation for acute kidney injury than trimethoprim (aOR 0.62, 95% CI 0.40–0.94 at eGFR 45–59;
  0.45, 95% CI 0.25–0.81 at eGFR <30) and no greater odds of any adverse outcome [S12]. The contraindication
  below 60 has been traced and found to rest on no supporting data [S13]. **Observe the threshold [R1]; know
  that the reason is likely treatment failure, not toxicity.**
- **Men are a different topic.** Always treat, culture-guided, seven days rather than three to five, and
  consider urethritis and prostatitis [S1].
- **Age alone does not change the regimen.** In non-frail adults ≥65 without relevant comorbidity, first-line
  agents and durations are unchanged, though culture is advised to adjust after empirical start [S1].
- **Prescribing behaviour is modifiable.** Audit-and-feedback plus an educational summary raised first-line
  nitrofurantoin prescribing (OR 1.28, 95% CI 1.07–1.52) [S7].

## Monitoring

No retrieved source specifies a monitoring interval, test-of-cure policy, or escalation threshold for
routine practice. The trials assessed clinical resolution at 7, 14 and 28 days after therapy [S2][S3],
which brackets a reasonable review point but is a trial schedule, not a recommendation. **Recorded as
unresolved — not inferred.**

## Australian context

PBS Schedule 4333, checked directly [S9]:

| Agent | PBS | Benefit type |
|---|---|---|
| Nitrofurantoin | listed — 50 mg, 100 mg capsules | **unrestricted** |
| Trimethoprim | listed — 300 mg tablets | unrestricted (also R, S) |
| Cefalexin | listed | unrestricted (also S) |
| **Fosfomycin** | **not on Schedule 4333** | — |
| **Pivmecillinam** | **not on Schedule 4333** | — |

This is where international evidence and the Australian answer separate. The two comparators dominating the
recent European trial literature [S3] are **not PBS-listed**. The agent that won both trials is listed and
unrestricted, so the best-supported choice is also the most accessible in Australian general practice.

Two limits: absence from the PBS schedule means not subsidised, which is not the same as not TGA-registered
— the ARTG was not checked. And the Australian resistance figures come from a single tertiary referral
hospital in Canberra covering 2009–2013 [S4]; 84.2% of those isolates were community-acquired, which helps,
but it is one centre and now dated.

Below eGFR 60 the best evidence favours fosfomycin over nitrofurantoin [S10] — an agent not on the PBS
schedule [S9]. That gap is live, not theoretical.
