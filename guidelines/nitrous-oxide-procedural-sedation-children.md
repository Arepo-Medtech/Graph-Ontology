# Nitrous oxide–oxygen for procedural sedation in children

> ⚠️ **Start at 70%; no weaning. Give 100% oxygen for 3–5 minutes afterwards** to prevent diffusion hypoxia. Procedures over 30 minutes are a contraindication.

---

## What it does

- **Nitrous oxide gives amnesia, anxiolysis and modest analgesia with minimal respiratory and cardiovascular compromise.** Onset within minutes; peak effect at 3–5 minutes. [S1]
- **Sedation uses a continuous-flow mixer titratable from 30–70% nitrous oxide**; premixed 50:50 Entonox can be used for analgesia on a demand-triggered system. [S1]
- **Consider it for procedures causing pain, discomfort or anxiety that need only short-term sedation.** [S1]

## Contraindications

- ⚠️ **Absolute — airway or breathing compromise:** acute respiratory depression, current asthma exacerbation. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Absolute — gas-filled spaces that would expand:** pneumothorax, lung cyst, obstructive airways disease, recent craniectomy or trauma with pneumocephalus, air embolus, decompression illness, bowel obstruction, middle-ear disease or surgery with air trapping. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Absolute — also:** pulmonary hypertension with reduced exercise tolerance · methionine synthetase deficiency, homocystinuria, methylmalonic acidaemia · **GCS under 15 from head injury** · very painful or prolonged procedures (over 30 minutes). [S1]
- **Relative:** acute respiratory infection or history of difficult airway or obstruction · chest injury, abdominal distension, head injury with GCS 15 but ongoing symptoms · nutritional compromise, severe illness or infection, extensive tissue damage. [S1]
- **Relative: age under 12 months** — some paediatric centres with expertise use it from 6 months. [S1]
- **Entonox needs a cooperative child who can open the demand valve**, which usually limits it to school-aged children. [S1]

## Before

- ⚠️ **At least two staff:** a proceduralist experienced in paediatric resuscitation including airway skills, and an assistant trained to give nitrous oxide and monitor for side effects. [S1]
- **Consider fasting times (local guidelines).** Plan for failure — **consider an alternate agent early** if sedation is inadequate. [S1]
- **Equipment:** gas circuit, suction, oxygen and nitrous sources, mask or mouthpiece, and a variable-concentration machine with flow meter, concentration control and oxygen flush. [S1]

## During

- **Saturation probe on, and line-of-sight monitoring at all times.** Start with 100% oxygen and the smallest well-sealing mask. [S1]
- **Starting at 70% is considered safe.** Titrating up does not reduce side effects and may slow sedation. [S1]
- **Keep the bag inflated but not over-expanded, and check it moves with each breath.** [S1]
- **Over 30 minutes of nitrous makes side effects such as vomiting more likely.** [S1]
- **Facial lacerations can be hard under a mask**; a nasal circuit or mouthpiece may help. [S1]

## After

- ⚠️ **At the end, switch to 100% oxygen for 3–5 minutes** to prevent diffusion hypoxia — no gradual weaning. **Monitor saturations for 5 minutes afterwards.** [S1]
- **Side effects:** mild — nausea, vomiting, dizziness, agitation. Rare but more serious — aspiration, airway obstruction, diffusion hypoxia, hallucinations. Tell families as part of consent. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do not discharge until back to premorbid neurological baseline.** If sedation fails, consider ketamine or general anaesthesia; **transfer if an alternative cannot be given safely where you are.** [S1]
