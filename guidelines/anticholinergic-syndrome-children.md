# Anticholinergic syndrome in children

## Key points

- **Combining anticholinergic medicines** can cause agitated delirium, dilated pupils, dry mouth and tachycardia. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Serious features are seizures, coma and cardiac conduction abnormalities.** **Delirium can persist for days** after an acute ingestion. [S1]
- **If you suspect it, get senior advice and discuss with a toxicologist.** For 24-hour advice, call the Victorian Poisons Information Centre on 13 11 26. [S1]
- **Polypharmacy overdoses can hide the anticholinergic pattern.** Seizures, coma and cardiovascular toxicity may come from effects at other receptors, not muscarinic ones. [S1]

## Who is at risk

- **Standard doses can be enough in susceptible children:** infants, children with Trisomy 21 or spastic paralysis, and blond/blue-eyed children. [S1]
- **Common culprits:** antihistamines (diphenhydramine, doxylamine, promethazine, chlorpheniramine, cyproheptadine) · dextromethorphan · tricyclic antidepressants · antipsychotics (chlorpromazine, droperidol, haloperidol, quetiapine, olanzapine) · carbamazepine · hyoscine · cyclopentolate and homatropine eye drops · plants such as Angel's Trumpet/Datura · oxybutynin, benztropine, glycopyrrolate. [S1]
- **Assess:** any symptomatic child, and every child or young person with intentional self-poisoning or a significant accidental ingestion. [S1]

## Recognise it

- **Central sign: agitated delirium** — restlessness or violence, visual hallucinations, picking at imaginary objects, mumbling or slurred speech, fluctuating mental state. [S1]
- **Mnemonic:** hot as a hare (fever) · red as a beet (flushed skin) · blind as a bat (dilated pupils) · dry as a bone (dry mouth and eyes, less sweating) · mad as a hatter (delirium) · full as a flask (urinary retention). Severity is generally dose dependent. [S1]
- **Consider the look-alikes:** serotonin syndrome, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, malignant hyperthermia, salicylate toxicity; and non-toxic causes such as encephalitis, sepsis, neurotrauma, post-ictal states, hypoglycaemia and hyponatraemia. [S1]

## Investigations

- **Deliberate self-poisoning: 12-lead ECG, blood glucose and paracetamol level.** Some cough medicines contain paracetamol, so consider a level in accidental ingestions too. [S1]
- **Consider** salicylate level if salicylate toxicity is on the differential, specific drug levels (eg carbamazepine), UEC and CK. [S1]

## Management

- **Stop the causative agents.** Active resuscitation is rarely needed. **Hypotension: start with a 20 mL/kg crystalloid bolus.** [S1]
- **Seizures: benzodiazepines.** Treat hypoglycaemia. ⚠️ **Hyperthermia: cool the child — antipyretics don't help.** [S1]
- **Mild cases:** reassurance and a quiet area may be enough; 1-to-1 nursing often helps. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Agitation/delirium: diazepam 0.1 mg/kg (max 5–10 mg), oral every 30 minutes or IV every 15 minutes, titrated until resting but rousable.** **Avoid haloperidol and droperidol** — they can worsen anticholinergic effects. [S1]
- **Urinary retention can drive agitation** — a bladder scan shows whether a catheter is needed. [S1]
- **Decontamination and enhanced elimination depend on the drug** — discuss with a toxicologist. **Physostigmine is controversial**; use it only after discussion with toxicology. [S1]

## Disposition

- **Admit symptomatic children** for monitoring and treatment. **Transfer** when care is beyond the hospital's comfort level — PIPER 1300 137 650. [S1]
- **Observe asymptomatic at-risk children for 6 hours.** **Discharge needs a normal GCS, a normal ECG and a safe discharge plan**; after deliberate ingestion, a risk assessment showing low risk of further self-harm. [S1]
