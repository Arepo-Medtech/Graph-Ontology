## ⚠️ The finding: this is treatable, cheap, and mostly untreated

> **An Australian study of people AFAB aged 16 to 29 found that 88% reported dysmenorrhoea, although only
> 34% had ever consulted a healthcare provider about period pain. In contrast, 86% had sought information
> about period pain from other sources, such as the internet, social media, family or friends** [S1].

⚠️ **Dysmenorrhoea is frequently undertreated despite effective therapy being widely available at minimal
cost** [S1]. **Practitioners should consider screening for dysmenorrhoea at the time of other preventative
health checks, such as cervical screening** [S1].

**There can be cultural influences on reporting or seeking care due to the normalisation of menstrual pain,
stigma, shame and misinformation** — but ⚠️ **the rates do appear to be consistent across cultures
irrespective of geographic location, economic status of the country and ethnicity** [S1].

**Clinicians have a role in recognising unique challenges in providing care safely for all patients,
including those living with disability, First Nations people, those with culturally and linguistically
diverse backgrounds, and those who identify as transgender, non-binary, lesbian, intersex and queer** [S1].

### What it costs

**The leading cause of recurrent, short-term school or work absenteeism among women of child-bearing
age** [S1]: **20% reported absences from school or university; 40% a negative effect on schoolwork or focus;
37% a reduction in social or sporting activities; and an estimated 10–30% of those working or studying lose
one to two working days per month** [S1].

## Dysmenorrhoea

**Prevalence varies from 45% to 95% of all menstruating people and up to 93% of adolescents** [S1].

### Primary or secondary

| | Primary | Secondary |
|---|---|---|
| **Share** | ~90% | ⚠️ **around 10% of cases** [S1] |
| **Pathology** | **no demonstrable pathology** [A1] | **pelvic pathology, eg endometriosis, fibroids** [A1] |
| **Pain** | **crampy pelvic pain just before and/or during menstruation, lasting 8–72 hours**, ⚠️ **may radiate to back or thighs** [S1][A1] | **usually occurs later in life** [A1] |
| **With it** | **nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, fatigue, bloating and insomnia are common** [S1] | **changes in the pain, abnormalities on examination, intermenstrual bleeding** [A1] |
| **Onset** | ⚠️ **typically 6–12 months from menarche** [S1] | **any time, depending on the underlying condition** |

⚠️ **The most common aetiology of secondary dysmenorrhoea is endometriosis**; others include **Mullerian
malformations, adenomyosis, leiomyomas, pelvic masses and infection** [S1].

⚠️ **Consider secondary dysmenorrhoea with worsening pelvic pain, abnormal bleeding, vaginal discharge,
dyspareunia, and a lack of response to NSAIDs or hormonal treatments** [S1].

**Risk factors** [S1]: **smoking, menarche <12 years, age <30 years, longer and heavier menstrual flow, low
or high BMI, nulliparity, positive family history, previous sexual assault, premenstrual symptoms, previous
PID and psychological disorders.**
**Protective** [S1]: **increasing age, increasing parity, exercise and oral contraceptive use.**

### Mechanism

**Uterine myometrial hypercontractility and vasoconstriction as a result of elevated prostaglandins released
by degenerating cells during endometrial sloughing.** ⚠️ **PGF2α is thought to have a direct effect in
sensitising pain receptors, and the intensity of pain is proportional to the amount of PGF2α present** [S1].

⚠️ **That is why the timing of the NSAID matters more than the choice of NSAID** — see below.

### ⚠️ Period pain as a chronic pain risk factor

> **There is a growing body of evidence to support the role of central sensitisation in its aetiology.
> There is a high co-occurrence with, or progression to, other chronic pain conditions, including irritable
> bowel syndrome, low back pain, interstitial cystitis/painful bladder syndrome, chronic pelvic and
> abdominal musculoskeletal pain, vulvodynia, fibromyalgia, chronic headache, temporomandibular joint
> disease and chronic fatigue syndrome** [S1].

**Experimental studies have found alterations in the central processing of noxious stimuli that persist when
there is no background pain and in response to stimuli at a distant site**, suggesting ⚠️ **possible
maladaptive functionality of pain modulatory systems, making them vulnerable to functional pain
disorders** [S1].

⚠️ **This reframes the consultation.** Treating period pain is not only symptom relief — S1 states that
**dysmenorrhoea might be a key contributor to the development of** other persistent pain syndromes.

### Assessment

⚠️ **Pelvic examination is not routinely indicated if the history suggests primary dysmenorrhoea**, but
**should be performed, along with a pelvic ultrasound, if there are features of secondary
dysmenorrhoea** [S1]. **Exclude pregnancy in patients who are sexually active and consider screening for
infections** [S1].

> ⚠️ **Both primary and secondary dysmenorrhoea are likely to respond to the same therapy, so initiation of
> treatment should not depend on establishing a diagnosis** [S1].

**Do not make the patient wait for an ultrasound to get pain relief.**

### Treatment — NSAIDs

**First-line therapy for primary dysmenorrhoea**, and ⚠️ **all currently available NSAID drugs are of
comparable efficacy and safety** [S1]. **NSAIDs provide satisfactory relief in 50–70% of women** [A1];
⚠️ **up to 20% report minimal or no relief** [S1].

> ⚠️ **NSAIDs should be taken on a regular dosing regimen and ideally should be initiated 1–2 days BEFORE
> the onset of menses and continued in regular dosing intervals through the first 2–3 days of bleeding,
> correlating with the highest levels of prostaglandins** [S1].
>
> **Patients reported lower pain scores when using a regimen with a higher loading dose followed by a lower
> scheduled amount over a traditional same-dose regimen** [S1].
>
> ⚠️ **Only one-third of young patients take their recommended daily dosage** [S1].

⚠️ **Three things are wrong with how this is usually prescribed: it is taken once the pain starts rather
than before, it is taken as needed rather than regularly, and it is taken at a flat dose rather than loaded.
None of those is a drug choice.**

**Paracetamol may help relieve pain but there is less evidence for paracetamol than for NSAIDs** [A1].

**GI side effects can be ameliorated by taking with food or switching to a COX-2 inhibitor** [S1] — see the
**peptic ulcer disease** guideline for the NSAID risk table.

### Treatment — hormonal

**Both combined and progesterone-only hormonal therapies should be offered to adolescent and adult patients
not currently planning pregnancy unless contraindications exist** [S1].

| | |
|---|---|
| **COCs** | ⚠️ **effective in up to 90% of patients** [S1]. **A well accepted, effective treatment although evidence from randomised controlled trials is limited** [A1]. ⚠️ **Continuous or extended use is recommended over cyclic use** [S1] — **extended regimens provide relief by decreasing the frequency of menses** [A1] |
| **Progestogen-only** | **oral progestogens, etonogestrel implants, depot medroxyprogesterone and levonorgestrel IUDs**; **mostly work by inhibiting ovulation and inducing endometrial atrophy**. ⚠️ **Irregular bleeding is common** [S1] |
| **Levonorgestrel IUD** | ⚠️ **improves both primary AND secondary dysmenorrhoea and is safe in nulliparous and adolescent patients** [S1]. **Useful when heavy menstrual bleeding is also a problem** [A1] |

⚠️ **COC dosing, starting rules and missed-pill rules are in the contraception guideline** — retrieved the
same day and cross-linked rather than duplicated here.

### Non-drug — and one of them matches ibuprofen

| Intervention | Evidence [S1] |
|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Local heat** | **heated pads or patches** — ⚠️ **comparable to ibuprofen, and superior to paracetamol or no intervention** |
| **Exercise** | ⚠️ **low-quality evidence that 45–60 minutes of any intensity, three or more times per week, gives a clinically significant reduction** |
| **TENS** | **superior to placebo**; ⚠️ **helpful in approximately 30% of cycles in people with severe dysmenorrhoea, with lower NSAID doses needed in the rest** |
| **Physiotherapy** | **almost all studies have shown a significant improvement in pain intensity** |
| **Acupuncture / acupressure** | **low-quality data**; **can be considered for those wishing to use complementary or alternative therapies** |
| **Ginger** | ⚠️ **evidence supports it as an effective treatment, with the added benefit of anti-emetic properties** — ⚠️ **no dose given** |

**A1 agrees on the first three:** **some evidence that local heat, TENS and acupuncture may relieve
pain** [A1].

**Most other supplements and medications either lack sufficient evidence of benefit or have unacceptable
side effects** [S1].

### Surgery

⚠️ **Only if a concerted trial of medical therapy has not been successful**, with **preoperative assessment
to look for secondary causes — detailed history, clinical examination, pelvic ultrasound and possibly
MRI** [S1].

## Premenstrual syndrome

**The cyclic occurrence of symptoms — mood or behaviour changes, cognitive disturbances and physical
problems — during the luteal phase. Symptoms begin up to 14 days before, and resolve within 3 days of, the
beginning of a period.** ⚠️ **There is a relative lack of symptoms in the follicular phase** [A1].

**Premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD) is a severe form characterised by severe mood disturbances that
disrupt the woman's life** [A1].

### ⚠️ The diary comes before the drug

> **Establish the cyclic nature of symptoms by encouraging the patient to keep a diary for AT LEAST 2
> CYCLES of symptoms in relation to menstruation.** ⚠️ **If not clearly cyclic, consider other causes, eg
> depression** [A1].

**Lifestyle changes — diet, exercise or rearrangement of workload — may help, and can start while the
patient completes the diary** [A1].

> ⚠️ **Clinical trials of drug treatment demonstrate a large placebo response, which suggests that
> counselling may often be more effective than drug therapy alone** [A1].

### Treatment

| | [A1] |
|---|---|
| **Calcium** | ⚠️ **may be effective — increase calcium intake to 1200–1500 mg daily via dietary intake and supplements** |
| **COCs (monophasic)** | **may be used for mild-to-moderate symptoms although evidence is limited**; **extended regimens may be used in some cases depending on response** |
| ⚠️ **Drospirenone with 20 mcg ethinylestradiol, 24 of 28 days** | **some evidence it may help PMDD symptoms.** ⚠️ **Efficacy in milder PMS, beyond 3 cycles for PMDD, or compared with other COCs is UNKNOWN** |
| **SSRIs** | ⚠️ **consider only if simple measures do not improve symptoms AND the diagnosis of PMDD is confirmed by the woman's diary.** **May be effective taken daily or cyclically; evidence is strongest for fluoxetine and sertraline** |

> ⚠️ **COCs are indicated for PMS and can also worsen or cause it** — more likely with multiphasic COCs, and
> **extended regimens may sometimes help** (see **contraception**). **The same drug class is on both sides
> of this condition.**
