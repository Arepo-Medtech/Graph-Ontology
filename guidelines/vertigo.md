## The one-sentence version

> ⚠️ **There is no singularly effective pharmacological treatment to abolish vertigo** [S1], and
> ⚠️ **there is little evidence of benefit for most drugs used for vertigo** [A1]. **The most effective
> treatment of vertigo is treatment of the specific cause if it can be identified** [S1] — and for the
> commonest cause that treatment is **a manoeuvre, not a drug**.

## ⚠️ What the patient means by "dizzy"

**Dizziness is a term used to describe a range of symptoms, but is rarely a sign of a serious
disorder** [S1]. Three questions separate the groups.

**1 · Vestibular or not?** **Dizziness of vestibular origin (vertigo) is usually provoked or aggravated by
movement and improved or relieved by rest (sitting or lying down)**, while **dizziness which is unaltered
between resting and moving about is usually non-vestibular in origin** [S1].

⚠️ **Dizziness which precedes a blackout or near blackout will usually indicate a syncopal/presyncopal
disorder which requires a different approach** [S1] — that patient is not in this guideline.

**2 · Is it the drugs?** **Drugs which can cause dizziness include antihypertensives, anticonvulsants,
antidepressants and sedatives. The dizziness occurs either through postural hypotension or direct effects on
the central or peripheral vestibular system** [S1].

**3 · Peripheral or central?** ⚠️ **Hearing loss is usually present in peripheral pathology and rare in CNS
pathology** [A1]. **Recurrent isolated vertigo is usually peripheral and benign, this is not always the case.
Central vestibular disorders include brainstem lesions and multiple sclerosis** [S1].

| Points peripheral [S1] | Points central [S1] |
|---|---|
| **deafness, tinnitus, aural fullness or pressure** | **headache, diplopia, facial/limb paraesthesiae, weakness or clumsiness, dysarthria or dysphagia** |
| **mixed horizontal/torsional nystagmus** | ⚠️ **purely vertical or torsional nystagmus** |
| **nystagmus beats the same direction regardless of eye position** | ⚠️ **nystagmus changes direction with gaze** |
| **head-impulse test positive** | ⚠️ **head-impulse test negative** |

## ⚠️ Duration is a diagnostic instrument

> **Vertigo lasts less than one minute in benign positional vertigo, minutes in transient ischaemic attacks,
> minutes to hours in migraine and Ménière's syndrome and for more than 24 hours in vestibular neuronitis or
> posterior circulation stroke** [S1].

**The common causes of vertigo are benign positional vertigo, Meniere's syndrome, vestibular neuritis and
migraine** [S1]. ⚠️ **In many cases vertigo is mild and self-limiting and a cause will not be found** [A1].

## Examination

**A patient with dizziness requires a thorough neurological, otological and cardiovascular examination,
including supine and upright blood pressure measurements, particularly if the cause of the dizziness is not
apparent from the history** [S1].

### The head-impulse test — the one that separates neuritis from infarct

> ⚠️ **The head-impulse test is also useful in the differential diagnosis of cerebellar infarcts and
> vestibular neuritis. The test is positive in vestibular neuritis, but negative with a cerebellar
> infarct** [S1].

**Ask the patient to fixate on the examiner's nose. The examiner then rapidly rotates the patient's head to
either side (after excluding any significant neck problem)** [S1]. **In healthy people the eyes remain fixed
on the examiner's nose regardless of the head position** [S1].

**In a patient with a labyrinthine lesion, the eyes will move with the head when turned to the side of the
lesion and then after a short delay the visual system will trigger a quick corrective eye movement back to
the examiner's nose. This quick corrective eye movement is the abnormality sought** [S1].

⚠️ **A "normal-looking" eye examination does not exclude a peripheral lesion.** **The nystagmus of peripheral
vestibular disorders tends to be attenuated by visual fixation and so nystagmus may not be detected in the
conventional manner** [S1] — and **nystagmus from vestibular neuritis is obvious in the acute phase, but in
many cases rapidly attenuates over 24-48 hours** [S1].

### The Hallpike manoeuvre

> **Perform it if there is any hint of positional vertigo and in all patients where there is no obvious cause
> for their symptoms** [S1].

**The patient is rapidly moved into a supine position on the examination couch with the head hanging over the
end of the couch or a pillow placed behind the shoulders** [S1]; **then the test is repeated with the
patient's head turned to the opposite side** [S1].

**Typically a patient with benign positional vertigo will develop, after a short latency of up to several
seconds, a torsional/vertical nystagmus with fast phases directed towards the lower ear accompanied by
vertigo. This means the lesion is on the side of the lower ear** [S1].

### Dynamic visual acuity

**In patients with bilateral peripheral vestibular impairment the visual acuity often drops several lines from
the static to dynamic condition, for example from 6/6 to 6/36 or 6/60** [S1]. ⚠️ **Normal dynamic visual
acuity does not rule out a unilateral vestibular lesion** [S1].

### Imaging

⚠️ **In patients with recurrent isolated vertigo and normal hearing, there is a low yield from imaging
studies particularly in regard to cerebellopontine angle tumours or masses** [S1].

## Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo — the manoeuvre beats the drug

**Severe, short-lasting vertigo induced by certain head positions** [A1].

> ⚠️ **Postural retraining using the Epley manoeuvre is useful. Medication is usually ineffective** [A1].

**The particle repositioning manoeuvres for benign positional vertigo are 80-90% effective following a single
manoeuvre compared to a spontaneous recovery rate of approximately 50% over six weeks** [S1].

**Recurrences are frequent** [S1]. **The Brandt-Daroff positional exercises are a possible alternative
treatment** [S1]. **Severe persistent symptoms may require surgery** [A1].

## Vestibular neuronitis (labyrinthitis)

**Inflammation of the vestibular nerve, frequently caused by a virus. Usually self-limiting, and may cause
severe vertigo with nausea, vomiting and nystagmus** [A1].

**Acute attacks may be treated as for Ménière's disease. Continual vomiting may require IV fluids** [A1].

## Ménière's disease

**Recurrent attacks of vertigo, tinnitus and hearing loss** [A1].

**Non-drug first:** ⚠️ **restrict salt intake and avoid cigarettes, alcohol, caffeine and CNS
stimulants** [A1]. **The majority of patients will respond to conservative medical management including a low
salt diet and possibly diuretics** [S1].

**Acute attacks:** **prochlorperazine or antihistamines are used for symptomatic relief; avoid long-term
use** [A1].

**Refractory:** **short-term corticosteroids may be useful in refractory cases with relatively preserved
hearing** [S1]. **More aggressive therapies such as intra-tympanic gentamicin and/or surgery are reserved for
refractory cases unresponsive to other measures and where there is significant hearing loss** [S1].
**Surgery is an option if medical treatment fails** [A1].

## Migraine and vascular disease — the two that get missed

⚠️ **Migraine is an important diagnostic consideration in patients presenting with dizziness, whether they
have an accompanying headache or not** [S1].

⚠️ **An acute vestibular syndrome resembling vestibular neuritis and recurrent brief isolated vertigo may
occur as manifestations of cerebrovascular disease. Increased diagnostic suspicion is required in older
patients with associated vascular risk factors** [S1].

## ⚠️ Drugs: what the evidence actually says

### The evidence base for vestibular sedatives is animal research

> ⚠️ **The evidence of efficacy of the vestibular sedatives is based on experimental laboratory animal
> research which shows reduced vestibular neural activity in response to these drugs. Clinical observation of
> this efficacy is anecdotal and there are no controlled trials** [S1].

**A patient can be supported through an acute episode of vertigo with short-term use of vestibular sedatives
such as benzodiazepines, antihistamines or prochlorperazine. Antiemetics and bed rest are also useful** [S1].

### ⚠️ And they may delay recovery

> ⚠️ **The drugs prescribed for acute vertigo should be used sparingly as they may impair the vestibular
> adaptation process** [S1].

**Patients with chronic or recurring vertigo will often do well with vestibular adaptation exercises or a
formal vestibular rehabilitation program** [S1] — and **avoid long-term use** of symptomatic drugs [A1]. The
two statements are the same instruction from two sources.

### ⚠️ Betahistine: three sources, one direction

| Source | On betahistine |
|---|---|
| **A1 (AMH, 2026)** | **it does not improve hearing, and a placebo-controlled trial found that betahistine was not effective in preventing attacks** |
| **S1 (2005)** | **commonly treated with betahistine and/or prochlorperazine although there is no evidence of the drugs' long-term efficacy** |
| **S2 (2021)** | in the pooled five-trial table, **betahistine 12 mg 40 5.7** against **placebo 51 6.4** — ⚠️ **worse than placebo** on the 40-point vertigo score |

⚠️ **Read the betahistine figure with its dose.** The table's betahistine arm used **12 mg**, which is
**below** the AMH adult range of **24–48 mg daily in 2 or 3 doses** [A1]. The head-to-head trial used
**betahistine 16 mg**, still below that range, and there **betahistine did work** — the composite score
**declined by 67.5% in the patients taking the combination and by 59.5% in those taking betahistine**, with
**71% versus 63%** much or very much improved [S2]. ⚠️ **This guideline does not claim betahistine is inert.
It claims the evidence offered for it is weak and was generated at doses below the one prescribed here.**

**Betahistine still has a place** [A1]: **with or without diuretics, for long-term control of vertigo,
dizziness or imbalance** — the only drug either source offers for **prevention** rather than symptom relief.

### Cinnarizine with dimenhydrinate — second line, four weeks, hard stop

**Indicated for short-term treatment of vertigo; not first line, consider when other vertigo treatments are
ineffective or inappropriate** [A1]. In Australia **it is only indicated for adults who have not responded to
other treatments. In view of the uncertainty about long-term safety, treatment should not usually exceed four
weeks** [S2].

> ⚠️ **Cinnarizine has been associated with extrapyramidal effects including tardive dyskinesia. As these
> effects may be irreversible, the combination should only be used for short-term management** [S2].
> **Do not use for more than 4 consecutive weeks or exceed the recommended dose; the risk of EPSE increases
> with length of treatment and higher doses** [A1].

**How much benefit is being bought:** **combining cinnarizine with dimenhydrinate has a greater effect on
vertigo than either drug alone, but the difference may be small** — **approximately two points between the
combination and cinnarizine 20 mg on the 40-point vertigo score** — **however, cinnarizine is not available on
its own in Australia** [S2].

⚠️ **The table does not show a dose-response curve.** **Cinnarizine 20 mg 60 11.5** and **dimenhydrinate
40 mg 59 11.4**, against **cinnarizine 50 mg 98 7.8** and **dimenhydrinate 100 mg 97 7.3** [S2] — **the higher
dose of each component performed worse than the lower dose.** S2 does not comment on this; it is recorded here
because it is in the table S2 prints.

### Dosage

| Drug | Adult dose for vertigo [A1] | Ceiling |
|---|---|---|
| **Prochlorperazine** | **oral 5–10 mg 2 or 3 times daily**; **IV/IM 12.5 mg initially, then every 8 hours as needed** — ⚠️ **IV over 2 minutes** | ⚠️ **short term only; risk of tardive dyskinesia increases with cumulative dose and length of treatment** |
| **Betahistine** | **24–48 mg daily in 2 or 3 doses** | **take with food**; **may take several weeks to reach full effect** |
| **Cinnarizine 20 mg with dimenhydrinate 40 mg** | **1 tablet 3 times daily** | ⚠️ **maximum 4 consecutive weeks** |

⚠️ **These three doses come from a licensed source and are NOT machine re-checkable from this repository.**
They are in the attestation queue and must be checked against a current subscription before use.

### Interactions

**Cinnarizine and dimenhydrinate are metabolised mainly by cytochrome P450 (CYP) 2D6 in the liver. There may
be a potential to interact with other drugs metabolised by CYP2D6, such as antidepressants and beta
blockers** [S2].

**Prochlorperazine potentiates CNS depression** [A1]; for the combination, **the sedative effects may be
increased by other drugs, including alcohol, which depress the central nervous system** [S2].

### SSRIs

**Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors such as sertraline may have a role in the management of vertigo,
however, they have not been extensively studied** [S1] — ⚠️ **a 2005 statement, not retested by any source
held here. No dose is given and none has been supplied.**
