# Low back pain

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-21 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults, primary care and emergency.

> **Australian primary source.** Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care (ACSQHC).
> ***Low Back Pain Clinical Care Standard***, 2022 [S1]. **Eight quality statements** describing safe and
> appropriate care, plus a set of indicators to support monitoring and quality improvement.

**A new instrument in this compendium.** A *clinical care standard* is not a guideline: it does not grade
evidence or rank drugs. It states **what care a patient should receive**, and pairs each statement with
**indicators that can be audited** — plus parallel text written **for clinicians, for healthcare services,
and for consumers**. What the patient is told is part of the standard, not an afterthought to it.

## ⚠️ The goal of pain medicines is redefined

> **A patient is advised that the goal of pain medicines is to enable physical activity, not to eliminate
> pain** *(Quality statement 7)* [S1].

> **Explain that the goal of pain medicines is to reduce pain to support continuation of usual activities,
> including physical activity and work, rather than to eliminate pain** [S1].

Thirty-five guidelines in, this is the first that **changes the target of analgesia** rather than the choice
of analgesic. Everything in statement 7 follows from it.

## ⚠️ Three drug classes ruled out

> **Anticonvulsants, benzodiazepines and antidepressants are avoided, because their risks often outweigh
> potential benefits, and there is evidence of limited effectiveness** *(QS7)* [S1].

> **Evidence has shown that anticonvulsants (pregabalin and gabapentin), tricyclic antidepressants, selective
> serotonin reuptake inhibitors and benzodiazepines have no or limited effectiveness in reducing low back pain
> or disability, and have significant risks** [S1].

Named at drug level, not class level in the abstract. And the consumer text names the disguise these arrive
in: **"benzodiazepines (sometimes prescribed as muscle relaxants), anticonvulsants (also used to treat
epilepsy) and antidepressants"** [S1].

### Opioids

> **Opioid analgesics are considered only in carefully selected patients, at the lowest dose for the shortest
> duration possible** *(QS7)* [S1].

> **Opioid analgesics have limited evidence of short-term effectiveness. In addition to side effects such as
> constipation and somnolence, they carry the risk of overdose and dependency, and can prolong the time to
> recovery** [S1].

**"Can prolong the time to recovery"** is the clause that distinguishes this from a generic opioid caution —
the harm named is not only dependency but **slower recovery from the condition being treated**.

If used for severe acute pain — the emergency department is the example given — **prescribe immediate-release
formulations on a limited trial basis for a defined duration**, as part of an overall pain management strategy
accounting for the patient's opioid status [S1]. **Before prescribing, it is essential that the patient and
anyone involved in their care understand how to use these medicines safely, including their correct storage
and disposal** [S1].

At service level: **policies should limit prescribing of opioid analgesics for low back pain to
immediate-release formulations for a limited duration** [S1].

### If a pain medicine is being considered *(QS7)* [S1]

- discuss individual **expectations, preferences, comorbidities, needs and treatment goals**
- **review current medicines, including over-the-counter medicines**, and previously prescribed pain medicines
- provide information on risks and benefits **including the potential for dependency** and how to manage side
  effects
- **prescribe the lowest effective dose for the shortest possible time, in line with current *Therapeutic
  Guidelines***
- **establish and document clear stopping goals**
- **encourage the patient to continue other self-management strategies after they start the medicine**
- **review early and frequently** to assess effectiveness, adverse effects, **misuse** and **functional gains**

## ⚠️ Reserve imaging

> **More than 95% of low back pain cases do not have serious underlying causes** [S1].

> **A patient without alerting features for serious underlying pathology may not receive any clinical benefit
> from diagnostic imaging. Explain that investigations are rarely helpful or indicated for low back pain.
> Explain that imaging outside recommendations can create unnecessary concerns and that incidental findings
> with no clinical significance can lead to unnecessary tests** *(QS3)* [S1].

**When imaging is indicated** — alerting features for specific and/or serious pathology — **consider specialist
involvement to obtain guidance and appropriate imaging studies** [S1].

### The Australian access fact

> **MRI is generally preferred because it offers better sensitivity and a superior safety profile. However,
> it is not covered by the Medicare Benefits Schedule if requested by a general practitioner, a
> physiotherapist or a chiropractor** [S1].

The preferred modality is **not rebatable when ordered by the clinicians most likely to see the patient**.
**CT has a limited role**, but in the presence of alerting features may be considered when **MRI is unavailable
or contraindicated, or early consultation with a specialist is not available** [S1].

This is the access gradient again, and here it shapes which scan a patient actually gets rather than which
drug — the sharpest example of it in the compendium so far.

### Making the referral and reading the result

**Include sufficient detail on the radiological referral — the provisional diagnosis and the clinical question
being asked — to ensure appropriate imaging and reporting**, and **discuss any risks of radiation exposure**
[S1].

> **Advise the patient that findings such as disc degeneration; facet joint arthritis; and disc bulges,
> fissures and protrusions are very common in people without pain and are usually a normal feature of
> ageing** [S1].

**Patient interpretations of imaging findings can lead to unnecessary distress and fear-avoidance
behaviour** [S1]. Where no serious pathology is found, **discuss the report in the context of history,
examination and other investigations**. **Epidemiological reporting of imaging findings may help patients
understand the probability and significance of incidental findings** — and services are asked to **consider a
reporting template that includes it** [S1].

> **Patients with unchanged symptoms should not undergo repeated imaging unless a diagnosis that was not
> considered on previous imaging is being contemplated.** The need for imaging should be reassessed for
> patients with **changing or worsening symptoms** [S1].

## Assessment

**Quality statement 1 — Initial clinical assessment** [S1]:

> The assessment of a patient with a new presentation of low back pain symptoms, with or without leg pain or
> other neurological symptoms, **focuses on screening for specific and/or serious pathology and consideration
> of psychosocial factors**. It includes a **targeted history and physical examination, with a focused
> neurological examination when appropriate**. Arrangements are made for follow-up **based on an
> evidence-based low back pain pathway**.

**Consider differential diagnoses — for example, nephritis, colitis, hip osteoarthritis and aortic
dissection** [S1]. Physical examination should include **postural assessment, bony and soft tissue palpation
for tenderness and tone, and movement assessment** [S1].

Validated tools named: **Brief Pain Inventory (short form)** and the **Clinically Organized Relevant Exam
(CORE) Back Tool** [S1]. Services should **ensure clinicians have current training in the clinical assessment
of back pain and, when relevant, in the use of validated tools** [S1].

**Quality statement 2 — Psychosocial assessment** [S1]:

> **Early in each new presentation**, a patient with low back pain... **is screened and assessed for
> psychosocial factors that may affect their recovery.** This includes assessing their **understanding of, and
> concerns about, diagnosis and pain, and the impact of pain on their life. The assessment is repeated at
> subsequent visits to measure progress.**

> **Certain psychosocial factors and emotional responses to pain are associated with delayed recovery, and
> their presence indicates the need for further assessment and appropriate intervention. Use a biopsychosocial
> approach that considers the relationship between the patient's beliefs and pain behaviours** [S1].

**Consider using risk assessment tools early in each new episode — first or second visit** [S1]. Note the
repetition rule: psychosocial assessment is not a one-off at first presentation, it is **repeated to measure
progress**.

## Education, activity and therapy

**Quality statement 4 — Patient education and advice** [S1]: the patient **receives targeted advice to increase
their understanding, and address their concerns and expectations**; **the potential benefits, risks and costs
of medicines and other treatment options are discussed**; and the patient is **supported to ask questions and
share in decisions about their care**.

> **Recognise and validate the patient's experience of pain. Reassure patients about the benign nature of low
> back pain, the low risk of serious underlying disease and the likelihood of recurrence** [S1].

**Recurrence is included in the reassurance**, not withheld from it — the patient is told in advance that it
may come back, which is what stops a recurrence being read as a missed diagnosis.

**Provide educational materials consistent with your verbal advice**, taking into account **the patient's
culture and health literacy**, and **address any unhelpful beliefs and thoughts identified in the psychosocial
assessment** [S1].

**Quality statement 5 — Encourage self-management and physical activity** [S1]: the patient is **encouraged to
stay active and continue, or return to, usual activity, including work, as soon as possible or feasible**;
**the patient and clinician develop a plan together** including **practical advice to maximise function**,
addressing **individual needs and preferences**.

> **Prolonged bed rest is harmful and should be discouraged** [S1].

> **Listen to the patient, and validate that their thoughts and feelings are understandable, and the pain
> they are experiencing is real** [S1].

**Discuss strategies to support the patient to continue to engage or quickly re-engage socially** [S1].

**Quality statement 6 — Physical and/or psychological interventions** [S1]: offered **based on clinical and
psychosocial assessment findings**, and **targeted at overcoming identified barriers to recovery**.

- **Few psychosocial risk factors, likely to improve quickly** → **simpler and less intensive support** —
  reassurance, guidance on self-management, advice to keep active. **Offer a review at one to two weeks** to
  assess recovery and the need for further intervention [S1].
- **Higher risk of a poor outcome** → **consider early referral for physical and/or psychological
  therapies** [S1].
- **A multidisciplinary team approach should be used** to address both physical and psychological
  components [S1].

## Review and referral

**Quality statement 8** [S1]:

> A patient with **persisting or worsening symptoms, signs or function is reassessed at an early stage to
> determine the barriers to improvement.** Referral for a multidisciplinary approach is considered.
> **Specialist medical or surgical review is indicated for severe or progressive back or leg pain that is
> unresponsive to other therapy, progressive neurological deficits, or other signs of specific and/or serious
> pathology.**

> **Any treatments trialled should be assessed for efficacy, and ineffective treatments should be discontinued
> rather than accumulated** [S1].

That sentence is the best short description of how chronic back pain management goes wrong, and it appears in
no other guideline in this set.

> **Discuss ongoing compensation or legal actions that may affect a patient's response to treatment, because
> there is an association between compensation-related factors and poorer physical and psychological
> function** [S1].

**For a patient with disabling low back or leg pain, or significantly limited function on review at 2–6 weeks,
consider referral to a healthcare provider with relevant expertise** [S1].

**Refer to a spine surgeon if a serious condition for which surgery is appropriate is suspected, or if there
is nerve compression that is getting worse** [S1].

**On review, assess whether lack of improvement relates to progression of the condition, or to physical,
functional or psychosocial factors; review the initial assessment and current management program — including
psychosocial factors, medicines and adherence to self-management — and adjust the plan** [S1].

Services should **ensure systems, processes and resources support clinicians to monitor symptoms, function and
psychosocial wellbeing**, with **protocols describing referral pathways and supporting timely access**; and
where a service provides only one component of multidisciplinary care, **mechanisms for regular communication
among providers to deliver consistent information to the patient** [S1].

## Indicators — what gets audited

Because this is a standard rather than a guideline, several statements carry measurable indicators [S1]:

| Indicator | Measure |
|---|---|
| **1a** | **Evidence of a locally approved low back pain assessment protocol** |
| **1b** | **Proportion of patients with acute low back pain with the findings of both their initial clinical assessment and screening for specific and/or serious underlying pathology documented in their medical record** |
| **8a** | **Evidence of a locally approved policy that defines the process for review and referral of patients with low back pain** |

## Where this connects in the compendium

The three avoided classes reach across the set. **Benzodiazepines** appear in the insomnia guideline;
**antidepressants** in major depressive disorder and generalised anxiety disorder; **anticonvulsants** in
dementia (carbamazepine for chorea) and as ADHD adjuncts. Effective elsewhere, **avoided here** — and the
reason given is specific to this condition, not a general caution.

The opioid position sits beside **osteoarthritis**, where both oral and transdermal opioids are a **strong
recommendation against**. Two of the commonest chronic painful conditions in Australian general practice, and
neither supports an opioid as ordinary care.
