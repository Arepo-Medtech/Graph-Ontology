# Non-IgE-mediated food allergy in children

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** recognition, food elimination, formula choice, re-introduction and acute FPIES care for non-IgE-mediated food allergy (proctocolitis, enteropathy, FPIES) in infants and children. Counterpart: `ige-mediated-food-allergy-children` (RCH, the IgE-mediated reaction and anaphylaxis).

> ✅ **OPEN AND QUOTED.** 19 of 19 claims quoted verbatim from the **RCH Melbourne Clinical Practice Guideline *Non-IgE mediated food allergy*** (Last updated October 2022); **38 fragments re-checked by machine. 1 quoted doses.**

> ⚠️ **No allergy testing, no adrenaline, no antihistamines** — diagnosis is clinical and treatment is eliminating the trigger. **FPIES can cause dehydration and shock: treat vomiting and resuscitate with fluids.**

---

## Recognise it

- **Non-IgE food allergies are delayed reactions**, usually hours to days after ingestion. **The exception is FPIES**, on average 2–4 hours after the offending food. [S1]
- **They are common in the first 1–2 years, mostly resolve by early childhood, and mostly involve the gut.** **They do not cause anaphylaxis and are not treated with adrenaline.** [S1]
- **Proctocolitis (FPIAP):** first few months of life; stools with blood ± mucus; infant usually well and thriving. [S1]
- **Enteropathy:** early infancy; persistent loose stools, vomiting common, possible poor weight gain; may have secondary lactose malabsorption with bloating and peri-anal excoriation. [S1]
- **FPIES:** repeated vomiting on average 2–4 hours after the trigger, ± pallor, lethargy, loose stools; usually in the first year after solids start. **Commonest Australian triggers: rice, cow milk, soy, oats** — but any food can cause it. [S1]

## Assess

- **The abdomen should be soft and non-tender**; consider other causes if it is distended or tender. **Assess for dehydration**, examine the perianal area and plot weight, length and head circumference. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Petechiae with bloody stools → consider thrombocytopenia and send an urgent FBE.** Haemangiomas can also be in the gut and cause rectal bleeding. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Differentials include** infection (sepsis, meningitis, UTI, gastroenteritis), pyloric stenosis, intussusception, bowel obstruction (suspect with bilious vomiting). [S1]
- **Investigations are not routinely required.** Persistent blood in stools: FBE for anaemia or thrombocytopenia. Stool MCS and viral PCR can exclude infection. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do not do skin prick or specific IgE testing** — it won't help the diagnosis and can harm by driving unnecessary food eliminations. If in doubt at an acute presentation, discuss with Allergy & Immunology. [S1]

## Proctocolitis and enteropathy: eliminate

- **Eliminate the suspected trigger. Cow milk first** — improvement may take two weeks — **including maternal elimination if breastfed.** If little or no improvement, also eliminate soy. [S1]
- **More than 2 eliminations is unusual — get a dietitian.** Suggest calcium supplements for a breastfeeding mother excluding cow milk and soy. [S1]
- **Formula: trial an extensively hydrolysed formula (EHF) or rice formula for two weeks.** No improvement → amino acid-based formula with a specialist; no improvement on that → re-evaluate the diagnosis. Keep monitoring weight gain. [S1]
- **Re-introduce trigger foods one at a time around 12 months, at least 2–3 weeks apart.** Cow milk goes up a ladder: baked goods → hard cheese → yoghurt → fresh milk. **Delayed reaction → stop and retry in a few months.** Most improve by 1–2 years. [S1]

## FPIES

- **Oral ondansetron for vomiting:** 8–15 kg 2 mg · 15–30 kg 4 mg · over 30 kg 6–8 mg. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Fluid resuscitation** (see Intravenous fluids). **In an unwell child or one not improving, keep a low threshold to investigate and treat for sepsis.** **Any bilious vomiting needs an urgent surgical opinion.** [S1]
- **Before discharge: an FPIES Action Plan and an ondansetron script.** Eliminate the trigger and refer to a paediatric allergist, who usually manages re-introduction (some under medical supervision). Most resolve by 2–3 years. [S1]
- **Discharge FPIES when the child is tolerating oral fluids and appears well**, with education, written advice and follow-up in place. [S1]

## Refer

- **No improvement with common eliminations → general paediatrician, paediatric gastroenterologist or allergist/immunologist.** ⚠️ **Poor weight gain, loose stools and eczema not responding → Immunology**, to exclude primary immunodeficiency such as SCID. [S1]
