## Key points

- ⚠️ **Septal haematoma must be excluded on the day of presentation**; delayed management causes significant complications. [S1]
- **Suspected deformity → ENT review once swelling settles, usually 3–7 days after injury.** No deformity → GP review at 3–5 days; any deformity found then → urgent ENT referral. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Fractures with nasal deformity should be reduced within 14 days of injury.** [S1]
- **Untreated septal haematoma** can cause septal necrosis or growth-centre disruption (classically a saddle-nose deformity), and septal abscess with possible CNS infection or cavernous sinus thrombosis. [S1]

## History

- **Midline and high-energy injuries risk instability and complex fractures**; a lateral blow (often assault) is more likely to injure the septum. [S1]
- ⚠️ **High-energy central facial trauma can cause an NOE fracture**, which may bring lacrimal duct injury, dural tears and traumatic brain injury. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Check the mechanism matches the injury; consider inflicted injury, especially in a non-mobile child.** [S1]
- **Ask about** clear nasal discharge (possible CSF rhinorrhoea) and facial paraesthesia, vision changes or malocclusion (other regional injury). Review pre-injury photos of the face if available. [S1]

## Examine

- **Signs of nasal fracture:** gross deformity of nasal bones or septum, local oedema, tenderness, step-off or bony crepitus, nasal bone mobility, ecchymosis, epistaxis. [S1]
- **Open fracture:** a laceration of the overlying skin or of the nasal mucosa on rhinoscopy. [S1]
- **Look for septal haematoma** — deviation, swelling or discolouration of the septum — by anterior rhinoscopy with a light source and nasal speculum after suctioning clots (consider Co-Phenylcaine spray), and by gently palpating the septum for fluctuance or bogginess. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Screen for septal haematoma at the first assessment** — delay can erode cartilage and cause significant deformity. **A septal haematoma mandates emergent ENT referral.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **NOE fracture:** telecanthus and a flattened nasal dorsum (in young children, an 'open book' nasal fracture) — **consult a senior clinician or ENT.** [S1]
- **Associated fractures:** maxillary/zygomatic (tenderness, crepitus, numb cheek or upper lip) · orbital/ocular (proptosis, gaze palsy, altered acuity) · cribriform plate/NOE (clear nasal discharge, CSF rhinorrhoea) · mandibular (altered jaw movement) · base of skull (raccoon eyes, Battle's sign, haemotympanum, blood in the ear canal). [S1]

## Investigate and refer

- **Isolated nasal fractures are clinical diagnoses — X-rays are not required.** CT may be needed for suspected associated fractures or intracranial injury. [S1]
- **ENT for** NOE fracture, clinical septal haematoma or abscess, or where local practice requires. **Maxillofacial/plastics for** non-nasal facial fractures or a possible open nasal fracture. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Transfer a septal haematoma if there is no ENT surgeon on site.** [S1]
- **Discharge when** septal haematoma, open fracture and other significant injury needing admission are excluded, and follow-up is arranged. [S1]

## Advice for parents

- **No sport until reviewed by the GP or ENT**, who will advise on further restrictions. [S1]
- **Cold compress to the nasal bridge for 10–15 minutes, several times a day, for the first 24 hours.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Return if** nasal obstruction progresses, pain persists or worsens, or rhinorrhoea or fever develop — these can mean an evolving septal haematoma or abscess needing urgent review. [S1]
