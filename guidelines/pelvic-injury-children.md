## Why it matters

- **Pelvic fractures are high-energy injuries, usually with multiple other injuries, and can bleed massively into the pelvis causing shock.** [S1]
- Pelvic fractures are **rare in children** and usually follow a high-energy mechanism; they can come with **life-threatening abdominal, genitourinary, spinal, thoracic and intracranial injuries.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Pelvic bleeding is mainly venous and often retroperitoneal** — hidden, so keep a high index of suspicion. [S1]

## Assess

- **High-risk mechanisms:** crush injury · motor vehicle collision (especially ejection, lateral impact, rollover or a fatality in the same collision) · pedestrian or cyclist vs car · fall from height (typically over 3 m) · heavy object falling onto the child (e.g. horse, quad bike). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Haemodynamic compromise:** tachycardia (bradycardia is late), hypotension, prolonged capillary refill, end-organ hypoperfusion such as GCS under 15. [S1]
- **Signs of pelvic injury:** bruising, wounds, asymmetry or deformity of abdomen, pelvis or legs; tenderness over the iliac crests, pubic symphysis, sacroiliac joints, ischial tuberosities or lumbosacral spine; rectal or vaginal bleeding, blood at the urethral meatus, scrotal bruising. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Test stability once only, by gentle iliac-crest compression, ideally by the most senior clinician** — skip it if instability is obvious or pelvic CT/X-ray is already planned. **Never 'spring' or rock the pelvis**; it can increase bleeding. [S1]
- **Log roll with caution — defer it if it risks worsening an unstable pelvis.** [S1]

## Pelvic binder

- ⚠️ **Suspected unstable pelvic fracture: manual stabilisation while a pelvic binder is placed urgently.** Resuscitate if haemodynamically unstable. [S1]
- **If a binder is indicated, apply it before intubation**, and have one ready in advance when the mechanism and pre-hospital findings suggest pelvic injury. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Apply at the level of the greater trochanters.** Too high (over the iliac crests) it won't work and may worsen the injury. [S1]
- **No proprietary binder is clearly better.** If the available binders are too big for the child, use a sheet. [S1]
- **If there are no lower-limb fractures, tie the feet together** with a figure-of-eight bandage or sling around the ankles — this internally rotates the hips and may help tamponade pelvic bleeding. [S1]
- **Sheet method:** folded sheet under the child at the greater trochanters, ends crossed and twisted over the pubic symphysis, pulled firmly by two team members; secure with clamps — **plastic rather than metal**, which can obscure X-ray and CT. [S1]

## Investigate and escalate

- **AP pelvic X-ray.** FAST is controversial and of limited use for pelvic fractures — **don't delay the binder to do FAST.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **No haemodynamic improvement after binder and resuscitation → urgent angio-embolisation or surgery may be needed**; consider CT and get paediatric trauma specialist advice. [S1]
- **All children with major trauma should be transferred to a major trauma service**, following local transfer guidelines. [S1]

## Binder removal

- **Unstable fracture: the binder stays on until definitive stabilisation.** If it seems to worsen instability by distracting the fracture (e.g. lateral compression fractures), loosening or removing it may help — **only with orthopaedics and senior oversight.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Remove a binder only where resuscitation is possible, and watch for deterioration** — a binder can fully reduce an unstable fracture so it doesn't show on X-ray or CT. **If the child deteriorates, reapply and re-image.** [S1]
- **Binders cause abrasions and pressure sores — remove at the earliest opportunity when there is no fracture.** With a stable fracture, consult orthopaedics before removal. [S1]
