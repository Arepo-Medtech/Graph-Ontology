# Antibiotic prescribing in children with a reported penicillin or cephalosporin allergy

## Key points

- **Take a detailed drug allergy history** to guide the antibiotic choice. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Immediate or severe reaction to either a penicillin or a cephalosporin: avoid drugs from both β-lactam classes.** [S1]
- **Non-immediate and non-severe reaction: an antibiotic from the alternate β-lactam class can be prescribed.** [S1]
- **Drug allergy testing (drug challenge or skin testing) belongs in controlled settings.** [S1]
- Up to a quarter of children presenting to hospital report an antibiotic allergy. **More than 90% of children with non-immediate β-lactam reactions do not react again when re-challenged** with the same drug. [S1]
- Three quarters of children who identify as penicillin-allergic do so by 3 years of age. [S1]

## History

- **Timing:** symptoms under 1 hour after ingestion = **immediate**; over 1 hour = **delayed**. Record the duration of symptoms and time to recovery. [S1]
- **Type of reaction:** rash (non-specific, maculopapular, urticarial) · respiratory symptoms (wheeze, stridor, increased work of breathing) · dizziness or collapse · airway angioedema (mouth, eyes, lips, tongue) · mucous membrane involvement · gastrointestinal symptoms · joint and muscle pain · whether immediate treatment was needed (adrenaline auto-injector, bronchodilator) · previous anaphylaxis. [S1]
- **Also record:** concurrent drugs · concurrent illness (e.g. possible viral infection) · **antibiotics tolerated since, especially after the reported reaction** · other drug and non-drug allergies · atopic disease. No specific examination is required. [S1]

## Choosing an alternative antibiotic

- ⚠️ **Immediate (<1 hour), severe** (airway angioedema, bronchospasm or anaphylaxis): **avoid all drugs from the same β-lactam class and those with potential for cross-reactivity.** **Immediate, non-severe** (e.g. isolated urticaria, pruritus): may consider a non-cross-reactive drug from the alternate β-lactam class. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Delayed (>1 hour), suspected severe cutaneous adverse reaction (SCAR): avoid all β-lactams.** SCARs include Stevens-Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis, DRESS and acute generalised exanthematous pustulosis. **Cytopenias, acute kidney or drug-induced liver injury:** discuss with the local paediatric team or drug allergy service. [S1]
- **Delayed reactions: all non-β-lactam antibiotics, or aztreonam, are safe to prescribe.** Non-severe delayed reactions (e.g. pruritus or isolated exanthem): **a β-lactam from the alternative class is safe.** [S1]
- **Timing unknown:** treat a severe reaction as immediate-severe, and a non-severe reaction as delayed-non-severe. [S1]
- **Refer for drug allergy assessment and document the allergy** in the medical record pending the outcome. [S1]
- **Unlikely allergy** — known drug side effects (e.g. nausea, headache) or a family history only: **all β-lactams are safe; remove the drug allergy label.** [S1]

## Cross-reactivity

- After an immediate reaction to a penicillin or cephalosporin, **the highest chance of cross-reactivity is with a β-lactam sharing an R1 side chain.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Suspected amoxicillin or ampicillin allergy: avoid cefalexin and cefaclor** — except for non-immediate and non-severe reactions. [S1]
- **Suspected ceftriaxone allergy: avoid cefotaxime and cefepime.** [S1]
- **Penicillin–carbapenem cross-reactivity (e.g. imipenem, meropenem) is rare — around 1%.** [S1]

## When to consult or refer

- **Consult the local paediatric team:** any child with a clear history of immediate or severe antibiotic allergy; children with non-severe reactions who need urgent or ongoing antibiotics. **Either immediate or non-immediate reactions may be referred to an outpatient allergy clinic** for formal assessment. [S1]
