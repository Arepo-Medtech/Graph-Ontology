# Lacerations in children

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** assessment, anaesthesia, cleaning, choice of closure, suture material and removal times by site, and antibiotics for lacerations in children. Related corpus pages: `animal-and-human-bites-children` (bite wounds), `local-anaesthesia` (maximum doses and toxicity), `tetanus` and `immunisation` (tetanus-prone wounds), `cellulitis-and-skin-infections-children`. > ⚠️ **Fast every child with a laceration from arrival**, and plan analgesia, anaesthesia and sedation before closure — a distressed child gets a poorer result.

---

## Key points

- **Assess thoroughly before closure.** Adequate analgesia and anaesthesia (local ± sedation) improve function, infection risk and cosmesis as well as the child's experience. [S1]
- **A child sedated for closure will likely need sedation again for suture removal — use absorbable sutures where appropriate.** [S1]

## Assess

- **Check for** contamination and foreign bodies, associated injuries (head/C-spine in falls, eye in facial trauma, teeth in mouth injuries) and **deeper structures** — in the face, the facial nerve, parotid/lacrimal ducts and medial canthus. **If a deep wound cannot be examined adequately, refer.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **A dusky or poorly perfused flap needs specialty assessment**; take special care in end-arteriolar areas (nose tip, fingertips, ear lobes). [S1]

## Prepare and anaesthetise

- ⚠️ **Fast all children with lacerations from arrival.** Consider sedation (e.g. nitrous or ketamine) and analgesia as well as anaesthesia for full examination, cleaning and repair. [S1]
- **Topical: ALA gel (adrenaline/lidocaine/tetracaine) applied directly to the wound under an occlusive dressing 20-30 minutes before the procedure.** EMLA (lidocaine 2.5% and prilocaine 2.5%) and AnGel (tetracaine 4%) are alternatives, most effective on limb wounds. [S1]
- **Local: 1% lignocaine with or without adrenaline, infiltrated slowly** — ⚠️ adrenaline with caution in body parts with end arteries. **Ring and digital blocks: 1% lignocaine without adrenaline.** [S1]
- **Bier block only by an appropriately trained clinician**; consider whether the child can tolerate tourniquet time of up to 20 minutes. General anaesthesia for extensive debridement or complex repair. [S1]

## Clean

- **Superficial wounds: 0.9% sodium chloride or aqueous chlorhexidine** — ⚠️ not alcohol-based chlorhexidine. [S1]
- **Deep wounds:** anaesthetise first, remove all foreign bodies, and **irrigate with about 50-100 mL per cm of wound of 0.9% sodium chloride under pressure** (e.g. 19 gauge needle on a 10-20 mL syringe). [S1]
- **Gravel rash:** after anaesthesia, scrub with a brush to prevent tattooing; larger areas may need sedation or GA. **Ragged wounds:** trim edges of doubtful viability before closure. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Glass can cut to bone** — explore thoroughly, X-ray if glass may be retained (small fragments may not show), and evacuate all haematomas. [S1]

## Choose the closure

- **Dressing alone** for simple lacerations (small, superficial, not gaping or contaminated). **Puncture wounds are usually best left open.** [S1]
- **Adhesive strips:** not where there is movement or tension across the wound; space them to allow drainage; **keep dry for 72 hours.** [S1]
- **Tissue adhesive:** clean edges, no deep sutures needed, no tension; **best for wounds under 3 cm**; ⚠️ **not on mucosa.** Hold the edges together for 30 seconds; keep glue out of the wound; control bleeding first. It comes off in 1-2 weeks. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Gluing near the eye:** pad the eye and position the child so glue cannot drip into it. [S1]
- **Sutures: absorbable where practicable.** Monofilament is less prone to infection than braided — consider for contaminated wounds. Vicryl Rapide is braided and scars worse — caution on the face. [S1]

## Site by site

- **Scalp:** firm digital pressure usually stops bleeding; shaving is not usually necessary; consider hair ties. **Close in 2 layers** — galea 3/0 or 5/0 chromic catgut or PDS; scalp 4/0 or 5/0 nylon or absorbable. **Remove sutures at 7 days.** [S1]
- **Forehead:** minimal debridement; **do not shave the eyebrow**; 5/0 or 6/0; **remove at 5-7 days.** **Cheek:** check for zygoma/orbit fractures and facial nerve injury; ophthalmology if hyphaema or a closed, swollen eye. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Eyelids: lid margin or tarsal plate involvement → ophthalmology.** Simple lid lacerations can be glued or sutured with absorbable 6/0. [S1]
- **Lips:** accurate approximation of the vermilion border is required; skin 6/0 nylon (remove at 5 days) or fast-absorbing catgut. Uncooperative child without adequate sedation → GA and surgeon. **Gum-margin (de-gloving) lacerations → dentist or maxillofacial surgery.** [S1]
- **Subungual haematoma:** under 50% of the nail bed — ice and analgesia; over 50% with significant pain — trephine. **Fingertip skin loss up to 1 cm²** heals with dressings; more than that → refer. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Partial amputation or crush:** X-ray for a distal phalanx fracture — it implies nail-bed matrix damage and may be an open fracture needing theatre; refer. **Palm:** careful neurovascular assessment; **compound injuries need antibiotic cover and specialist management.** [S1]
- **Tongue:** most need no sutures; refer to plastics if large, through the free edge, full thickness or still bleeding. **Ear:** full-thickness laceration involving cartilage → refer. [S1]
- **Limbs:** 4/0 or 5/0 nylon or fast-absorbing Vicryl, deep 4/0 PDS; ⚠️ **lower limb — debride, and do not close under undue tension, especially pretibial.** Immobilise after repair; **remove at 7-10 days.** **Trunk:** fat 3/0 PDS, skin 4/0 or 5/0; **remove at 10-14 days.** [S1]

## Antibiotics, tetanus and discharge

- **Antibiotics are not indicated for simple lacerations** — reserve them for bites and wounds with extensive tissue damage or massive contamination, and even then they come second to decontaminating the wound. [S1]
- **Discharge when the laceration is adequately cleaned and closed; refer every patient for early wound review** with their primary care physician. [S1]
