# Type 2 diabetes

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-21 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** non-pregnant adults. Gestational diabetes and diabetes in pregnancy are noted but out of scope.

> **Australian primary source throughout.** *Management of type 2 diabetes: a handbook for general practice*,
> RACGP with Diabetes Australia [S1]. Recommendations below are reproduced with the source's own labels —
> where it marks a statement **consensus-based**, that is stated, because consensus and evidence-based
> recommendations should not read alike.

## Glycaemic targets

**A reasonable HbA1c goal for many non-pregnant adults is <7% (53 mmol/mol)** without significant
hypoglycaemia [S1].

**Older people and residential aged care:** targets may be higher — *"an HbA1c target of 8% [64 mmol/mol]
rather than 7% [53 mmol/mol]"* [S2]. The target moves because the harm of hypoglycaemia moves, not because
the disease differs.

**Self-monitored blood glucose:** 4.0–7.0 mmol/L fasting and preprandial; 5.0–10.0 mmol/L two-hour
postprandial [S3]. **CGM/Flash users:** target range 3.9–10 mmol/L [S4].

**Review interval:** dose adjustments to, and/or addition of, glucose-lowering medications should be made to
attain the target HbA1c **within 3–6 months** [S1]. That is the cadence at which non-attainment becomes a
decision rather than an observation.

## Remission

**Intensive lifestyle change, including weight loss, may achieve remission** — defined as HbA1c remaining
**below 6.5% (48 mmol/mol) for at least three months in the absence of glucose-lowering medications**
(consensus-based) [S1].

Metabolic surgery may induce remission in selected non-pregnant adults with **BMI 27–45 kg/m², diabetes
duration <6 years, HbA1c <12%, not using insulin** [S1]. It **should also be considered** at BMI
30.0–34.9 kg/m² where hyperglycaemia is inadequately managed despite optimal oral or injectable treatment
(consensus-based) [S1].

## Glucose-lowering medication

If glycaemic targets are not achieved, other classes should be **added or substituted** (consensus-based)
[S1].

**The organ-protection recommendations are not glycaemic recommendations**, and this is the structural point
of current practice:

> **Add an SGLT2 inhibitor** to other glucose-lowering medication(s) in adults with type 2 diabetes who also
> have **CVD, multiple cardiovascular risk factors and/or kidney disease** [S1].
>
> **Add a GLP-1 receptor agonist** where the person has CVD, multiple cardiovascular risk factors and/or
> kidney disease **and is unable to be prescribed an SGLT2i** due to intolerance or contraindication [S1].

SGLT2i is separately recommended as an addition in adults with type 2 diabetes **who also have kidney
disease** [S1]. A non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist is named as an option where eGFR is
≥25 mL/min/1.73m² [S1].

So the sequence is not "metformin, then whatever lowers glucose next". It is: metformin for glycaemia, then
SGLT2i or GLP-1RA **by comorbidity**, irrespective of whether glycaemic target has been missed.

**Obesity pharmacotherapy** should be considered for people with diabetes and overweight or obesity
alongside lifestyle change [S1].

## Other management

**Hypertension:** any first-line antihypertensive that effectively lowers blood pressure is recommended.
**ACE inhibitors or ARBs are recommended first-line** for hypertension in people with diabetes **and
coronary artery disease** [S1].

**Retinopathy screening:** examine higher-risk patients without diabetic retinopathy **at least annually**
(higher risk: longer diabetes duration, suboptimal glycaemic or blood pressure management) — consensus-based
[S1]. **Annual screening for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people with diabetes** — consensus-based
[S1].

**Feet:** where no risk factors or previous foot complications are identified (low-risk feet), foot
examination **annually** (consensus-based) [S1].

**Early-onset diabetes:** for people aged **18–30 years**, given management complexity and higher
complication risk, consider **timely referral to an endocrinologist** (consensus-based) [S1].

**Sick-day management plans** are an integral component of diabetes education and should be developed with
the person [S1]. People on SGLT2 inhibitors should be informed about **diabetic ketoacidosis risk around
clinical procedures**, ideally with written information and a management plan [S1].

## Australian context — PBS

PBS Schedule 4333, checked directly [S5]:

| Class | Agents | Benefit type |
|---|---|---|
| Biguanide | **metformin** | restricted |
| Sulfonylureas | gliclazide, glimepiride | **unrestricted** (also R) |
| **SGLT2 inhibitors** | empagliflozin, dapagliflozin | **authority required (streamlined)** |
| **GLP-1 receptor agonists** | semaglutide, dulaglutide | **authority required** (also streamlined) |
| DPP-4 inhibitors | sitagliptin, linagliptin | authority required (streamlined) |
| Thiazolidinedione | pioglitazone | restricted |
| ns-MRA | finerenone | authority required (streamlined) |
| Insulin | glargine | **unrestricted** |
| **Not listed** | ertugliflozin, exenatide, liraglutide, **tirzepatide** | — |

The two classes the guideline singles out for **organ protection** — SGLT2i and GLP-1RA — are the two that
require authority. The oldest agents (sulfonylureas, insulin glargine) are unrestricted.

This is the migraine pattern again, and it matters more here because the recommendation is not about
glycaemic control: it is about reducing cardiovascular and kidney events. The administrative gradient runs
against the organ-protective agents.
