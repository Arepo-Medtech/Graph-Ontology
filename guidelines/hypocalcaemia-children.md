# Hypocalcaemia in children

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** severity grading, investigation, and oral and IV calcium replacement for hypocalcaemia in children aged 1 month to 18 years; **neonates under 1 month are excluded**. Related: `vitamin-d-deficiency-children` (the commonest cause), `hyperkalaemia-children` (calcium gluconate for membrane stabilisation). No adult hypocalcaemia guideline is in this corpus.

> ✅ **OPEN AND QUOTED.** 18 of 18 claims quoted verbatim from the **RCH Melbourne Clinical Practice Guideline *Hypocalcaemia*** (Last updated July 2026); **26 fragments re-checked by machine. 8 quoted doses.**

> ⚠️ **Severe or symptomatic hypocalcaemia can cause life-threatening arrhythmia or seizures:** cardiac monitoring, IV access, specialist advice. Otherwise **oral calcium is preferred.**

---

## Define and grade

- **Hypocalcaemia = total calcium under 2.2 mmol/L or ionised calcium under 1 mmol/L**; symptoms typically start below a total of 1.8 mmol/L. Not for neonates under 1 month. [S1]
- **Severity by total calcium:** mild under 2.2 (asymptomatic) · moderate under 1.8 (paraesthesia, cramps, tetany) · severe under 1.6 (arrhythmia, stridor, apnoea, confusion, seizures). ⚠️ **Any severe feature = treat as severe whatever the level.** [S1]
- **Commonest causes: vitamin D deficiency and PTH deficiency.** [S1]

## Investigate

- **Collect before replacing if possible:** corrected and ionised calcium, magnesium, phosphate, PTH, 25-hydroxy vitamin D, UEC, and an **ECG for QTc prolongation or arrhythmia.** [S1]

## When and how to replace

- **Replace calcium if corrected calcium is under 2.0 mmol/L or ionised under 1.0 mmol/L, or there are symptoms, signs or ECG changes.** Moderate–severe hypocalcaemia needs ECG monitoring and IV access. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Correct magnesium and potassium too** — untreated hypomagnesaemia can make hypocalcaemia resistant to treatment. [S1]
- **Oral/enteral is preferred, given with food.** Consider IV for severe acute hypocalcaemia or ECG changes. [S1]

## Oral calcium

- **Acute oral dose (elemental calcium):** 1 month–12 years **10–20 mg/kg orally qid**, increasing as needed (up to 120–200 mg/kg/day); 12–18 years **1–2 g daily in 3 or 4 divided doses.** [S1]
- **Add calcitriol 0.015 microg/kg (max 0.25 microg) daily**; it increases calcium absorption within 1–2 days. Monitor calcium and phosphate every few days until stable, then every 1–2 weeks. [S1]
- **Maintenance / recommended daily elemental calcium:** 1–3 years 500 mg · 4–8 years 700 mg · 9–11 years 1000 mg · 12–18 years 1300 mg orally; monitor 6-monthly. [S1]
- **Tablets:** Cal-500 / Cal-Sup = 500 mg elemental calcium (1250 mg carbonate); Cal-600 = 600 mg (1500 mg carbonate). Check other products' strengths carefully. [S1]

## IV calcium (severe)

- ⚠️ **Both calcium chloride and gluconate are vesicants** — extravasation risks necrosis; **gluconate is preferred without central access.** ⚠️ **Rapid administration can cause hypotension, bradycardia, arrhythmia and cardiac arrest.** [S1]
- **Target a safe range: corrected calcium over 1.8 or ionised over 1.0.** Switch to oral as soon as the child is stable. Discuss with ICU/retrieval if an IV infusion is needed. [S1]
- **Calcium gluconate (2.2 mmol/10 mL), 1 month–18 years:** large vein IV or IO, **0.07–0.14 mmol/kg (max 4.4 mmol) over 10–60 minutes**, repeated every 4–6 hours if needed until asymptomatic; may follow with an infusion of 0.44–1.76 mmol/kg/day. [S1]
- **Calcium chloride 10%, 1 month–18 years:** large vein IV or IO, **0.07–0.14 mmol/kg (max 6.8 mmol) over at least 6 minutes**; may follow with an infusion of 0.5–1 mmol/kg/day. Check calcium every 6–8 hours initially. [S1]
- **Dilution:** calcium gluconate (0.22 mmol/mL) to 0.11 mmol/mL or weaker; calcium chloride (0.68 mmol/mL) to 0.14 mmol/mL or weaker. [S1]

## Escalation and discharge

- **Consider transfer if** ionised calcium under 1.0 or corrected under 1.8, symptomatic, significant ECG changes (prolonged QT, arrhythmia), neonate, or complex medical condition. Involve endocrinology if a cause other than vitamin D deficiency is suspected. [S1]
- **Discharge when asymptomatic with a stable corrected calcium over 1.8 mmol/L** and a plan for monitoring and follow-up of the cause. [S1]
