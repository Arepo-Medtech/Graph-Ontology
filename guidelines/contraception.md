# Contraception, including emergency contraception

**Edition:** 1.1 · 2026-09-22 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adolescence to menopause, general practice. **Abortion care is out of scope.**

> **Mixed sources.** **S1** is the **open** RACGP *AJGP* LARC review (**2022**), **quoted verbatim** — 29
> claims, 4 of them figures that are machine re-checkable. **A1** is the **Australian Medicines Handbook**,
> read under subscription and **paraphrased** across three pages — 83 claims, 35 doses, **no passage
> stored**.

> **Edition 1.1** added the AMH **Combined oral contraceptives** monograph, retrieved while working on
> dysmenorrhoea (for which COCs are first line). ⚠️ **It closes the two largest holes edition 1.0 recorded:
> there is now COC dosing, and a full set of MISSED-PILL RULES** — which 1.0 named as the commonest
> contraceptive question in general practice and could not answer.

**AMH topics closed by this guideline (2):** *Contraception*, *Contraception, emergency*.

> ⚠️ **The sources conflict on IUD duration, and the OPEN one is the out-of-date one.** S1 (**2022**) says
> both hormonal IUDs are **licensed for up to five years**. A1 (**July 2026**) gives the levonorgestrel IUD

## Effectiveness, in the order that matters

**Failure rate = estimated percentage with an unintended pregnancy in the first year; the lower figure is
perfect use, the higher typical use** [A1].

| Method | Failure rate [A1] | |
|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Etonogestrel implant** | ⚠️ **0.05%** | **3 years**, doctor inserts/removes; ⚠️ **efficacy affected by some drugs** |
| **Levonorgestrel IUD** | **0.2%** | **5 or 8 years**; **lighter, less painful periods**; ⚠️ **efficacy unaffected by drug interactions** |
| **Depot medroxyprogesterone IM** | **0.2–6%** | **every 12 weeks**; ⚠️ **efficacy unaffected by drug interactions**; ⚠️ **cannot be removed** |
| **Copper IUD** | **0.6–0.8%** | **5 or 10 years**; ⚠️ **efficacy unaffected by drug interactions** |
| **COC / vaginal ring / progestogen-only pills** | **0.3–9%** | ⚠️ **the gap between 0.3 and 9 is the whole argument for LARC** |
| **Male condom** | **2–18%** | ⚠️ **the only method that reduces STI risk** |
| **Diaphragm** | **6–12%** | |
| **Female condom** | **5–21%** | |

> ⚠️ **Three methods are unaffected by drug interactions: both IUDs and depot medroxyprogesterone.** That
> is the decisive fact for anyone on an enzyme-inducing drug.

**LARCs have very few contraindications and can be recommended as first-line contraceptive options across
the reproductive lifespan, from adolescence to perimenopause** [S1].

## Choosing

### Combined hormonal (COC, vaginal ring)

**Advantages** [A1]: **regular, lighter, less painful periods; can choose when periods occur; improve acne
and menstrual disorders; reduce risk of PID, anaemia, ovarian and endometrial cancer.**

**Disadvantages** [A1]: **efficacy affected by some drugs, vomiting and severe diarrhoea; need to be taken
each day; may cause spotting, nausea, vomiting, breast enlargement and tenderness, headache, fluid
retention, increase in BP, mood changes, VTE (rare)**; ⚠️ **increase risk of MI and stroke in smokers >35
years or those with hypertension.**

**The ring:** **no daily tablets, woman inserts and removes**, but **requires monthly insertion and
removal, may cause vaginal irritation, infection or discharge, and may be expelled** [A1].

### Progestogen-only pills

| | Useful when [A1] | Catch |
|---|---|---|
| **Drospirenone** | **COC contraindicated / less appropriate (eg breastfeeding, migraine) or not tolerated** | **every day** |
| **Levonorgestrel or norethisterone** | **COC contraindicated / less appropriate (eg breastfeeding, migraine, history of VTE, smokers >35 years)** | ⚠️ **the same time every day, within 3 hours** |

### Injection and implant

**Depot medroxyprogesterone:** ⚠️ **cannot be removed**; **return to fertility may be delayed after
stopping (rarely >12 months)**; **may cause amenorrhoea, irregular bleeding, breast tenderness, acne,
weight gain, decreased BMD** [A1].

**Etonogestrel implant:** **99.95% effective for up to three years** [S1]. ⚠️ **There is no need to replace
the implant earlier than three yearly for overweight/obese individuals due to lack of evidence of increased
pregnancy risk** [S1].

⚠️ **Bleeding is what stops people using it** — **troublesome bleeding is the most common side effect
leading to discontinuation.** Tell them the spread up front [S1]:

- **one in five have amenorrhoea**
- **three in five have infrequent, irregular bleeding**
- **one in five have frequent or prolonged bleeding** — and ⚠️ **approximately half of those will improve
  after three months**

### IUDs

**Copper IUDs are effective immediately after insertion** [S1]; ⚠️ **hormonal IUDs are only immediately
effective if inserted between days one and five of the menstrual cycle, otherwise they require seven days**
[S1].

**Copper IUDs are not PBS-listed, generally costing $70–$100** [S1], and **may cause heavy periods and
period pain** [A1] — **which can limit their use for some patients** [S1].

**Both IUDs increase the risk of pelvic infection for 3 weeks after insertion, and the device may be
expelled, especially in the first year** [A1].

| | **19.5 mg LNG IUD** | **52 mg LNG IUD** [S1] |
|---|---|---|
| **Effectiveness** | **99.7%** | **99.9%** |
| **Serum LNG at 90 days** | **~140 ng/L** | **~280 ng/L** |
| **Dimensions / insertion tube** | **28 × 30 mm / 3.8 mm** | **32 × 32 mm / 4.4 mm** |
| **Amenorrhoea** | **12.3% at 1 year, 23% at 5 years** | **18.6% at 1 year, 30–40% at 5 years** |
| **Heavy menstrual bleeding** | ⚠️ **not licensed** | **licensed** |
| **Endometrial protection with MHT** | ⚠️ **not licensed** | **licensed** |
| **Extended use from 45** | ⚠️ **not recommended** | **recommended** |

⚠️ **The smaller device may be easier to insert and associated with less discomfort, suiting people with a
relatively small cervical canal and/or uterine cavity, including nulliparous and younger people** [S1].

**Both:** **increased bleeding and spotting days in the first 6 months; by 3 years most people have fewer
than 4 bleeding or spotting days per month** [S1]. **In a study, the proportion with no period pain rose
from 50% at baseline to 80% at 3 years** [S1].

> ⚠️ **The ectopic point that gets communicated backwards.** **If pregnancy occurs with a hormonal IUD,
> about half of those pregnancies will be ectopic. HOWEVER, because IUDs are so effective at preventing
> pregnancy, IUD users are LESS likely to have an ectopic pregnancy while they have an IUD than when they
> do not have an IUD** [S1].

**Menstrual cups:** **advise how to check IUD threads after menses and how to gently release the suction
before cup removal to avoid the threads getting caught** [S1]. ⚠️ **Review if threads cannot be felt** —
**speculum examination, urine pregnancy test, and pelvic ultrasound ± X-ray if threads are not
visible** [S1].

## ⚠️ Implant insertion — the site changed

> **Updates to the recommended insertion site followed a small number of case reports of NERVE INJURIES OR
> INTRAVASCULAR INSERTION**, informed by a cadaver study finding **no major neurovascular structures 8–10 cm
> proximal to the medial epicondyle and 3–5 cm posterior to the sulcus**. **The Australian product
> information was updated in January 2020** [S1].

**Positioning:** ⚠️ **arm flexed at the elbow with the hand underneath the head** [S1].
**Site:** ⚠️ **8–10 cm proximal to the medial epicondyle and 3–5 cm posterior to the bicipital groove**, in
the **non-dominant arm**, **subdermally over the triceps muscle**. **Measure and mark.** **If it is not
possible to insert 3–5 cm from the sulcus (eg thin arms), insert as far from the sulcus as practical** [S1].

⚠️ **Superficial insertion remains crucial** — **visualise the needle throughout and lift or 'tent' the skin
while the needle is inserted horizontally into the subdermal plane** [S1].

> ⚠️ **Do NOT attempt removal of impalpable implants** [S1]. **Consider pregnancy test and additional
> contraception, check the previous insertion site and arm, arrange local imaging and removal under
> ultrasonography guidance if possible.** ⚠️ **If the implant cannot be found in the arm, consider chest
> X-ray, as rare events of migration to the pulmonary vessels have been reported** [S1].

**Only professionals who have undergone training should perform implant procedures** [S1].

## Special situations

### Adolescents

**Combined hormonal contraception is a reasonable choice, but long-term contraception (eg etonogestrel
implant, IUDs) may be particularly useful as it is highly effective and less user dependent. IUDs can be
used safely in adolescents including those who are nulliparous**, though **the risk of expulsion may be
higher than in older women (evidence is conflicting)** [A1].

⚠️ **Use depot medroxyprogesterone only if other methods are unsuitable or unacceptable, due to its effect
on BMD** [A1].

### Postpartum

| | [A1] |
|---|---|
| **First 21 days** | ⚠️ **contraception is not necessary** |
| **Condoms** | **immediately** |
| **Diaphragm** | **from 6 weeks postpartum, when vaginal tone returns** |
| **Progestogen-only** | **any time after delivery** (except the LNG IUD); ⚠️ **depot medroxyprogesterone before 6 weeks may cause heavy, irregular bleeding** |
| ⚠️ **COC / ring** | ⚠️ **delay until at least 21 days postpartum — increased risk of thrombosis before this** |
| **IUD** | **within 48 hours of delivery, or >4 weeks after (usually >6 weeks)** |

### Breastfeeding

**The efficacy of all methods is increased due to lactation-induced anovulation. Progestogen-only
contraceptives, barrier methods and IUDs can be used** [A1].

⚠️ **Avoid COCs or the combined hormonal vaginal ring as estrogens may decrease milk supply** — **they may
be considered if breastfeeding is established and other methods are unacceptable** [A1].

**In women <6 months postpartum who are amenorrhoeic and fully breastfeeding, the lactational amenorrhoea
method can be >98% effective** [A1].

### Over 40, and stopping

**Contraception should generally be continued for 1 year after the last period if >50 years, and for 2
years if <50** [A1]. ⚠️ **MHT is not contraceptive** [A1].

**Menopause is diagnosed after 12 months of amenorrhoea after turning 50, or 24 months if under 50** [S1].

| | [A1] |
|---|---|
| **Until menopause** | **progestogen-only pills, etonogestrel implant, IUDs** |
| **Until 50** | **COC or ring, if no cardiovascular risk factors** |
| ⚠️ **Avoid after 50** | ⚠️ **depot medroxyprogesterone, due to its effect on BMD** |
| **Copper IUD inserted at ≥40** | **may be used until menopause without replacement** |
| **52 mg LNG IUD inserted at ≥45** | **may be used until age 55, or until menopause if amenorrhoeic, without replacement** |

⚠️ **In women using hormonal contraceptives or MHT it may be difficult to determine when menopause has
occurred, and contraception should be continued until age 55, when about 96% of women will be
postmenopausal** [A1].

**A shortcut S1 offers:** for patients on an **LNG IUD, implant or progestogen-only pill** who have been
**amenorrhoeic ≥12 months since turning 50**, ⚠️ **a single serum FSH can decide it — >30 IU/L means
contraception for one more year; ≤30 IU/L means contraception continues and FSH can be rechecked in a
year** [S1].

⚠️ **When the 52 mg LNG IUD is used for ENDOMETRIAL PROTECTION as part of MHT it must be replaced at five
years — extended use beyond five years for that indication is not advised** [S1]. **The same device, two
indications, two replacement intervals.**

## Emergency contraception

**Choice depends on time since unprotected intercourse, use of enzyme-inducing drugs, recent use of a
progestogen or ulipristal, need for ongoing contraception, obesity, cost, availability and patient
preference** [A1].

| Method | Dose [A1] | Efficacy [A1] | Window |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Levonorgestrel** | **1.5 mg single dose** (one 1.5 mg tablet or **fifty 30 microgram tablets**) | **84% if taken within 72 hours** | ⚠️ **up to 96 hours (4 days), preferably within 72** |
| **Ulipristal** | **30 mg single dose** | **similar to levonorgestrel** | **up to 120 hours (5 days)** |
| ⚠️ **Copper IUD** | **1 device** | ⚠️ **99% if inserted within 5 days** | **up to 120 hours (5 days)** |

⚠️ **The copper IUD is the most effective emergency contraceptive method** [A1] — and it **provides
contraception for the rest of the cycle, or for the next 5–10 years if desired** [A1]. ⚠️ **It may still be
appropriate if intercourse occurred >5 days ago, depending on estimated time of ovulation — seek specialist
advice** [A1].

**S1 adds the timing rule:** **if all episodes of intercourse in the cycle were in the past five days, the
copper IUD can be inserted regardless of when it is in the cycle.** **If unprotected intercourse occurred
earlier than five days prior, conservative advice is that it should be inserted by day 12 of the cycle** to
ensure insertion occurs before implantation [S1].

### ⚠️ Mechanism — state it precisely

> **Oral levonorgestrel acts mainly by preventing or delaying ovulation. IT CANNOT DISRUPT AN IMPLANTED
> FERTILISED EGG, and evidence suggests it will not prevent implantation** [A1].
>
> **Ulipristal acts mainly by preventing or delaying ovulation; ITS EFFECT ON AN IMPLANTED FERTILISED EGG IS
> UNKNOWN** [A1].
>
> **The copper IUD interferes with sperm movement (preventing fertilisation) and WITH IMPLANTATION of the
> fertilised ovum** [A1].

⚠️ **The three methods do not share a mechanism, and the difference is material to some patients.** A
person who declines a method that acts after fertilisation can be told, from the source, that **oral
levonorgestrel does not** — and that **the copper IUD does**. ⚠️ **This guideline states each as A1 states
it and draws no further inference.**

### Practicalities

**There are no absolute contraindications to either oral method** [A1]. **Neither provides contraception
for the remainder of the cycle**, and **although there is no limit to recurrent use (even within a cycle),
repeated use is less effective than a regular method** [A1].

⚠️ **Ulipristal may theoretically be less effective if a progestogen — including hormonal contraception or
levonorgestrel emergency contraception — is taken in the 7 days before or 5 days after** [A1].

| Situation | Preferred [A1] |
|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Enzyme-inducing drugs in the last 4 weeks (particularly rifamycins)** | ⚠️ **copper IUD** — efficacy of both oral methods is reduced |
| **Obesity** | **copper IUD**; **data are conflicting for the oral methods but consensus is they may be used** |
| **Breastfeeding** | **levonorgestrel is safe; ulipristal is an alternative; copper IUD if >4 weeks postpartum** |

**Afterwards:** **neither oral method induces a withdrawal bleed** though **irregular bleeding or spotting
may occur**. **Most women will have their next period within 5–7 days of the expected time** — ⚠️ **advise
a pregnancy test if the period is delayed by >1 week or is unusually light or heavy** [A1]. ⚠️ **Neither
increases the likelihood of ectopic pregnancy if emergency contraception fails** [A1].

## ⚠️ The combined oral contraceptive in detail

*Added in edition 1.1.*

### Which one to start

> **In general, start with a monophasic COC containing ethinylestradiol (30 micrograms or less) and either
> levonorgestrel or norethisterone** [A1].

**Why those progestogens:** ⚠️ **the main difference between the progestogens used in COCs is the risk of
VTE**, and **levonorgestrel or norethisterone are a good first choice for new users as they are associated
with a lower risk than other COCs** [A1]. ⚠️ **There is no evidence that estrogen choice offers any clinical
benefits**, and ⚠️ **no compelling evidence that lower or anti-androgenic progestogen activity provides any
advantages** [A1].

| Estrogen dose [A1] | | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Low** | **20 micrograms ethinylestradiol or 1.5 mg estradiol** | **as effective as standard dose, with a slightly higher incidence of breakthrough bleeding, especially at first** |
| **Standard** | **30–35 micrograms ethinylestradiol** | **as effective as high dose, but the lower estrogen reduces adverse effects** |
| ⚠️ **High** | **50 micrograms ethinylestradiol** | ⚠️ **generally not used due to increased risk of adverse effects, eg VTE** |

**Multiphasic COCs:** ⚠️ **no advantage over monophasic has been demonstrated, and the variable dose makes
it difficult to change the timing of withdrawal bleeds** [A1].

### ⚠️ VTE risk, quantified

**Incidence of VTE per 10,000 women per year** [A1]:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Non-pregnant non-users** | **1–5** |
| ⚠️ **Levonorgestrel or norethisterone with ≤35 mcg ethinylestradiol** | ⚠️ **5–7 — the lowest of any COC** |
| **Etonogestrel** | **6–12** |
| **Dienogest** | **8–11** |
| **Cyproterone, desogestrel, drospirenone or gestodene** | **9–12** |
| ⚠️ **≥50 mcg ethinylestradiol** | **10–16** |
| ⚠️ **Pregnancy** | ⚠️ **5–29 — and even higher in the first 6 weeks postpartum** |

> ⚠️ **Pregnancy carries a higher VTE risk than any COC on this list.** **The absolute risk of VTE in COC
> users is very small** [A1], and **risk is highest in the first year of use — including when restarting
> after a break of 1 month or more** [A1].

⚠️ **The incidence for COCs containing drospirenone with estetrol, or nomegestrol with estradiol, is
unclear** [A1].

### Regimens

**Most COCs are 28-day regimens with active tablets for 21, 24 or 26 days followed by inactive tablets.**
⚠️ **A hormone-free interval of <7 days is thought to reduce hormone withdrawal symptoms, and in some cases
may increase contraceptive effectiveness by reducing the risk of ovulation** [A1].

**Extended regimens** take active tablets of a **monophasic** COC continuously [A1]:
- **for a fixed period (eg 9 weeks), then a hormone-free interval of 4 or 7 days**
- **until breakthrough bleeding occurs for 3–4 days, then a hormone-free interval of 4 days**
- **with no hormone-free interval**

**Used to delay, minimise or eliminate withdrawal bleeds; avoid heavy or painful withdrawal bleeds; or
avoid symptoms (eg headache, mood change) associated with the hormone-free interval** [A1]. ⚠️ **May cause
irregular bleeding in some women** [A1].

### When to start

| Situation | [A1] |
|---|---|
| **No preceding hormonal contraception** | **start an active pill within the first 5 days of the period for immediate cover**; ⚠️ **if later, use additional contraception until 7 days of active pills** |
| **Changing from another COC** | **start active pills the day after stopping the old pill, any day of the cycle** — ⚠️ **no additional contraception required** |
| **Changing from a progestogen-only pill** | **start the day after stopping**; ⚠️ **additional contraception until 7 days of active pills** |
| ⚠️ **After levonorgestrel emergency contraception** | ⚠️ **start active pills within 12 hours** |
| ⚠️ **After ulipristal** | ⚠️ **wait at least 5 days before taking active pills** |

### ⚠️ Missed pills, vomiting and diarrhoea

*This is what edition 1.0 could not answer.*

| Situation | What to do [A1] |
|---|---|
| **Vomiting within 2 hours of an active pill** | **take another active pill as soon as possible** |
| **<24 hours late** | **take it as soon as you remember and take the next at the usual time** — ⚠️ **contraception will not be affected** |
| ⚠️ **>24 hours late, OR vomiting/severe diarrhoea >24 hours** | **take the missed pill as soon as you remember** (**this may mean taking 2 pills on the same day**), **continue the daily pill**, and ⚠️ **use another method until you have taken active pills for 7 days in a row** |
| ⚠️ **Missed pill in the LAST 7 days of active pills** | ⚠️ **finish the active pills in the present pack, then start a new pack's active pills WITHOUT any break** |
| ⚠️ **Missed pill in the FIRST 7 days of active pills, with unprotected sex during or after** | ⚠️ **seek emergency contraception** |

**What to expect:** **a withdrawal bleed should start during the inactive pills, but sometimes may not.
Continue as normal, but consider pregnancy if pills have not been taken correctly or if 2 withdrawal bleeds
in a row are missed** [A1]. **Irregular bleeding or spotting is common at first but usually settles after
2–3 months** [A1].

### Breakthrough bleeding after 3 months — the ladder

**If another cause cannot be identified (eg missed pills, drug interaction), try in order** [A1]:

1. **change to a monophasic COC if taking a multiphasic COC**
2. **change the progestogen or increase the dose** (**especially if bleeding occurs late in cycle**)
3. **change to a standard dose COC (30–35 mcg ethinylestradiol) if taking a low-dose COC**
4. **change the progestogen again**
5. **change to a high-dose COC (50 mcg ethinylestradiol)**

### ⚠️ Contraindications and cautions

⚠️ **Contraindicated:** **breast cancer**; ⚠️ **migraine WITH AURA (increased risk of stroke)**; **history
of VTE**; **history of cerebrovascular or coronary artery disease**; **complicated valvular disease**;
**compromised liver function** [A1].

⚠️ **Avoid:** **smokers >35 years (unacceptable risk)**; **uncontrolled hypertension**; **other thrombosis
risk factors**; **hereditary angioedema**; **breastfeeding** [A1]. ⚠️ **Generally not recommended in women
>35 years with migraine WITHOUT aura** [A1].

⚠️ **Surgery:** **stop the COC 4 weeks before major elective surgery where prolonged immobilisation is
expected** — **consider a progestogen-only contraceptive** — and **restart at least 2 weeks after full
mobilisation** [A1].

**Postpartum:** **delay until at least 21 days if there are no other VTE risk factors, or until day 42 if
other risk factors are present** [A1].

### Benefits, and the cancer question

**Benefits include prevention of pregnancy and its complications, regular and reduced menstrual loss
(reducing the risk of iron deficiency anaemia), and reduced risk of ovarian cysts and PID.** ⚠️ **There is
also a reduced risk of ovarian and endometrial carcinoma, which persists for 15 or more years after
stopping** [A1].

⚠️ **Against that:** **a small increased risk of breast cancer in some (but not all) studies, declining to
that of never users within 10 years of stopping**, and **a small increased risk of cervical cancer that
increases with duration of use and declines to never-user levels 10 years after stopping** [A1].

### ⚠️ Stop and seek urgent advice

> **Severe and sudden pain in the chest; severe headache; sudden blurred vision or loss of sight;
> unexplained tenderness or pain and swelling in one leg** [A1].

### Two practice points worth carrying

⚠️ **Although indicated for premenstrual syndrome, COCs can also WORSEN OR CAUSE PMS — which may be more
likely with multiphasic COCs. Using extended regimens may sometimes help** [A1].

⚠️ **There is no evidence to support the belief that anti-infectives — other than rifampicin, rifabutin,
griseofulvin and some antiretrovirals — alter the effectiveness of COCs** [A1]. **Most antibiotics do not
require extra precautions.**

**Ensure users of COCs are included in the cervical screening program** [A1].
