# Third and fourth degree perineal tears

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-22 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** prevention, identification, repair and recovery after severe perineal trauma in childbirth.

> **Australian primary source.** Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care (ACSQHC).
> ***Third and Fourth Degree Perineal Tears Clinical Care Standard*** [S1]. **Seven quality statements.**

**The second obstetric guideline in this compendium**, after **stillbirth**. Together they cover the two
outcomes a birth service is measured on.

## The size of it

> **Third or fourth degree perineal tears affect around 3 out of 100 women giving birth vaginally, and around
> 5 out of 100 women giving birth vaginally for the first time** [S1].

**Most women who sustain a third or fourth degree perineal tear recover well with appropriate treatment and
support, although some will need specialised care** [S1]. **While not all third and fourth degree perineal
tears can be prevented, it is possible to reduce the risk of their occurrence** [S1].

## ⚠️ What is measured, and what is not

**Ten indicators — and the list begins at Indicator 2** [S1]:

| Statement | Indicators |
|---|---|
| **1 — Information, shared decision making and informed consent** | ⚠️ **none** |
| **2 — Reducing risk** | **2** — warm compresses in the second stage |
| **3 — Instrumental vaginal birth** | **3a–3d** — vacuum, forceps, and episiotomy with each |
| **4 — Identifying tears** | **4a–4e** — genito-anal examination, and the **rate of 3a, 3b, 3c and fourth degree tears** |
| **5 — Repairing tears** | ⚠️ **none** |
| **6 — Post operative care** | ⚠️ **none** |
| **7 — Follow-up care post-discharge** | ⚠️ **none** |

**The standard measures everything up to and including the diagnosis, and nothing after it.** Prevention is
measured, instrument choice is measured, the tear itself is counted by grade — **but the repair, the
postoperative care and the follow-up are not measured at all**, and neither is the consent conversation that
statement 1 is entirely about.

**Set that against the stillbirth standard in this compendium**, whose **indicator 7a audits the proportion of
clinicians providing bereavement care who have completed a professional development program** — a measure of
the recovery half. **Here the recovery half is unmeasured.**

⚠️ **Indicators 4b–4e count the injuries themselves.** Like the heavy menstrual bleeding standard's
hysterectomy rate and the stroke standard's planned-birth-before-39-weeks, the Commission counts **how often
the thing happens**, not only whether the response was correct.

## Statement 1 — telling her before it happens

**By the third trimester, discuss the potential for a third or fourth degree perineal tear** [S1], covering
**that perineal tears are common and most heal well without complications**; **that third or fourth degree
tears are less common**; **relevant risk factors for the individual woman, including her obstetric history**;
**what can be done to reduce risk**; **the possible use of induction of labour, epidural analgesia,
instruments, episiotomy and an unplanned caesarean section, and their risks and benefits**; **the woman's
preference for how she would like to give birth**; **assessment and examination to expect after the birth**;
and **how a tear will be treated if it does occur** [S1].

⚠️ > **noting that it is not possible to predict who will have a third or fourth degree perineal tear** [S1]

**A risk-factor table immediately followed by a statement that the risk cannot be predicted** — the same
honesty the stillbirth standard shows in *not every stillbirth can be prevented*.

And a parenthesis that does real work: **"noting that many women do not have faecal incontinence"** [S1] —
the standard names the fear the woman actually has.

**Table 1: risk factors** [S1]:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Individual (mother)** | **women having their first vaginal birth** · **women of south Asian ethnicity** |
| **Fetal** | **infants with a higher birth weight\*** |
| **Arising during labour and birth** | **persistent occipito-posterior position** · **shoulder dystocia** · **prolonged second stage of labour** · **instrumental vaginal birth** · **epidural pain relief\*\*** · **midline episiotomy\*\*\*** |

> **\* >3.5 or 4 kg in epidemiological studies** · **\*\* Risk may be indirectly associated with prolonged
> second stage of labour or instrumental delivery** · **\*\*\* A midline episiotomy is associated with an
> increased risk compared to medio-lateral episiotomy** [S1]

⚠️ **This is the only ethnicity-based risk factor in this compendium that carries a qualifier**: the table
attributes the birth-weight threshold to *epidemiological studies* and explains the epidural association as
probably indirect. **The five paediatric documents that list Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander origin give
no such framing** — see `docs/unresolved-audit.md`, Finding 4.

**Midwives providing antenatal care for women with added risks (for example female genital mutilation or a
previous third or fourth degree perineal tear) should arrange consultation with an obstetrician or GP
obstetrician** [S1].

## ⚠️ Statement 2 — things that work, graded

| During pregnancy [S1] | |
|---|---|
| **Perineal self-massage (or by her partner) after 34 weeks of pregnancy** | **can reduce the risk** |
| **Pelvic floor muscle training** | **may help women prepare for labour and birth and reduce the risk** — **ensure that the woman understands the correct technique** |

| During a vaginal birth [S1] | Evidence grade |
|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Applying warm compresses on perineal distention** | **can significantly reduce risk (moderate grade evidence)** |
| **Slowing the fetal head at crowning and the birth of the shoulders** | **may reduce risk (low to moderate grade evidence)** |
| **Perineal massage during the second stage of labour** | **may reduce risk, however, the acceptability of this practice to women has not been established (low to moderate grade evidence)** |

**Warm compresses is the strongest-graded intervention and the only one with an indicator** [S1]. **The
perineal massage entry names its own acceptability problem** — a rare thing for a guideline to say about a
practice it is recommending.

**Episiotomy** [S1]: **selective use may result in fewer women experiencing a third or fourth degree perineal
tear (low to moderate grade evidence)**. ⚠️ **If an episiotomy is performed, a medio-lateral technique with
the incision angle 60° from the midline is recommended**, and **medio-lateral episiotomy should be offered in
instrumental vaginal birth, especially for nulliparous women**.

**RANZCOG indications for considering episiotomy** [S1]: **a high likelihood of a third or fourth degree
perineal tear · shoulder dystocia · a need for accelerated birth of a compromised fetus · a history of female
genital mutilation**.

## ⚠️ Statement 3 — forceps versus vacuum, with the numbers

**Forceps compared with vacuum** [S1]:

| | Risk estimate | Absolute rates (% of operative births) |
|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Third or fourth degree tears** | **RR 1.89 (95% CI 1.51–2.37)** | **14% vs 7%** |
| **Any type of vaginal trauma** | **RR 2.48 (95% CI 1.59–3.87)** | **26% vs 12%** |
| **Facial injury** | **RR 5.10 (95% CI 1.12–23.25)** | **1.7% vs 0.2%** |
| **Achieving a vaginal birth** | **RR 0.65 (95% CI 0.45–0.94)** | **91% vs 86% successful vaginal birth** |

> **No significant difference between instruments**: **any neonatal injury** · **low Apgar score (<7) at 5
> minutes** · **low pH (<7.2) in umbilical artery at birth** [S1]

**Forceps doubles the tear rate and is five percentage points better at achieving a vaginal birth.** The
standard lays both out and then **audits the choice** — indicators 3a and 3b count vacuum and forceps
separately.

**During a first vaginal birth where instruments are used, medio-lateral episiotomy (with an incision angle of
60°) reduces the risk** [S1]. ⚠️ **Tools to help ensure the correct angle include special episiotomy scissors,
such as Episcissors-60, which may assist midwives and obstetricians achieve a post-suturing angle of
40–60°** [S1]. **The protective effect of episiotomy is highest when forceps are used, especially with a first
time vaginal birth** [S1].

**Additional planning is recommended in case a vaginal birth is not achieved with the first approach**, and
**the risks of subsequent options should be considered (for example, sequential use of instruments or
caesarean section at full dilatation)** [S1].

## ⚠️ Statement 4 — when in doubt, grade it higher

**After a vaginal birth, offer to examine the woman for a perineal tear** — **discuss why examination may be
recommended**, **explain what is involved**, **seek consent**, and **document the offer, and any examination
conducted** [S1]. **Offer appropriate pain management and conduct the examination with due respect for the
woman's recent trauma** [S1].

**If a tear is suspected or identified, further assessment is recommended, including a rectal examination to
assess whether the internal or external anal sphincters have been damaged** [S1].

> ⚠️ **Whenever possible, ask a second, experienced clinician to be present during the examination to assist
> with identifying and classifying the tear. If in doubt about the degree of injury, classify the tear to a
> higher degree. Incorrect classification can result in a suboptimal repair and may increase maternal
> morbidity in the longer term** [S1].

**A deliberate bias toward the more severe grade, with the reason attached.** Over-grading costs a more
thorough repair; under-grading costs continence.

**RCOG classification** [S1]:

| | |
|---|---|
| **First degree** | **injury to perineal skin and/or vaginal mucosa** |
| **Second degree** | **injury to perineum involving perineal muscles but not involving the anal sphincter** |
| **Third degree** | **injury to perineum involving the anal sphincter complex** |
| **— 3a** | **less than 50% of external anal sphincter (EAS) thickness torn** |
| **— 3b** | **more than 50% of EAS thickness torn** |
| **— 3c** | **both EAS and internal anal sphincter (IAS) torn** |
| **Fourth degree** | **injury to perineum involving the anal sphincter complex (EAS and IAS) and anorectal mucosa** |

⚠️ **The injury that is not in the classification**:

> **Rectal buttonhole injuries involve a tear of the rectal mucosa with an intact anal sphincter complex and,
> visually, there is no obvious damage to the perineum. If not recognised and repaired, this type of tear may
> lead to a rectovaginal fistula.** **Rectal buttonhole tears are not included in the RCOG classification and
> should be documented separately** [S1].

**A serious injury with no visible perineal damage, sitting outside the grading system that drives the
audit.** It cannot appear in indicators 4b–4e, which is precisely why the standard says to document it
separately.

## Statement 5 — the repair

**Surgical repair should be conducted as soon as possible to minimise the risk of infection and blood
loss** [S1], **in a suitable environment with good lighting, sterile conditions and access to appropriate
equipment and clinical support** — ⚠️ **in most cases in an operating theatre**; **if the labour ward
replicates the environmental conditions of an operating theatre, a risk assessment should be conducted** [S1].

- **Under adequate anaesthesia, using surgical techniques and materials consistent with evidence-based
  guidelines** [S1]
- ⚠️ **A rectal examination should be performed after repair to ensure that sutures have not been
  inadvertently inserted through the anorectal mucosa** [S1]
- **Only appropriately trained and experienced clinicians should repair a third or fourth degree perineal
  tear.** **Registrars should be supervised by a senior clinician, unless they have completed their
  Assessment of Procedural and Surgical Skills or the equivalent level of credentialing** [S1]

⚠️ > **Some women may decline surgical treatment because they do not want to be separated from their baby.
> Advise the woman whether it is possible for her baby and support person to be present during repair and
> support her to maintain uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact and breastfeed during the procedure, whenever
> possible** [S1].

**A named reason for refusal, and an operational answer to it.** The standard does not treat declining repair
as irrationality; it identifies the cause and removes it, and asks services to write theatre policy that
allows the baby in.

**Prophylactic antibiotics**: **the decision should be made on a case-by-case basis, following recommendations
in the current *Therapeutic Guidelines: Antibiotic*. Although evidence is limited, prophylactic antibiotics
for third and fourth degree perineal tears are recommended or considered reasonable in most clinical practice
guidelines** [S1].

## Statements 6 and 7 — the recovery nobody measures

**Post operative care** includes **the opportunity for debriefing, physiotherapy and psychosocial
support** [S1]: **give her an opportunity to discuss her recent experience with the clinician(s) present
during the birth and to ask them questions**; **arrange an appointment with a healthcare professional with
experience in pelvic floor health such as a physiotherapist, as well as with a psychologist, or social
worker**; and **ensure that the woman's discharge summary notes the care received and any follow-up
required** [S1].

**Follow-up** is **individualised continuity of care… to optimise her ongoing physical, emotional,
psychological and sexual health** [S1], through a **multidisciplinary perineal clinic** or specialist
services, and covering **late onset symptoms or signs of faecal incontinence, dyspareunia, postnatal anxiety
or depression, or relationship difficulties** [S1].

⚠️ > **Acknowledge that, if the woman's support person or partner witnessed a traumatic birth, it may affect
> their health and wellbeing. Offer them an opportunity to debrief and refer them for support if
> required** [S1].

**The partner is offered care in their own right** — the same move the stroke standard makes for carers and
the stillbirth standard makes for clinicians.

⚠️ > **Advise women that a third or fourth degree tear does not exclude a subsequent vaginal birth** [S1].

**And because faecal incontinence may emerge late**: **inform women that support is available if needed and
encourage them to report symptoms** [S1].
