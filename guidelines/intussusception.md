# Intussusception in children

## Recognise it

- ⚠️ **Keep a high index of suspicion:** consider it in infants and children with intermittent distress, pallor, vomiting, or isolated unexplained lethargy. [S1]
- **Most common between 3 months and 2 years**, peaking at 4–9 months. Younger infants may present atypically and more acutely; older children may have a pathological lead point. [S1]
- **Typical pain is paroxysmal (colicky), severe and cyclic, lasting 4–5 minutes**, recurring over minutes to hours; the child may look very well between episodes. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Lethargy may be the only presenting symptom.** Bile-stained vomiting is a late sign of bowel obstruction. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Early diarrhoea can lead to a misdiagnosis of gastroenteritis.** Rectal bleeding or "red currant jelly" stool is a late sign of ischaemia; the classic triad occurs in under 15%. [S1]
- **Examine between episodes:** a sausage-shaped right-sided mass may be felt; distension suggests obstruction; tenderness or guarding suggests perforation. Rectal examination is rarely indicated. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Delayed presentation** can mean small bowel obstruction, perforation, peritonitis or shock; infants may present in hypovolaemic shock. [S1]
- **Risk factors:** male sex (3:1), recent intussusception (may present more subtly), Henoch–Schönlein purpura. RCH states **current rotavirus vaccines are not linked to intussusception** — see Unresolved. [S1]

## Resuscitate first

- ⚠️ **Resuscitation and analgesia precede investigation. Secure IV access before imaging.** [S1]
- **Treat hypovolaemic shock with IV boluses of 0.9% saline**, then IV fluids to optimise circulating volume. [S1]
- **Analgesia: usually intranasal fentanyl or IV morphine.** [S1]
- **Nil by mouth.** Pass an NG tube for obstruction or perforation on AXR, or before air transfer. [S1]

## Investigate

- **Ultrasound is the initial study of choice** — over 98% sensitive and specific with an experienced paediatric sonographer. [S1]
- ⚠️ **POCUS by trained clinicians can confirm but must not be used to exclude intussusception.** [S1]
- **Ileo-ileal intussusception** (two small-bowel segments) may present with mild symptoms, is more likely to resolve spontaneously, and rarely needs enema reduction, as ischaemia is rare. [S1]
- ⚠️ **AXR only for signs of obstruction or perforation. A normal AXR does not exclude intussusception** (sensitivity under 50%), and it must not delay transfer. [S1]
- **Bloods:** glucose; venous gas, FBE and UEC if unwell; group and hold before theatre. [S1]

## Enema reduction

- **Contrast or gas enema is diagnostic and therapeutic**, done with a surgical team **where paediatric surgery is available**, because of a small risk of perforation and bacteraemia. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Enema is contraindicated** in peritonitis, shock, radiological perforation, or an unstable child. Fluid resuscitation comes before sedation and reduction. [S1]
- **Strongly consider procedural sedation**, which may improve success of hydrostatic and pneumatic reduction. **No routine prophylactic antibiotics.** [S1]
- **Success exceeds 80%**; repeat enemas may add 10%. **Recurrence is most common in the first 24–48 hours.** [S1]

## Disposition

- ⚠️ **Discuss every suspected intussusception with a paediatric surgical team.** [S1]
- **After non-operative reduction, surgical admission is routine in most centres**; with the surgical team's agreement, a feed may be trialled at 2 hours and ED discharge considered 2 hours after a successful feed. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Before transfer, ensure analgesia, venous access and IV fluids** — third-space losses can cause haemodynamic collapse. **NG tube on free drainage for air transfer.** [S1]
