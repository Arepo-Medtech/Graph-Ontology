# Hepatitis B

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-21 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults, primary care — **first response, monitoring and contacts. Not regimen selection.**

> **Australian primary source.** ASHM *Australian STI Management Guidelines for use in primary care* —
> *Hepatitis B* [S1], modified **29 June 2026**.

## ⚠️ A healthy carrier does not exist

> **Anyone with a positive hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) needs ongoing monitoring and needs to be
> considered for treatment. A healthy carrier does not exist** [S1].

Six words retiring a term still in circulation. The practical consequence is that **"carrier" is not a
disposition** — an HBsAg-positive result without symptoms is an indication for lifelong follow-up, not
reassurance.

> **Chronic hepatitis B infection (HBsAg positive) can cause hepatocellular carcinoma even in the absence of
> liver cirrhosis** [S1].

That is why. The usual mental model — cancer follows cirrhosis, so watch for cirrhosis — does not hold here.

## What this guideline does not contain

**No antiviral appears below, because none appears in the source.** Both diagnosis and management are
delegated in full:

> **For assistance with ordering and interpreting hepatitis B serology, please follow the ASHM Decision
> making tool for hepatitis B** [S1].
> **For management of hepatitis B, please follow the ASHM Decision making tool for hepatitis B** [S1].

This is the second topic in the set that names no drug — and it delegates further than HIV did, which at
least gave the "start ART at diagnosis" rule before handing off. Serology interpretation and regimens are
recorded as **`input_unavailable`** rather than reconstructed.

What the page does give primary care is the surveillance schedule, the contacts, and the situations where
hepatitis B changes another decision.

## ⚠️ Time-sensitive: the vaccination-era heuristic

> **In Australia, routine adolescent immunisation commenced in 1997 and universal infant vaccination
> commenced in May 2000. Therefore those who were 34 years old or younger in 2020 and who grew up in
> Australia can generally be assumed to have been vaccinated and do not need testing. However, for certain
> high-risk population groups it may still be warranted to check their hepatitis B serology (e.g. people
> with HIV, or people who inject drugs)** [S1].

**The age is anchored to 2020, not to today.** The programme dates are fixed; the age cut-off is not a
standing number and will mislead anyone who applies "34 or younger" to the current year. Marked
`time_sensitive` — **carry the arithmetic forward from the stated programme dates, not from the stated
age.**

## Who is affected

- **Risk of developing chronic hepatitis B is >90% if infection is acquired at birth or under 5 years, and
  <5% if acquired as an adult** [S1]. Age at acquisition, not exposure severity, drives chronicity.
- **Most people living with chronic hepatitis B in Australia were born overseas** — particularly the Asia
  Pacific region, Europe, Africa and the Middle East [S1].
- **Elevated prevalence among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people living in remote areas, and among
  some culturally and linguistically diverse populations** [S1].
- **Transmission: mother to child (vertical); parenteral exposure such as shared injection equipment; or
  sexual or household contact (horizontal)** [S1].

**Priority populations for HBV testing** are held at the ASHM Hepatitis B Testing Portal [S1].

## ⚠️ What is not a transmission risk

> **HBV is a blood borne virus and sexually transmissible infection, and hence casual household contact
> (e.g. sharing cutlery) is not a risk for transmission of hepatitis B** [S1].

Stated inside the contact-tracing section, where it matters most: household contacts are tested because of
shared blood or sexual exposure, **not** because they eat at the same table. Nothing else in the set
explicitly names a *non*-route of transmission.

## Where hepatitis B changes another decision

| Situation | Recommended [S1] |
|---|---|
| **People who use HIV PrEP** | **People living with hepatitis B can use PrEP if at risk of HIV, but must use the daily method rather than the on-demand method** |
| **People who need HIV PEP** | **Seek immediate specialist advice** — **some PEP medications also treat HBV** |
| **Pregnancy** | **Needs specialist review. The infant needs hepatitis B vaccination and immunoglobulin (HBIG) at birth; the pregnant person may need antiviral therapy to reduce transmission at birth. People who have given birth may be at risk of hepatitis B flares in the post-natal period** |

The PrEP row is the kind of interaction a per-condition library loses: the HIV guideline recommends PrEP,
and hepatitis B constrains **which** PrEP. The PEP row is subtler still — starting or stopping an HIV drug
can act on the hepatitis B as a side effect, in either direction.

The post-natal flare is worth flagging: the risk period continues **after** delivery, when antenatal care
has ended.

## Immediate management

- **Advise no sexual contact during acute illness unless the partner is immune** [S1].
- **Commence contact tracing.** **Consider testing sexual contacts, household contacts and close family
  members, and vaccinate if not immune** [S1].
- **Notify the state or territory health department** [S1].
- **Consider comprehensive STI testing, depending on the patient's sexual history** [S1].

## Contact tracing

**Notifiable condition.** **Contact tracing is important to prevent re-infection and reduce
transmission** [S1].

- **For acute hepatitis B: trace back 6 months before onset of symptoms** [S1].
- > **Most people diagnosed with hepatitis B in Australia will have acquired hepatitis B at birth, and hence
  > contact tracing will need to be conducted differently than for acute hepatitis B** [S1].

  The trace-back interval applies to a minority of the people who will be diagnosed. For the majority, the
  exposure was decades ago and the relevant contacts are family, not recent partners.
- **Infectious for 2 weeks before onset of symptoms and until the patient becomes HBsAg negative —
  lifelong if chronic infection** [S1].
- **Test sexual and household contacts and family members, offer vaccination if susceptible, and further
  assessment for those with current infection** [S1].
- **Hepatitis B immunoglobulin (HBIG) can be given as post-exposure prophylaxis in certain high-risk
  situations — sexual, injecting or occupational exposure — or as a birth dose to reduce transmission risk
  from an HBsAg-positive person to their child** [S1].

## Follow-up — the surveillance schedule

- **Acute infection should be monitored until HBsAg negative** [S1].
- **Chronic infection requires lifelong follow-up**, including **6–12 monthly hepatitis B monitoring,
  yearly hepatitis B DNA viral load, liver function testing, and antiviral therapy if indicated** [S1].
- **Certain populations with chronic hepatitis B require 6-monthly hepatocellular carcinoma screening —
  abdominal ultrasound and alpha-fetoprotein** [S1].
- **Check hepatitis A status and vaccinate if susceptible** [S1].
- **Check hepatitis C status, and discuss with a hepatologist if hepatitis C positive** [S1].
- **Assess patients with chronic hepatitis B for other causes of hepatitis — e.g. alcohol, fatty liver — and
  counsel to reduce these factors if relevant** [S1].
- > **Check hepatitis D status in patients diagnosed with hepatitis B who were born in hepatitis D virus
  > (HDV) endemic regions, including Mongolia, Moldova, West and Central Africa** [S1].

  The only named-country test trigger in twenty-eight guidelines.

**Prior infection provides lifelong immunity to re-infection. Patients with resolved infection may
experience a hepatitis B flare if immunocompromised — e.g. if receiving chemotherapy** [S1].

"Resolved" is therefore not "finished". That fact belongs in the notes of anyone who may later need
immunosuppression.

## Auditable outcomes

- **100% of patients with chronic hepatitis B infection have annual reviews** [S1].
- **100% of sexual, household and family contacts are tested and vaccinated if susceptible** [S1].

## Presentation

- **Asymptomatic infection: common** [S1].
- **Acute hepatitis: right upper quadrant pain, lethargy, nausea, fever, anorexia for a few days, then
  jaundice. Incubation 45–180 days (mean 60 days)** [S1].
- **Chronic hepatitis: infection lasting over 6 months, can be lifelong** [S1].
- **Complications: liver cirrhosis and liver failure; hepatocellular carcinoma** [S1].
