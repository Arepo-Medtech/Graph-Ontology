# Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease in infants

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** distinguishing physiological reflux (GOR) from reflux disease (GORD) in otherwise healthy infants, red flags, feeding measures, cow milk protein exclusion and acid suppression. The adult counterpart is `gord-and-dyspepsia` (PPI therapy in adults). For the vomiting child in general see `vomiting`.

> ⚠️ **Reflux in a thriving infant needs no tests and no drugs. Do not use a PPI as a diagnostic trial for an irritable infant.** Reconsider the diagnosis if any red flag is present.

## GOR or GORD?

- **GOR is common in healthy, thriving infants and needs no investigation or treatment.** [S1]
- **GORD = troublesome symptoms** from GOR that adversely affect feeding, growth or respiratory status. [S1]
- **GOR affects at least 40–65% of healthy infants**, usually starts before 8 weeks, peaks at 4 months and mostly resolves by 1 year. **It does not cause crying and irritability in healthy infants.** It rarely needs investigation and is usually managed with education, support and anticipatory guidance. [S1]
- **There is insufficient evidence to support diagnosing or managing "silent reflux".** [S1]
- **Consider cow milk protein allergy (CMPA)** in infants with GORD symptoms — more likely with blood or mucus in the stool, chronic diarrhoea, or personal or family atopy. [S1]

## Red flags — reconsider the diagnosis

- ⚠️ **General:** vomiting starting under 1 week or over 6 months of age · increasing or persisting beyond 12–18 months · lethargy or fever · excessive irritability · weight loss or faltering growth · feeding problems or oral aversion · respiratory symptoms. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Neurological:** seizures · micro- or macrocephaly · developmental delay · nocturnal vomiting (possible raised ICP). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Gastrointestinal:** bilious vomiting or abdominal rigidity/distension → intestinal obstruction · forceful vomiting → pyloric stenosis · haematemesis → possible severe upper GI bleed. [S1]
- **Investigations are rarely indicated.** Barium studies, pH-impedance or endoscopy only on an individual basis after paediatrician or paediatric gastroenterologist assessment. [S1]

## General measures

- **Hold the infant head-up for 20–30 minutes after feeds.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Never recommend prone or side sleeping, or inclining the sleep surface** — SIDS risk. [S1]
- **Continue direct breastfeeding** — thickening brings only modest benefit and expressing is a significant burden. In bottle-fed babies, thickened feeds may reduce vomiting. [S1]
- **Do not thicken breast milk with cereal-based thickeners** — breast milk amylases digest them. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Thickened feeds can cause abdominal pain, diarrhoea or constipation. Make sure the infant is not also getting alginate (Gaviscon Infant®)** — co-administration may increase the risk of bowel obstruction. [S1]
- **Try smaller, more frequent feeds**, and reduce the volume if total intake is excessive for weight. [S1]
- **GOR alone is no reason to change formula or stop breastfeeding**; no brand of standard cow milk formula is better than another. [S1]
- **Prebiotics, probiotics and herbal medicines are not recommended** for GOR or GORD. [S1]

## Specific measures for GORD

- **Suspected CMPA: 2–4 week trial of strict cow milk protein elimination, and refer to a paediatrician.** Breastfed infants: strict maternal avoidance. Formula-fed: specialised formula. [S1]
- **Acid suppression: first-line PPI (e.g. omeprazole or lansoprazole) as a 2–4 week trial.** If it helps, continue 4–8 weeks, then consider weaning; review the need for ongoing therapy. [S1]
- ⚠️ **PPIs carry risk** — community-acquired pneumonia, gastroenteritis, future fractures, micronutrient deficiencies. **Do not use acid suppression as a diagnostic trial for irritable infants.** [S1]
- **Alginate (Gaviscon Infant®)** may reduce visible regurgitation, with limited evidence; **long-term use in infants is not recommended.** [S1]
- **Prokinetics only under specialist direction**, possibly before surgery when other drugs have failed. [S1]
- **Surgery (e.g. fundoplication) is reserved** for intractable GORD unresponsive to optimal medical therapy, or significant complications. [S1]

## Escalate and discharge

- **Involve the paediatric team** if red flags are present or GORD does not respond to initial management. [S1]
- **GOR and GORD can almost always be managed as outpatients** — ensure carer support and handouts, and arrange timely follow-up to assess response. [S1]
