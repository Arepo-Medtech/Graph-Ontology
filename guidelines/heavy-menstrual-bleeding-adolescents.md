# Heavy menstrual bleeding in adolescents

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** causes, severity grading, acute and prophylactic non-hormonal and hormonal treatment, and escalation for heavy menstrual bleeding in adolescents. The general counterpart is `heavy-menstrual-bleeding` (ACSQHC Clinical Care Standard); period pain is `dysmenorrhoea-adolescents`.

> ✅ **OPEN AND QUOTED.** 22 of 22 claims quoted verbatim from the **RCH Melbourne Clinical Practice Guideline *Adolescent gynaecology - heavy menstrual bleeding*** (Last updated June 2025); **30 fragments re-checked by machine. 7 quoted doses.**

> ⚠️ **Severe = haemoglobin under 100 g/L and/or haemodynamic instability:** IV access, resuscitation, tranexamic acid, high-dose hormonal therapy and gynaecology review. **Always consider pregnancy and a bleeding disorder.**

---

## Recognise it

- **The most common cause in adolescents is anovulatory cycles.** ⚠️ **Pregnancy-related bleeding and bleeding disorders are important differentials.** [S1]
- **Excessive flow** = more than 7 days or more than 80 mL per cycle · changing a super pad/tampon more often than every 2 hours · symptomatic anaemia · lifestyle disturbance. [S1]
- **Causes to consider:** pregnancy (miscarriage, ectopic, gestational trophoblastic disease); **von Willebrand disease**, platelet function disorder, thrombocytopenia; thyroid dysfunction, PCOS; hormonal contraception and anticoagulants. [S1]
- **Severity:** mild — slightly or moderately increased flow, normal haemoglobin · moderate — menses every 1–3 weeks, moderate–heavy flow, haemoglobin reduced but at least 100 g/L · ⚠️ **severe — haemoglobin under 100 g/L and/or haemodynamic instability.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Vaginal examination is rarely indicated in an adolescent**; discuss with a senior clinician, and if needed perform it only once. [S1]

## Investigate

- **Consider FBE, ferritin, coagulation screen, TSH and βhCG (with consent)**; group and hold if bleeding is severe. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Suspected bleeding disorder: consider PFA-100 and a von Willebrand screen — not during acute bleeding or after recent NSAID use.** [S1]
- **Pelvic ultrasound is rarely required** — only for atypical features or a palpable mass. [S1]

## Acute management by severity

- **Mild:** if contraception is not wanted, reassure and observe; consider regular NSAIDs and tranexamic acid during menses. **Moderate:** tranexamic acid during menses plus oral progestogen-only or combined oral contraceptive pill. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Severe:** consider pregnancy or a bleeding disorder; IV access, resuscitation, tranexamic acid, hormonal therapy (e.g. high-dose norethisterone); iron infusion, or packed red cells if haemodynamically unstable despite fluids; **gynaecology review and consider inpatient observation.** [S1]
- **Consider iron supplementation** and a menstrual calendar or app. [S1]

## Non-hormonal medicines

- **Tranexamic acid 15–25 mg/kg (max 1 g) 2–3 times a day on heavy days** — cuts flow by 25–50%. [S1]
- **NSAIDs taken regularly during menstruation** (e.g. mefenamic acid 500 mg tds, naproxen 500 mg bd) reduce flow and can be combined with tranexamic acid. [S1]

## Progestogens

- **Active bleeding:** norethisterone 5–10 mg three times daily for 10 days, then wean slowly (e.g. by 5 mg/week) **or** medroxyprogesterone acetate 10–20 mg tds for 10 days, then stop or continue 10 mg daily. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Stopping progestogen-only treatment abruptly will precipitate bleeding.** Seek specialist advice for higher doses or non-response. [S1]
- **Prophylaxis:** norethisterone 5–10 mg or medroxyprogesterone acetate 10 mg daily continuously · depot medroxyprogesterone acetate 150 mg IM every 12 weeks · Mirena LNG-IUD 5–8-yearly. [S1]

## Combined oral contraceptive pill

- **The COC can cut flow by 50%**; usually start low-dose oestrogen with moderate progestin (e.g. ethinylestradiol 30 microg + levonorgestrel 150 microg). Check contraindications. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Active bleeding:** one pill every eight hours until bleeding stops (usually within 48 hours), then one pill every 12 hours for 3 days, then one daily continuously until outpatient review. [S1]
- **Prophylaxis:** one pill daily; move to continuous use after the first month's withdrawal bleed. [S1]

## Follow-up, escalation and discharge

- **GP or paediatrician review in 2 weeks** to confirm active bleeding has resolved; after starting maintenance, **review in 3–4 months.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Involve gynaecology for:** severe bleeding · haemodynamic instability · symptomatic anaemia · haemoglobin under 100 g/L · no response to tranexamic acid and/or hormonal treatment. [S1]
- **Discharge when stable with review booked:** within a month for mild bleeding, **within 48 hours for ongoing heavy bleeding.** [S1]
