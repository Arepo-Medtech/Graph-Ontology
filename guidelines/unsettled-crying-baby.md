# Unsettled or crying babies

## What is normal

- **Crying is a normal part of infant communication.** Assessment aims to **exclude medical conditions, inflicted injury or parental mental health concerns.** [S1]
- **Crying peaks at 6–8 weeks, averaging 2–3 hours a day**, and improves by 3–4 months. It is often worse in the late afternoon or evening, may last hours and can resist soothing. **Night waking is normal.** [S1]
- **Avoid the term 'infant colic'** — it inaccurately implies an abdominal cause. [S1]
- **Straining, crying, grunting and going red before passing soft stools (infant dyschezia) is common and self-resolving.** [S1]

## Red flags and causes to exclude

- ⚠️ **Red flags:** sudden onset irritability and persistent crying · an acutely unwell infant · faltering growth, feeding difficulties or oral aversion · rapid head circumference growth crossing percentiles · unexplained injuries. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Excessive crying is a risk factor for abusive head trauma**, and the peak crying period is strongly linked to increased risk. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Screen parents for postnatal depression with the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale.** Concern if the score is over 12 (mothers), over 9 (fathers), or any score over 1 on question 10 (suicidal thoughts). Social isolation is also a red flag. [S1]
- **Sudden onset crying — consider:** incarcerated inguinal hernia · intussusception · raised intracranial pressure · accidental or inflicted injury · infection · hair tourniquet · corneal foreign body or abrasion. [S1]
- **Also consider** inadequate caloric intake and non-IgE mediated food allergy. **Reflux disease is not a common cause of unexplained crying in otherwise well infants**, and there is no evidence for 'silent reflux'. [S1]
- **Functional lactose overload** (frothy green stools, perianal excoriation, excess gas) may occur with non-IgE food allergy or excessive maternal breast milk supply. [S1]

## Management

- **No investigations are needed** if history and examination fit a typical infant crying pattern. [S1]
- **Once red flags and medical causes are excluded, reassurance and education are essential.** Share resources on normal crying (e.g. PURPLE crying). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Arrange urgent mental health assessment** if postnatal depression screening raises significant concern or there are safety concerns for parent or infant. [S1]
- **Responding sensitively to cues and crying** fosters secure attachment, improves parental wellbeing and sleep, and reduces crying. [S1]
- **Safe sleep:** baby on their back, on a firm, flat, level surface, head and face uncovered, in a clear space free of objects. [S1]
- **Babywearing — TICKS:** tight; in view; close enough to kiss; keep chin off the chest (a finger-width gap); supported back. [S1]

## Treatments — mostly avoid

- ⚠️ **Avoid medications** — limited evidence of benefit and potential for harm. Treatments families seek are often not indicated, lack evidence and can cause harm. [S1]
- **Feeding:** flexible on-demand feeding helps. **Maternal diet or formula changes rarely help** unless non-IgE food allergy is suspected; introducing formula or solids does not improve sleep or crying; bottle and teat changes are usually ineffective. [S1]
- **Probiotic (emerging evidence), exclusively breastfed babies under 3 months only:** *Lactobacillus reuteri* DSM 17938, **5 drops/day orally for 21 days** — the only strain shown effective. [S1]
- **Acid suppression (PPIs, H2 receptor antagonists) is not effective** for physiological reflux, irritability or unsettled behaviour. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do not use:** simethicone or herbal 'colic' mixtures such as gripe water (not effective, possibly harmful); **anticholinergics such as dicyclomine** (drowsiness, apnoea, seizures — belladonna toxicity); spinal manipulation (not effective, risk of significant harm). [S1]
- **Tongue tie:** most infants with a short frenulum need no intervention. If feeding is ineffective, get a trained assessment (lactation consultant, midwife, speech pathologist); first-line is improving positioning, latch and feeding frequency. [S1]

## Escalation and discharge

- **Consult the paediatric team** if a medical cause is identified or suspected, the infant is clinically unwell, or there is concern about inflicted injury or significant parental mental health. [S1]
- **Consider transfer** when infant **or parent** needs care beyond the hospital's comfort level (including inpatient mental health support). [S1]
- **Discharge when** medical conditions, inflicted injury and parental mental health concerns are excluded or addressed, and the family has reassurance, education, resources and referral for early ongoing support. [S1]
- **Ongoing support** can include an early parenting centre for sleep and settling, or a parent-baby unit when close professional or inpatient mental health support is needed. [S1]
