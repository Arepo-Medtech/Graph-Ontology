# Dementia

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-21 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults, primary care and aged care.

> **Australian primary source.** Guideline Adaptation Committee. *Clinical Practice Guidelines and Principles
> of Care for People with Dementia.* Sydney: Guideline Adaptation Committee; **published February 2016** [S1].
> NHMRC Partnership Centre for Dealing with Cognitive and Related Functional Decline in Older People.
> **109 recommendations.** An adaptation of NICE *Dementia: Supporting people with dementia and their carers
> in health and social care* (NCC-MH, 2006) [S1].

## ⚠️ The NHMRC approval has expired

> **The guidelines (recommendations) ... were approved by the Chief Executive Officer of the National Health
> and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) on 1 February 2016 under section 14A of the *National Health and
> Medical Research Council Act 1992*. ... This approval is valid for a period of five years** [S1].

**Five years from 1 February 2016 expired on 1 February 2021.** This is the strongest currency finding in
thirty-three guidelines — not a body warning that an update is due, but a **statutory approval with a stated
expiry that has passed.** A replacement is in public consultation.

Recorded prominently rather than quietly carried, because the document remains in wide circulation. **Confirm
the current approved guideline before clinical use.**

## What kind of evidence this is

The 109 recommendations are graded in three tiers [S1]:

- **EBR** — evidence-based recommendation, carrying a quality rating
- **CBR** — consensus-based recommendation
- **PP** — practice point

**Most of the 109 are PP or CBR.** Of the evidence-based recommendations, **the great majority are graded
Low or Very low.** The handful graded **Moderate** are concentrated in a revealing place:

| Moderate-quality EBR | Subject [S1] |
|---|---|
| 70 | **adverse reactions** to acetylcholinesterase inhibitors |
| 71 | memantine as an option where AChEIs are contraindicated or not tolerated |
| 76 | Souvenaid® — **insufficient evidence** |
| 86 | SSRI for agitation |
| 88 | **uncertainty** of antidepressants for depression in dementia |
| 89 | **antipsychotics should not usually be prescribed** — increased cerebrovascular events and death |
| 91 | conditions attached to antipsychotic use in severe symptoms |

**The best evidence in dementia care is about harm, not benefit.** Four of the seven Moderate ratings are
warnings, negatives or statements of uncertainty. That pattern only appears when the whole recommendation set
is read at once.

## ⚠️ Do not screen — but do not dismiss

> **General population screening for dementia should not be undertaken** *(22, CBR)* [S1].

> **Concerns or symptoms should be explored when first raised, noted or reported by the person, carer(s) or
> family and should not be dismissed as 'part of ageing'** *(23, PP)* [S1].

> **Medical practitioners working with older people should be alert to cognitive decline, especially in those
> aged 75 years and older** *(24, CBR)* [S1].

No programmatic screening; high clinical alertness; and an explicit instruction against the most common
dismissal in the consulting room.

**People with a possible diagnosis of dementia should be offered referral to memory assessment specialists or
services for a comprehensive assessment** *(25, EBR Low)* [S1].

**Mild cognitive impairment identified by a memory service should typically be followed up at six to 18
months** — at the service, or with a GP, other medical practitioner or nurse practitioner — **to monitor
cognitive changes and other signs of possible dementia** *(27, CBR)* [S1].

## Diagnosis

> **A diagnosis of dementia should be made only after a comprehensive assessment** *(28, PP)* [S1], including:
> history from the person; **history from a person who knows the person well, if possible**; **cognitive and
> mental state examination with a validated instrument**; physical examination; **a review of medication to
> identify and minimise use of medications, including over-the-counter products, that may adversely affect
> cognitive functioning and to simplify medication dosing**; and **consideration of other causes, including
> delirium or depression**.

**A basic dementia screen should be performed at presentation, usually within primary care** *(30, PP)* [S1]:
**routine haematology; biochemistry including electrolytes, calcium, glucose, renal and liver function;
thyroid function tests; serum vitamin B12 and folate**.

**Syphilis serology or HIV testing only in those with histories suggesting they are at risk** *(31, PP)* [S1].

**Chest X-ray or ECG as clinical presentation determines — and an ECG should be considered if intending to
prescribe acetylcholinesterase inhibitors** *(32, PP)* [S1].

**Assess for medical comorbidities and key psychiatric features, including depression and psychosis, at
diagnosis and at regular intervals** *(29, PP)* [S1].

### What not to do

- **Cerebrospinal fluid examination should not be performed as a routine investigation.** It may be indicated
  **if Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease is suspected, or in rapidly progressive dementia** *(33, PP)* [S1].
- **Electroencephalography should not be used routinely.** Consider it if **delirium or CJD** is suspected, or
  in assessing **associated seizure disorder** *(35, PP)* [S1].
- **Brain biopsy only in highly selected people whose dementia is thought to be due to a potentially
  reversible condition that cannot be diagnosed any other way** *(36, PP)* [S1].
- **HMPAO SPECT should not be used in mild cognitive impairment**, either to differentiate dementia from MCI
  or progressive from non-progressive MCI *(44, EBR Very low)* [S1].

### ⚠️ Biomarkers — the most dated recommendation here

> **Many diagnostic technologies including biomarkers for β-amyloid or neuronal injury (e.g.,
> 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose Positron Emission Tomography [FDG-PET] or CSF tau) are currently being evaluated and
> may prove to be useful in the assessment of dementia in the future. The routine use of these technologies in
> clinical practice is considered to be premature** *(37, PP)* [S1].

Written in 2016. This is the single area of dementia practice that has moved furthest since, and it is flagged
`time_sensitive` accordingly. **The statement is transcribed as the source has it and is not updated from
model memory.**

**Structural imaging (CT or MRI) should usually be used** to exclude other cerebral pathologies and help
establish subtype, **unless clinical judgement indicates this inappropriate**; it **may not always be needed
in moderate-to-severe dementia if the diagnosis is already clear** *(43, PP)* [S1].

**Subtype diagnosis should be made by healthcare professionals with expertise in differential diagnosis using
international standardised criteria** *(34, PP)* [S1].

## Cognitive assessment — the Australian instruments

| Tool | Recommendation [S1] |
|---|---|
| **KICA-Cog or KICA-Screen** | **Recommended for remote-living Indigenous Australians** for whom alternative tools are not considered appropriate *(39, EBR Low — very low for KICA-Screen)* |
| **Modified KICA (mKICA)** | **Recommended as an alternative to the MMSE in urban and rural Indigenous Australian populations** when illiteracy, language or cultural considerations deem it appropriate *(40, EBR Low)* |
| **RUDAS** (Rowland Universal Dementia Assessment Scale) | **Should be considered for assessing cognition in CALD populations** *(41, EBR Very low)* |

These are the guideline's most distinctly Australian recommendations: a cognitive instrument developed for
remote Aboriginal communities, and one designed to be culture- and language-fair.

**Take full account of factors known to affect performance** — age, educational level, non-English speaking
background, prior level of functioning, aphasia, hearing or visual impairments, psychiatric illness,
physical/neurological problems — **when interpreting scores** *(38, PP)* [S1].

**Formal neuropsychological testing may form part of the assessment where the diagnosis is uncertain**
*(42, PP)* [S1].

## Communicating the diagnosis

**The diagnosis should be communicated to the person with dementia by a medical practitioner** *(45, PP)*,
**honestly and respectfully, using a gradual and individualised approach** *(46, PP)* [S1].

> **The medical practitioner should recognise that people have the right to know their diagnosis and the right
> not to know their diagnosis. In rare cases where the person with dementia indicates that they do not wish to
> be told his or her diagnosis, this wish should be respected** *(47, PP)* [S1].

The consequences must still be managed — **driving** is the example given — and **conflicts, such as when
carers request the diagnosis not be communicated, should be resolved by further discussions over time** [S1].

> **Emphasise that progression is often slow, symptomatic treatments are available and that research is
> striving to find cures, though so far without success** *(48, PP)* [S1].

An unusually unvarnished sentence for a patient-facing message, and the guideline chose it deliberately.

> **People with a history of depression and/or self-harm may be at particular risk of depression, self-harm or
> suicide following a diagnosis of dementia, particularly in the first few months post diagnosis. While such
> reactions are believed to be uncommon, counselling should be offered** *(49, PP)* [S1].

## Drug treatment

| Recommendation [S1] | Grade |
|---|---|
| **Any one of the three acetylcholinesterase inhibitors — donepezil, galantamine or rivastigmine — is recommended for mild to moderately severe Alzheimer's disease.** Any one **could be considered** for **severe** Alzheimer's disease | *69, EBR Low* |
| **Memantine** is recommended for **moderate-to-severe Alzheimer's disease in people intolerant of, or with a contraindication to, AChEIs.** **In severe renal impairment (creatinine clearance < 30 mL/min) the dose should be halved** | *71, EBR Moderate* |
| Any AChEI **could be considered** for **Dementia with Lewy Bodies, Parkinson's Disease dementia, vascular dementia or mixed dementia** | *72, EBR Low* |
| **AChEI plus memantine combination could be considered** for moderate-to-severe Alzheimer's disease | *73, EBR Low* |
| **Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors should not be prescribed for people with mild cognitive impairment** | *75, EBR Low* |

**Before starting an AChEI:** consider **performing an ECG, recording weight and undertaking a falls risk
assessment**. **Concomitant administration of medications with anticholinergic effects should be avoided**
*(69, PP element)* [S1].

**Adverse reactions** *(70, EBR Moderate)* — *"a number of adverse reactions that have a risk of harm... nausea,
vomiting, diarrhoea, dizziness, increased urinary incontinence and frequency, falls, muscle cramps, weight
loss, anorexia, headache and insomnia. **Heart block is a rare, but serious potential adverse event**"* [S1].

> **Review within a short time (e.g. one month) for adverse effects and dose titration, and within six months
> to determine whether there is a clinically meaningful response. Review and consideration of de-prescribing
> is recommended at regular intervals, including at the time of admission to residential care** *(74, PP)* [S1].

### ⚠️ Four PBS facts attached to those recommendations

The guideline footnotes the access gradient directly [S1]:

| Recommendation | PBS status as footnoted |
|---|---|
| AChEIs for **severe** Alzheimer's disease | **"Not currently listed for severe Alzheimer's disease on the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme"** |
| AChEIs for **DLB, PDD, vascular or mixed dementia** | **"Not currently listed for these indications"** |
| **AChEI + memantine combination** | **"Listing on the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme is for single therapy"** |
| Antipsychotics for BPSD | **"The only antipsychotic that is currently listed for BPSD on the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme is risperidone"** |

Four of the guideline's own drug recommendations are for uses the PBS does not subsidise. This is the same
access-versus-evidence gradient this compendium has now found in migraine, insomnia, GAD, type 2 diabetes,
heart failure and chronic kidney disease.

### A marketed product, named

> **There is currently insufficient evidence to recommend the routine use of Souvenaid® in people with mild
> Alzheimer's disease. Souvenaid® should not be recommended for people with moderate or severe Alzheimer's
> disease** *(76, EBR Moderate)* [S1].

## Behavioural and psychological symptoms (BPSD)

> **People with dementia who develop behavioural and psychological symptoms should usually be treated using
> non-pharmacological approaches in the first instance. Pharmacological intervention should usually only be
> offered first if the person, their carer(s) or family is severely distressed, pain is the suspected cause, or
> there is an immediate risk of harm... If pharmacological management is used, this should complement, not
> replace, non-pharmacological approaches** *(79, PP)* [S1].

**Look for the cause first** *(77, PP)* [S1] — **unmet needs** (pain, hunger, need to eliminate, lack of
privacy, lack of meaningful activities, communication) and **lowered stress threshold** (conflicts or poor
communication within the family or between staff, carer stress).

**Comprehensive assessment** *(78, PP)* includes an **ABC analysis** (antecedent, behaviour, consequence),
frequency, timing and presentation; physical and mental health; **level of pain or discomfort**; **medication
side effects**; religious, spiritual and cultural influences; environmental and interpersonal factors;
**assessment of the carer's health and communication style**; and **understanding the behaviour as a form of
communication** [S1].

> **If a person with dementia is suspected to be in pain due to their distress or behaviour, as indicated by
> responses on an observational pain assessment tool, analgesic medication should be trialled using a stepped
> approach. The trial should be for a defined time period, particularly if opioids are used** *(81, EBR Low)* [S1].

**Non-drug options** *(84, EBR Very low to Low)* [S1] — multicomponent interventions involving enjoyable
activities plus individualised support where available; otherwise, **for depression and/or anxiety**:
therapeutic use of music and/or dancing, support and counselling, reminiscence therapy. **For agitation**:
behavioural management interventions, therapeutic use of music and/or dancing, **massage**, reminiscence
therapy.

**Carer-directed interventions** *(85, EBR Low)*: **carer skills training** in managing symptoms and
communicating effectively, **meaningful activity planning**, **environmental redesign and modification** to
improve safety and enjoyment, **problem solving and management planning** [S1].

### Psychotropics in BPSD

> **People with dementia who experience agitation should be offered a trial of SSRI antidepressants (the
> strongest evidence for effectiveness exists for citalopram) if non-pharmacological treatments are
> inappropriate or have failed. Review with evaluation of efficacy and consideration of de-prescribing should
> occur after two months** *(86, EBR Moderate)* [S1].

> **The role of antidepressants in the treatment of depression in people with dementia is uncertain. Larger
> trials conducted in people with dementia have not shown benefit (in group data) for antidepressants for
> treatment of depression per se. Nevertheless, those with a pre-existing history of major depression (prior to
> developing dementia) who develop a co-morbid major depression should be treated in the usual way**
> *(88, EBR Moderate)* [S1].

An SSRI has Moderate evidence **for agitation** and uncertain evidence **for depression** — in the same
population, at the same grade. That distinction is easily lost.

**Avoid antidepressants with anticholinergic effects, e.g. tricyclics, because they may adversely affect
cognition** *(87, PP)* [S1].

### ⚠️ Antipsychotics

> **People with Alzheimer's disease, vascular dementia or mixed dementias with mild-to-moderate behavioural and
> psychological symptoms of dementia should not usually be prescribed antipsychotic medications because of the
> increased risk of cerebrovascular adverse events and death** *(89, EBR Moderate)* [S1].

> **As far as possible, antipsychotics should be avoided in people with Dementia with Lewy Bodies due to the
> risk of severe untoward reactions, particularly extrapyramidal side effects. Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors
> could be considered.** If used, **atypical or second generation antipsychotics with low propensity to cause
> extrapyramidal side effects should be used; quetiapine and olanzapine are considered to have the best
> tolerability. Use low dosage and closely monitor** *(90, PP)* [S1].

**For severe BPSD — psychosis and/or agitation/aggression causing significant distress** — an antipsychotic
**may** be offered *(91, EBR Moderate)*. **Risperidone has the strongest evidence for treating psychosis.
Risperidone and olanzapine have the strongest evidence for treating agitation/aggression, with weaker evidence
for aripiprazole** [S1]. Conditions that **should** be met [S1]:

- **Full discussion with the person and their carers and family about possible benefits and risks.** In
  particular, **cerebrovascular risk factors should be assessed and the possible increased risk of
  stroke/TIA and possible adverse effects on cognition discussed.**
- **Target symptoms identified, quantified and documented.**
- **Effect of comorbid conditions, such as depression, considered.**
- **Choice made after an individual risk–benefit analysis.**
- **Dose initially low, titrated upwards if necessary.**
- **Monitoring for adverse effects including the metabolic syndrome.**
- > **If there is no efficacy observed within a relatively short timeframe (usually one to two weeks),
  > treatment should be discontinued.**
- **Review every four to 12 weeks**, considering the need for antipsychotics and **possible cessation**, with
  **regular assessment and recording of changes in cognition and target symptoms.**

A one-to-two-week stopping rule with a mandated review cycle — a **deprescribing schedule written into the
prescribing recommendation itself.**

**Refer to a specialist service where moderate to severe symptoms put the person or others at risk**
*(92, PP)* [S1].

### Emergency sedation

**Oral medication before parenteral** *(94, PP)*. **If parenteral is necessary, intramuscular is preferable
because it is safer than intravenous; intravenous only in exceptional circumstances** *(96, PP)*. **Olanzapine
or lorazepam are preferred; wherever possible a single agent rather than a combination** *(97, CBR)* [S1].

> **Health professionals should be aware that loss of consciousness can be mistaken for sleep. If the person
> appears to be or is asleep, more intensive monitoring is required because of the risk of loss of
> consciousness** *(96, PP)* [S1].

Staff using these medications must be **trained in their correct use for behavioural control**, able to assess
risks **particularly in people who may be dehydrated or physically ill**, understand cardiorespiratory effects
and dose titration, know the **recovery position** and vital sign monitoring, be **trained in resuscitation
with annual retraining**, understand airway maintenance, and **know the laws for informed consent in their
jurisdiction** *(93, PP)* [S1].

> **People with dementia who have received involuntary sedation should be offered the opportunity, along with
> their carer(s) and family, to discuss their experiences and be provided with a clear explanation of the
> decision to use urgent sedation. This should be documented in their notes** *(98, PP)* [S1].

## Living well, and function

**People with dementia should be strongly encouraged to exercise. Assessment and advice from a physiotherapist
or exercise physiologist may be indicated** *(68, EBR Low)* [S1].

**Occupational therapy for people living in the community** *(67, EBR Low)* — **environmental assessment and
modification**, **prescription of assistive technology**, and **tailored intervention to promote independence
in activities of daily living**, which may involve **problem solving, task simplification, and education and
skills training for carers and family** [S1].

**Promote functional and social independence** *(66, PP)* through **consistency of care staff**, **stability in
living environment**, **flexibility to accommodate fluctuating abilities**, **tailored activities that are
meaningful and enjoyable**, and **support to retain continence** [S1].

**Monitor weight and assess nutritional status regularly**; consider a dietitian and/or speech pathologist in
undernutrition *(64, PP)* [S1]. **Upon diagnosis, recommend an appointment with a dentist** for assessment and
a long-term treatment plan *(65, PP)* [S1].

## Carers

> **Carers are at an increased risk of poor health and their needs should be assessed and reviewed regularly by
> their own health practitioner. Carer and family needs should be addressed regularly, including if the person
> with dementia has entered residential care, and after their death** *(100, PP)* [S1].

That last clause — **after their death** — is the one most often missed.

**Respite appropriate to their needs** should be offered: **in-home, day respite, planned activity groups and
residential respite** *(101, CBR)* [S1].

**Carer support programs** *(102, EBR Low)*: tailored to the individual, **delivered in the home or another
accessible location**, **over multiple sessions**, covering education about dementia and its consequences,
services including respite, referral to support organisations, **individualised strategies and skill building**,
training in care and communication, **coping strategies to maintain their own wellbeing including stress
management**, and **training in the use of pleasant and meaningful activities** [S1].

**Information about how to join a mutual support group**, with groups of the preferred composition available
*(104, EBR Low)* [S1]. **Psychological therapy by a specialist practitioner where necessary** *(105, PP)* [S1].

## Palliative approach

**Care for people with advanced dementia should be based on a palliative approach and involve a palliative care
service if indicated, as per the person's Advance Care Plans** *(106, PP)* [S1].

> **Continue to offer people with dementia food and drink by mouth... Nutritional support, including artificial
> (tube) feeding, should be considered if dysphagia is thought to be a transient phenomenon, but artificial
> feeding should not generally be used in people with severe dementia for whom dysphagia or disinclination to
> eat is a manifestation of disease severity** *(107, PP)* [S1].

**If a person with severe dementia has a fever, undertake clinical assessment. Simple analgesics, antipyretics
and mechanical means of cooling may suffice. Antibiotics may be considered as a palliative measure in the
terminal stages, but this needs an individual assessment** *(108, PP)* [S1].

**In the absence of a valid and applicable advance directive to refuse resuscitation**, the decision should take
account of the person's expressed wishes or beliefs, the views of carers, family and the multidisciplinary
team, **guidance from the Australian Resuscitation Council**, and **state or territory mental health and
guardianship laws** where capacity is lacking. **Advance Care Plans must be recorded in the medical notes and
care plans** *(109, PP)* [S1].

## Legal, ethical and equity provisions

**Valid informed consent should always be sought** for decisions on financial affairs, health care and living
arrangements; where capacity is lacking, **the relevant state and territory substitute decision-making laws
must be followed** *(4, PP)* [S1].

**Discuss, while the person still has capacity: Enduring Power of Attorney and enduring guardianship, and
Advance Care Plans** — revisited regularly and **following any significant change in health condition or
circumstance**, and **completed or updated at the time of Aged Care Assessment Team assessment** *(6, PP)* [S1].

> **People with dementia should not be excluded from any health care services because of their diagnosis,
> whatever their age** *(8, PP)* [S1].

**Where language or culture is a barrier**, provide **information in the preferred language and in an
accessible format, professional interpreters, and interventions in the preferred language** *(9, CBR)* [S1].

**People with younger onset dementia have unique needs; services should be age appropriate** *(13, PP)* [S1].

**For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people** *(14–18, PP)* [S1]: consult Indigenous community
representatives and the local Indigenous medical service in developing, implementing and reviewing any
initiative; **employ Indigenous staff members at all levels**; consult family and community representatives in
developing a culturally appropriate care plan, with a case manager who may be an Indigenous community-based
staff member; and **display sensitivity at the transition to residential care**, which is **"a particularly
difficult step for the person living with dementia, their family and community"**.

**For CALD populations** *(19–21, PP)* [S1]: ongoing community consultation with feedback back to community;
**bilingual bicultural staff or professional interpreters across the whole service pathway, particularly during
assessment, when communicating the diagnosis and gaining consent**; and support, education and information for
carers **delivered by bilingual, bicultural workers**.

**People with dementia are vulnerable to abuse and neglect**; all staff supporting them **should receive
information and training about how to prevent and manage suspected abuse** *(61, PP)* [S1].
