# Major depressive disorder

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-21 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults with unipolar major depressive disorder. Excludes bipolar, psychotic and
treatment-resistant depression, which the principal evidence source excludes [S1].

> Every clinical sentence carries a source tag. Claims no retrieved source supports are listed under
> *Unresolved*. **Australian coverage is partial**: the RANZCP mood disorders guideline is flagged by RANZCP
> itself as more than five years old and no longer current guidance. Sources here are international.

## Diagnosis and evaluation

Major depression is defined by depressed mood, loss of interest in activities, and associated psychological
and somatic symptoms **lasting at least 2 weeks** [S2].

Evaluation should include structured assessment of **severity**, and of **risk of self-harm, suspected
bipolar disorder, psychotic symptoms, substance use, and co-occurring anxiety disorder** [S2]. Each of those
five changes management if present, which is why they are assessed rather than assumed absent.

## Treatment aims

Symptom remission and restored function. Both psychotherapy and antidepressants are first-line, and the
comparative evidence for each is quantified below rather than asserted.

## Psychological treatment

First line [S2]. A network meta-analysis found **cognitive therapy, behavioural activation, problem-solving
therapy, interpersonal therapy, brief psychodynamic therapy and mindfulness-based psychotherapy** all had at
least medium-sized effects on symptom improvement over usual care without psychotherapy, with standardised
mean differences ranging from **0.50 (95% CI 0.20–0.81) to 0.73 (95% CI 0.52–0.95)** [S2].

Six named therapies, all effective, none singled out.

## Drug treatment

First line [S2]. A network meta-analysis of **21 antidepressants** found all had small- to medium-sized
effects over placebo, with standardised mean differences from **0.23 (95% CI 0.19–0.28) for fluoxetine to
0.48 (95% CI 0.41–0.55) for amitriptyline** [S2].

Read the two ranges together. The psychotherapy effect sizes (0.50–0.73 vs usual care) and the antidepressant
effect sizes (0.23–0.48 vs placebo) are **against different comparators** and cannot be compared directly.
Anyone tempted to conclude psychotherapy is stronger from these numbers is comparing across comparators.

The underlying comparison ranked 21 antidepressants for acute treatment of adults with unipolar major
depression, from published and unpublished double-blind randomised trials, placebo-controlled and head to
head [S1].

**Combination.** Psychotherapy with antidepressant medication may be preferred, **especially for more severe
or chronic depression** [S2].

As with GAD and insomnia, the source notes antidepressants are used more frequently than psychological
interventions **because of inadequate resources** [S1] — a resourcing fact, not a clinical one.

## Australian context

PBS Schedule 4333, checked directly [S3]:

| Agent | PBS | Benefit type |
|---|---|---|
| **Amitriptyline** | listed | **unrestricted** |
| Sertraline, escitalopram, fluoxetine | listed | restricted |
| Mirtazapine, venlafaxine, desvenlafaxine | listed | restricted |
| **Vortioxetine** | **not on Schedule 4333** | — |
| **Agomelatine** | **not on Schedule 4333** | — |

Amitriptyline — the agent with the **largest** effect size in S2's range (SMD 0.48) — is the only
unrestricted option, while fluoxetine at the bottom of that range (0.23) is restricted.

That is not an argument for amitriptyline: effect size is one axis and tolerability and overdose toxicity
are others the retrieved abstracts do not quantify. It is an observation that here, unusually, the subsidy
and the efficacy ranking do not point in opposite directions.
