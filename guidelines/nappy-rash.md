# Nappy rash

## Recognise it

- **Nappy rash is an irritant contact dermatitis affecting more than half of infants by twelve months.** [S1]
- **Typical:** widespread erythema on convex skin in direct contact with the nappy, **usually sparing the groin folds**, with scaling, swelling, scattered papules and ulcers from broken skin. [S1]
- **Contributors:** urine and faecal enzymes overhydrating and macerating the skin (worse with infrequent changes, cloth nappies and plastic pants), friction, *Candida*, irritants in wipes, powders, creams and moisturisers, eczema, and recent antibiotics. [S1]
- **History to take:** nappy-change frequency and type, growth, feeding and stool output, prior treatments, atopy, prematurity or bowel surgery/short gut, and **maternal vegan/vegetarian diet in the third trimester (zinc deficiency).** Examine the whole body including scalp, the mouth for thrush, and the growth trend. [S1]

## Think of something else

- **Consider other causes when the rash is severe, unusual or not responding** — more than one condition can coexist. [S1]
- ***Candida*: erythema in the skin folds with satellite spots or pustules.** [S1]
- **Perianal streptococcal cellulitis:** well-demarcated perianal erythema with fissuring and maceration; may cause itch, painful defecation or constipation. [S1]
- **Psoriasis:** sharp, non-scaly, bright red plaques. **Seborrhoeic dermatitis:** non-itchy salmon-pink patches ± greasy scale, also on scalp, face and folds. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Malabsorption** (lactose overload, cow milk protein intolerance, cystic fibrosis, IBD) can present with diarrhoea, erosive dermatitis and poor growth, driving intractable nappy rash. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Zinc deficiency (acrodermatitis enteropathica):** sharply defined, often extensive anogenital rash — look for perioral, perinasal and hand/foot dermatitis, alopecia, diarrhoea and failure to thrive. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Langerhans cell histiocytosis:** chronic, often erosive inguinal or anogenital rash with brownish-red scale and petechiae, unresponsive to treatment; purpura, fever, diarrhoea or hepatosplenomegaly may be present. [S1]
- **Generally no investigations are required.** [S1]

## Treat

- **Cleanse with warm water ± bath oil and a soft cloth at every change**; pat or air dry, no rubbing; maximise nappy-free time; daily warm bath with fragrance-free cleanser or bath oil; **avoid soaps and bubble baths.** [S1]
- **A thick layer of barrier cream at every change** — zinc, white soft paraffin or petrolatum. **Not responding to low-zinc cream → switch to a higher concentration, eg 40%.** [S1]
- **Don't strip the barrier at each change** — remove visible soiling and layer more on top. [S1]
- **Not responding to simple measures: add 1% hydrocortisone ointment 1–2 times daily for 1–2 weeks** on top of the barrier cream. [S1]
- ***Candida*:** single agent — clotrimazole 1% cream twice daily **or** miconazole 2% cream twice daily; **or** combination — miconazole 2% + hydrocortisone 1% cream twice daily, **or** miconazole 2.5 mg + zinc oxide 150 mg/g ointment as a thick layer at every change. A topical antifungal can be combined with 1% hydrocortisone. [S1]
- **Bacterial infection:** see Cellulitis and skin infections. Formulary restrictions may apply — check local guidelines. [S1]

## Prevent and follow up

- **Prevention:** change wet or soiled nappies as soon as possible, wash and rinse cloth nappies thoroughly, **no plastic pants**, maximise nappy-free time, clean with good baby wipes or warm water, **barrier cream at every change**, and **avoid powders (eg talc) and soaps.** [S1]
- **Consult the local paediatric team** if the rash isn't improving or a cause other than irritant dermatitis is suspected. **Discharge** once the family knows the plan and follow-up is in place. [S1]
