# Bacterial vaginosis

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-21 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults, primary care.

> **Australian primary source.** ASHM *Australian STI Management Guidelines for use in primary care* —
> *Bacterial vaginosis* [S1], modified **29 June 2026** — the most recently revised page in this set.

## ⚠️ This topic has just changed

Of twenty-five guidelines, this is the only one built on a **trial that changed the recommendation**.

> **A recent randomised controlled trial, published in the New England Journal of Medicine, has shown that
> treating an ongoing/regular sexual partner with a penis with a combination of oral and topical
> antibiotics, at the same time a person with a vagina is receiving first-line antibiotic therapy for BV,
> improves BV cure over 12 weeks. This trial provides evidence that reinfection is a very significant driver
> of BV recurrence, and that BV is sexually transmissible** [S1].

Two consequences, both stated by the source:

1. **Partner treatment is now recommended**, not merely considered.
2. **BV is described as sexually transmissible** — a reclassification of the condition itself.

The trial's intervention was **7 days of oral metronidazole twice daily plus 2% clindamycin cream applied
to the penile skin twice daily**, and it was **effective in people with vaginal BV with and without IUDs,
and with circumcised and uncircumcised partners** [S1].

This is the mirror image of the tecovirimat entry in the mpox guideline: there, two trials removed a
treatment's justification; here, one trial created one. Both are recorded because a static library that
merely lists drugs would show neither.

## Why the recurrence figures matter

| Figure [S1] | |
|---|---|
| BV prevalence, people with a vagina of reproductive age, globally | **one in four** |
| Post-treatment recurrence **with an ongoing/regular partner, without partner treatment** | **60–80%** |
| Post-treatment recurrence within 3–12 months, **prior to the partner-treatment evidence** | **more than 50%** |

A condition that recurs in more than half of those treated was not a treatment-failure problem. The source
now attributes a very significant share of it to **reinfection**.

## What BV is

> **A polymicrobial vaginal dysbiosis characterised by a change from a *Lactobacillus*-dominant state to one
> with high diversity and quantities of anaerobic bacteria including *Gardnerella vaginalis*,
> *Fannyhessea vaginae*, *Mobiluncus* spp., *Prevotella* spp., and other BV-associated bacteria** [S1].

**Studies have identified a polymicrobial biofilm adherent to vaginal epithelial cells** [S1].

**BV has the epidemiological profile of a sexually transmissible infection** — strong association with young
age of first sex, increased numbers of sex partners, exposure to new sex partners, and lack of condom use
for penile-vaginal sex [S1]. **BV-associated bacteria are present in the urethra and penile skin of
partners**, with **high concordance in couples where one partner has a penis and the other a vagina**, and
**very high concordance in couples with vaginas** [S1].

## Diagnosis — Amsel criteria

**If 3 or 4 of the following are present, presumptive treatment can be offered** [S1]:

1. **Thin white/grey homogenous discharge** on speculum examination
2. **Elevated vaginal pH (> 4.5)**
3. **Whiff test** — malodour with addition of potassium hydroxide to vaginal secretions, or if not
   available, **genital malodour on examination**
4. **Clue cells** on microscopy of Gram stain of high vaginal swab

**Clinician collection ensures visualisation of secretions and measurement of vaginal pH; microscopy can be
performed on self-collected pH strips or clinician-collected swabs smeared on a slide** [S1].

> **Isolation of *Gardnerella vaginalis* by NAAT is reported by some laboratories but cannot be used to
> diagnose bacterial vaginosis, as this organism can also be isolated in people with an optimal vaginal
> microbiota and no bacterial vaginosis** [S1].

A molecular result that looks diagnostic and is not. **If your laboratory uses NAAT testing, speak to your
pathology provider about its comparative performance** [S1]. **Gram stain scoring (Nugent score, Ison-Hay
method) is increasingly only used in specialised services** [S1].

**Clinical indication for testing: symptoms — abnormal vaginal discharge and/or malodour** [S1].

## Treatment of the index patient

| Situation | Recommended [S1] | Alternative [S1] |
|---|---|---|
| **Symptomatic BV** | **Metronidazole 400 mg PO BD with food for 7 days**<br>**OR** **metronidazole 0.75% gel 5 g intravaginally nocte for 5 nights** *(not on PBS)*<br>**OR** **clindamycin 2% vaginal cream 5 g, one applicator intravaginally nocte for 7 days** *(not on PBS)*<br>**For people with an ongoing or regular partner with a penis, concurrent partner treatment is recommended** | **Clindamycin 300 mg PO BD for 7 days**<br>**OR** **metronidazole 2 g PO stat** |

> **Stat dose and short-duration regimens are associated with higher rates of recurrence** [S1].

The convenient option is the one that fails more often, and the source says so rather than listing it
neutrally.

**Treatment is aimed at alleviating symptoms, or offered to asymptomatic people with BV requesting
treatment** [S1]. The auditable outcome is correspondingly narrow: **100% of symptomatic patients are
treated** [S1].

**During treatment: refrain from vaginal penetrative sex, or use condoms for penile-vaginal sex
consistently** [S1]. **Douching and intravaginal cleaning practices should be avoided** [S1].

**Note the PBS gap:** both intravaginal options are **not on the PBS**. The only subsidised recommended
option is oral metronidazole.

## Treatment of partners with a penis

**Oral metronidazole 400 mg twice daily for 7 days**, in combination with **topical 2% clindamycin cream
twice daily to the penile skin for 7 days** [S1] — given **at the same time** the index partner is being
treated.

> **Partner treatment is most effective in closed/monogamous relationships where all partners have been
> synchronously treated, due to the risk of introduction of BV-associated bacteria from concurrent untreated
> partners** [S1].

The application instructions are reproduced because the trial's effect depends on them [S1]:

- **Squeeze a line of cream from the fingertip of the index finger to the first crease** (a fingertip unit).
- **If they have foreskin, retract it before applying.** Rub the cream gently over the skin of the head of
  the penis, **covering the whole head from the tip to the groove just below the head**.
- **Squeeze a second fingertip unit** and rub it **down the shaft, starting at the groove under the head and
  moving to the base**, covering **front and back**, rubbing all the cream gently into the skin. **They may
  not need the full two fingertips of cream.**
- **Repeat twice daily for 7 days.**
- **Apply the cream immediately after showering, not before.**
- **100% adherence and avoidance of unprotected sex during the treatment period.**
- **Clindamycin cream may potentially weaken latex if the cream has just been applied.**

That last point and the intravaginal-preparation note below are a pair worth noticing: the source recommends
condoms during treatment **and** flags that two of the treatments can compromise them.

**Partner treatment resources** — clinician information, a decision-making tool, consumer information,
medication instructions and a pharmacy letter — are published by Melbourne Sexual Health Centre and linked
from the source [S1].

## Partners with a vagina

> **No trials of partner treatment for couples with vaginas have been conducted to inform clinical practice.
> However, high concordance for BV is consistently reported within couples with vaginas. Testing of partners
> with vaginas should be offered in order to detect and treat BV in the partner, which is likely to reduce
> reinfection of the index and BV recurrence. There are no published trials to determine whether empiric
> treatment without testing for BV in ongoing partners with vaginas improves BV cure** [S1].

The source distinguishes cleanly between what has been trialled and what has been reasoned — and lands on
**test, then treat** rather than empiric treatment, precisely because the trial does not exist.

## Contact tracing

- **Contact tracing is not indicated** [S1].
- **There are insufficient data to recommend treatment of casual/non-regular partners with a penis** [S1].
- **Assessment of partner(s) with a vagina is recommended, as concordance is high** [S1].

Note the distinction the source is drawing: **partner treatment is not contact tracing.** One is part of
curing the index patient; the other is finding cases. BV now has the first and explicitly not the second.

## IUDs, pregnancy, and what not to screen

> **Evidence does not support routine screening for bacterial vaginosis in low-risk pregnancies, before
> surgical termination of pregnancy, or insertion of an IUD in asymptomatic people** [S1].

> **Bacterial vaginosis diagnosed or suspected at the time of IUD insertion should be treated, with no need
> to delay the procedure** [S1].

For surgical abortion antibiotic prophylaxis, the source refers to eTG and local guidelines [S1].

- **Copper IUDs have been associated with increased risk of BV acquisition and recurrence.** Treat as
  recommended if BV develops; **partner treatment has been shown to improve cure for people with an IUD**;
  **if recurrent BV with a copper IUD, consider switching to an alternative method** [S1].
- **An association between hormonal IUD use and BV is unclear and more data are needed** [S1].

| Situation | Recommended [S1] |
|---|---|
| **Pregnancy** | **Standard treatment if symptomatic. No clinical benefit of treatment for asymptomatic patients in low-risk pregnancies** |
| **Breastfeeding** | **Consider intravaginal treatment.** Metronidazole may affect the taste of breast milk; avoid high doses |
| **Recurrent infection** | **Intravaginal metronidazole 0.75% gel 5 g twice per week for 4 months** reduces recurrence **during** treatment, **although this benefit does not persist when discontinued.** Intravaginal **boric acid** regimens (via compounding pharmacy) have also been used but **have not shown sustained benefit on discontinuation.** Seek specialist advice if required |
| **Allergy to the principal treatment** | **If allergic to nitroimidazoles, use clindamycin** |
| **Intravaginal preparations** | **May affect condom integrity** |

> **There is currently insufficient evidence to recommend the use of intravaginal lactic acid or vaginal
> probiotics** [S1].

A direct statement about two of the most widely sold self-treatments for this condition.

## Complications

**Increased risk of** spontaneous abortion, premature labour, chorioamnionitis, postpartum endometritis,
**pelvic inflammatory disease** (including after surgical termination of pregnancy, IUD insertion or other
gynaecological instrumentation), **acquisition of chlamydia, gonorrhoea and herpes simplex type 2**, and
**acquisition and transmission of HIV** [S1].

## Follow-up

**Unnecessary if symptoms resolve.** **Test of cure not required** [S1].

**If symptoms persist or recur, review — it is important to confirm the diagnosis. Test for STIs if not
undertaken at first presentation, as concurrent STIs are common** [S1].
