# Facial weakness and Bell palsy in children

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** assessment of acute facial weakness, red flags for causes other than Bell palsy, eye care, steroids and referral in children. > ⚠️ **Bell palsy is a diagnosis of exclusion.** Protect the eye that cannot close. **Under 2 years or atypical features: consult Neurology.**

## Recognise it

- **Bell palsy is an idiopathic, unilateral lower motor neuron facial nerve palsy. Exclude other causes before making the diagnosis.** [S1]
- **Other causes include infection, inflammation, trauma, tumour and vascular events.** Idiopathic or post-viral facial palsy is uncommon under 2 years. [S1]
- **Typical Bell palsy:** rapid onset over hours or no more than 1–2 days; usually mild pain in the face or behind the ear; dry eyes and poor tear clearance. [S1]
- **Bell palsy involves all facial nerve branches on the affected side**; forehead weakness (and ptosis, as the page puts it) may be evident, and **these are absent in upper motor neuron lesions.** [S1]

## Red flags for another cause

- ⚠️ **Other neurological features** — severe headache, blurred or double vision, limb weakness or numbness, ataxia — **consider stroke.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Also against Bell palsy:** hyperacusis and altered taste (both uncommon in it), preceding viral infection or head/face trauma, and **severe pain (mastoiditis, VZV).** [S1]

## Examine

- **A full cranial and peripheral neurological examination is essential**, plus an eye examination for corneal abrasions. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Look for:** vesicles on the face or in the ear canal (Ramsay Hunt syndrome); pallor, hepatosplenomegaly or lymphadenopathy (malignancy); otitis media, mastoiditis or parotitis; and **check blood pressure** — hypertension is a rare association — and temperature. [S1]
- **The House Brackmann scale grades severity** and is useful to monitor progress. [S1]

## Investigate

- **The diagnosis is clinical; investigations are not usually required.** [S1]
- **FBE (to exclude haematological malignancy)** if there are features of an alternative cause, particularly malignancy, **or if starting steroids.** [S1]
- **If an alternative cause is suspected:** swab for HSV and VZV if vesicles are present; neuroimaging in consultation with neurology. [S1]

## Treat

- **Almost all children recover within 12 months without treatment, most within 6 weeks** — but some have prolonged functional impairment, facial asymmetry and emotional distress. Give the parent handout. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Any child who cannot close the eye needs eye care** to prevent corneal abrasions: **hypromellose lubricating drops at least three times during the day**, and **pad the eye shut after lubricating ointment at night.** [S1]
- **Steroids: not enough evidence in children** that early prednisolone improves complete recovery; most recover untreated. [S1]
- **In adults, steroids 1 mg/kg/day (max 50 mg) orally daily for 10 days improve recovery, particularly within 72 hours of onset.** The age at which adult evidence applies is unknown. [S1]
- **Aciclovir may be considered if a vesicular rash is present.** [S1]

## Refer and discharge

- ⚠️ **Consult Neurology if under 2 years or atypical on history or examination; consult ENT** for otitis media, mastoiditis or parotitis. [S1]
- **Discharge when there are no signs of complications and GP or paediatrician follow-up within 1–2 weeks is arranged.** [S1]
