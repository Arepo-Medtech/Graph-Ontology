# Haemophilia in children: acute bleeding

## Key points and background

- ⚠️ **Assessment and investigation should not delay factor replacement. Discuss every child with haemophilia and bleeding with a haemophilia treatment centre.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Plan with a haematologist before any procedure** (e.g. lumbar puncture). **Arterial and lumbar puncture only after factor replacement. No IM injections.** [S1]
- **Haemophilia A = Factor VIII deficiency; B = Factor IX deficiency.** X-linked, affecting 1 in 6,000–10,000 males and under 1 in 300,000 females. [S1]

## Assess

- **History:** mechanism of each injury (internal and joint injuries are often missed); type of haemophilia, treatment plan and inhibitor status; home prophylaxis and time of last dose. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Major or suspected bleeding in the head, neck, chest, GI tract, abdomen or pelvis: give clotting factor immediately**, before a full assessment is complete. [S1]
- **Severity by factor level:** **severe under 1%** — frequent, often spontaneous joint and muscle bleeds · **moderate 1–5%** — bleeds after minor injury, severe bleeds with moderate trauma or surgery · **mild over 5–40%** — bleeds with major trauma such as surgery. [S1]

## Investigate

- **Do not delay factor replacement for investigations. Routine coagulation studies are not required**; imaging depends on the mechanism of injury. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Emicizumab (Hemlibra) makes standard coagulation tests inaccurate** — do not use them to monitor APTT, factor VIII or factor VIII inhibitor. [S1]
- **Consider FBE and ferritin** after recurrent mucosal bleeding — iron deficiency is common. [S1]

## Clotting factor replacement

- **Most bleeds need factor replacement**, except minor soft-tissue injuries and bruising that do not affect function or mobility. **Prompt replacement reduces pain and long-term harm.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do not delay factor replacement in severe or life-threatening bleeding. Recombinant concentrates are the product of choice.** [S1]
- **Round doses up to whole vials** — e.g. a 20 kg child needing 30 units/kg factor VIII gets 750 units. [S1]
- **In an emergency any brand of recombinant FVIII is acceptable**, though children should ideally stay on their established brand. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Ongoing bleeding despite adequate replacement → suspect a new inhibitor**, especially if previously responsive. **Proven or suspected inhibitors need a bypassing agent.** [S1]
- **Concentrates are not stocked in every hospital**; regional and rural children should have management plans and ready access to blood products. [S1]

## Supportive care

- **Joint and muscle bleeds: start P.R.I.C.E on arrival** — Protection (immobilise), Rest, Ice, gentle Compression, Elevation. [S1]
- **Venepuncture: press for at least 3 minutes afterwards**; minimise failed or distressing attempts (distraction, consider nitrous oxide). IV cannulas are generally not left in on discharge unless discussed with a specialist. [S1]
- ⚠️ **No aspirin or NSAIDs** (e.g. ibuprofen, diclofenac). Paracetamol may suffice; opiates for severe pain; splinting helps. [S1]

## Transfer and discharge

- ⚠️ **Consider transfer** for care beyond local comfort, or any of: suspected intracranial haemorrhage · neck/throat bleeding · forearm or calf bleed at risk of compartment syndrome · hip or inguinal bleeding (iliopsoas risk) · undiagnosed abdominal pain · persistent haematuria · bleeding causing severe pain. [S1]
- **Discharge when** there is no active bleeding, follow-up is arranged and the child has a management plan (e.g. Haemophilia Centre Treatment Card). [S1]
