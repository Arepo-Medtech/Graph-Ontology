## Mechanism and severity

Acne vulgaris arises from four processes that treatment is aimed at in turn: **increased sebum production,
abnormal follicular keratinisation, proliferation of *Cutibacterium* (*Propionibacterium*) *acnes*, and
inflammation** [A1].

AMH grades severity by lesion type rather than count, and ties the grade to scarring risk [A1]:

| Grade | Lesions | Scarring |
|---|---|---|
| **Mild** | mainly comedones, open or closed, with some papules and pustules | — |
| **Moderate** | more widespread papules and pustules | mild scarring may occur |
| **Severe** | nodules, pseudocysts and widespread papules and pustules | extensive scarring may occur |

⚠️ **Start treatment early to prevent scarring**, and tailor it to severity [A1]. The stated aims include
**limiting disease duration** and **reducing the psychological stress — depression and low self-esteem —
related to acne**, alongside improving complexion and preventing scarring [A1].

## ⚠️ Decide about pregnancy before choosing the drug

AMH puts this *before* the treatment sections, not in a cautions list [A1]:

| Safe to use | Contraindicated / restricted |
|---|---|
| **benzoyl peroxide** · **topical clindamycin** · **oral erythromycin** | ⚠️ **cyproterone**, **retinoids (topical and oral)** and **spironolactone** are **contraindicated** |
| **azelaic acid** — no data, but considered safe because absorption is low | ⚠️ **doxycycline** is **contraindicated after 18 weeks (16 weeks post conception)** but safe before this |

That doxycycline boundary is unusually precise and easy to misremember: it is a **gestational cut-off, not a
blanket contraindication**.

## Choice of treatment

**Topical retinoids and benzoyl peroxide are first line in mild or moderate acne**; if either is poorly
tolerated, **azelaic acid** may be used instead [A1].

- **Mild, with inflammation** — add **topical clindamycin**
- **Moderate to severe, with inflammation** — add **oral antibacterials**
- **Severe, or moderate unresponsive to the above** — **isotretinoin**
- **Females with moderate or severe acne** — **oral hormonal treatment** may be added [A1]

### Topical retinoids

**The treatment of choice for both comedonal and inflammatory acne**, usable alongside other topical and oral
therapy. ⚠️ **Irritation may increase when more than one topical is used.** **Not to be used in pregnancy
because of teratogenic risk** [A1].

### Benzoyl peroxide and azelaic acid

**Benzoyl peroxide** is antibacterial and mildly comedolytic — useful in comedonal and mild inflammatory
acne, and combinable with retinoids or with topical or oral antibacterials [A1].

**Azelaic acid** is antibacterial and comedolytic, and **a less irritating alternative to benzoyl peroxide in
mild inflammatory acne** [A1]. ⚠️ **Use cautiously in patients with dark complexions — it may cause
hypopigmentation** — though **that effect may be useful where acne has left hyperpigmented scars** [A1].

### Topical clindamycin, and the resistance problem

**Antibacterial resistance in skin flora is an increasing problem**, and **combining with benzoyl peroxide or
azelaic acid may reduce the risk of *C. acnes* resistance** [A1].

⚠️ Two limits AMH sets explicitly: **change treatment if there is no response after 6–8 weeks**, and **stop
topical clindamycin once inflammatory lesions resolve adequately — limit treatment to 3 months where
possible** [A1].

### Oral antibacterials

**Doxycycline is the drug of choice** for moderate-to-severe inflammatory acne. **Minocycline is second line
because it has more adverse effects.** **Erythromycin may be used where tetracyclines are not tolerated or are
contraindicated** [A1].

**Always combine with a topical retinoid and either benzoyl peroxide or azelaic acid**, and [A1]:

- **change the antibacterial or consider an alternative treatment if there is no response after 6–8 weeks**
- ⚠️ **limit treatment to 3–6 months when possible**
- **consider other treatment options if longer-term treatment is necessary**

> ⚠️ **Practice point** — **do not switch or rotate antibacterials in a patient who is responding**: response
> indicates efficacy, and changing agents may promote resistance [A1].

### Oral hormonal treatment

Limited to females; these agents **reduce sebum secretion, which is under androgen control**. **Several months
of treatment are usually required before benefit appears, and prolonged treatment is needed to maintain
it** [A1].

**All combined oral contraceptives are beneficial for acne** — **a systematic review showed little difference
in their efficacy, though further comparative data are needed** [A1]. A COC may be added to a topical
retinoid, benzoyl peroxide or azelaic acid in moderate acne, **either in addition to or instead of an oral
antibacterial** [A1].

**Cyproterone** is an anti-androgen; **doses higher than those in the COC are occasionally recommended by
specialists, particularly where there are signs of androgenisation such as hirsutism**. **Spironolactone** has
potent anti-androgen activity and **has been used by specialists for severe acne** [A1].

### Isotretinoin

**Acts primarily by reducing sebum secretion**; used in severe acne, or moderate acne responding poorly to
other treatment [A1].

⚠️ **Prescription is restricted to dermatologists or other authorised medical practitioners** — AMH directs
the reader to the relevant state or territory health department [A1].

⚠️ **A known teratogen. Pregnancy should be avoided during treatment and for at least 1 month after
stopping** [A1].

### What AMH does not endorse

**Abrasive agents (eg aluminium oxide), degreasing agents (eg triclosan), and preparations containing sulfur,
salicylic acid, resorcinol and allantoin** are **widely used, but their effectiveness is questionable** [A1].
⚠️ **Exfoliants may limit tolerance of other, more effective agents and have no effect on sebaceous gland
activity** [A1].


## Dosing

### Topical

| Agent | Dose [A1] |
|---|---|
| **Adapalene** | apply **once a day at bedtime** |
| **Tretinoin (topical)** | apply **once a day at bedtime** |
| **Trifarotene** | apply **once a day at bedtime** |
| **Adapalene with benzoyl peroxide** | **once a day at bedtime, starting with the 0.1% strength** |
| **Clindamycin with tretinoin** | **once a day at bedtime for up to 12 weeks** |
| **Clindamycin with benzoyl peroxide** | adult and child, **once daily in the evening** |
| **Benzoyl peroxide** | ⚠️ **begin treatment with the lower strength product**; adult and child, **once or twice a day** |
| **Azelaic acid** | adult and child, **morning and night** |
| **Clascoterone** | adult and child **>12 years**, **twice a day** |

### Oral

| Agent | Dose [A1] |
|---|---|
| **Doxycycline** | adult and child **>8 years**, oral **50 mg once daily for at least 6 weeks**; then if necessary **increase to 100 mg once daily** |
| **Minocycline** | adult and child **>8 years**, oral **50 mg once or twice daily, or 100 mg once daily** |
| **Erythromycin** | adult and child **>12 years**, oral **250–500 mg twice daily** |
| ⚠️ **Isotretinoin** | age **>12 years**, oral, **initially 0.5 mg/kg daily in 1 or 2 doses**. **After 4 weeks the dose may be adjusted according to response and tolerance; maximum 1 mg/kg daily**, which **may be poorly tolerated** |

⚠️ Note the **8-year floor on both tetracyclines** and the **12-year floor on erythromycin, clascoterone and
isotretinoin** — the age limits differ by agent and are easy to conflate.

⚠️ **Doxycycline starts at 50 mg, not 100 mg**, and is held there **for at least six weeks** before any
increase. That is a lower and slower start than the dose often used.

### Isotretinoin — the parts that are not the dose

**Monitoring**: **liver function tests and lipids at baseline, after the first month of treatment, and then
as clinically required** [A1].

**Contraception**: **two effective methods are recommended, for example a COC and a barrier method**, and
⚠️ **oral progestogen-only contraceptives are considered unsuitable** [A1]. Cover is required **during
treatment and for 1 month after stopping** [A1].

⚠️ **Do not donate blood during treatment and for 8 weeks after stopping** [A1].

**Course and relapse**: **most patients remain disease-free after a single course or have long remissions;
approximately 10–20% relapse, and repeat courses are recommended if recurrence is severe** [A1].
⚠️ **Allow at least 2 months after completing a course before deciding whether further treatment is
necessary, as improvement may continue for several months after stopping** [A1].

**Do not combine with topical anti-acne preparations — local irritation may increase** [A1]. ⚠️ **Avoid
combining with tetracyclines: the combination may increase the risk of benign intracranial hypertension and
is contraindicated by manufacturers** [A1].

**An acne flare usually occurs during the first few weeks of treatment; most cases are mild and improve with
continued treatment, but severe cases require urgent dermatologist review — dose reduction and oral
corticosteroids may be required** [A1].

**Psychiatric monitoring**: **the evidence for an association between isotretinoin and psychiatric disorders
is conflicting, and acne itself has been associated with psychiatric disorders; it is prudent to counsel
patients and monitor for psychological symptoms** [A1].

## When to refer

**Refer to a dermatologist** if acne is **severe or unresponsive to conventional treatment — for example
after 12 weeks of oral antibacterials** — or for **any patient at risk of scarring, including a family history
of acne scarring, or in significant psychological distress** [A1].

⚠️ **Acne conglobata, acne fulminans and pyoderma faciale** are severe inflammatory forms needing **urgent
dermatology referral for isotretinoin and oral corticosteroids** [A1].

**Endocrine evaluation** is indicated where there are **signs of androgen excess — hirsutism, androgenetic
alopecia, menstrual irregularities** [A1].

## Counselling

**Apply topical treatment to the entire affected area, not to individual lesions** [A1]. **Wash gently; avoid
vigorous scrubbing and abrasive cleansers, which may worsen inflammation** [A1]. **Avoid toners and oil-based
moisturisers** [A1]. **Do not squeeze or pick lesions — this increases scarring risk** [A1].

> **Eat a healthy balanced diet; there is no relationship between particular foods and acne** [A1].

## Skin of colour and postinflammatory hyperpigmentation

The AJGP source adds what AMH's single hypopigmentation caution gestures at. **Postinflammatory
hyperpigmentation (PIH) is a common complication of acne in patients with skin of colour, and should not be
ignored** [S2] — and it is often the patient's primary concern rather than the acne itself.

> **Because the patient is yet to trial any therapies, general measures should be suggested first.
> Photoprotection through the use of broad-spectrum sunscreen (SPF 50+) and protective clothing, such as
> hats, will reduce further darkening of the affected skin. Avoiding skin irritation, such as from
> scratching, rubbing or irritating fragrant skincare products, is also important** [S2].

⚠️ **However, to best treat the PIH, the acne must also be treated** [S2].

**Hair products as a cause**: the patient should be **educated on how the use of occlusive hair oils and
pomades contributed to her acne and encouraged, in a culturally sensitive way, to stop using these
products** [S2]. The article models what that means in practice — **learn about the hair care practices for
this patient and provide alternatives to oil-based products, such as silicone, along with practical
recommendations for washing her hair weekly or fortnightly with clarifying shampoo**; and if oil-based
products remain the patient's preference, **apply 2–3 cm away from the hairline with a covering over the hair
at night to prevent indirect spread to the skin** [S2].

**Retinoid introduction in skin of colour**: **topical tretinoin and adapalene are commonly prescribed
retinoids in Australia for both acne and PIH. Due to the irritant potential of all retinoids, low
concentrations should be used initially, with application starting a few times per week and gradually
increasing as tolerated** [S2].

**Escalation**: **if the acne does not demonstrate improvement over a period of approximately eight weeks,
oral antibiotics could be considered. Oral isotretinoin (dermatologist referral needed) might be required for
severe cases that are not responsive to topical therapy alone and in which triggers are unable to be
completely eliminated** [S2].

**Additional PIH measures** named: **topical hydroquinone, azelaic acid, chemical peels and laser-based
treatments** [S2]. ⚠️ For hydroquinone, **application to the entire face is preferred over spot treatment for
multiple areas of involvement to prevent irregular pigmentation** [S2].
