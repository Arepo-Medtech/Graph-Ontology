# Fitness to drive — seizures and epilepsy

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-22 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** the Australian national medical standard for driver licensing after a seizure.

> **Australian primary source.** Austroads. ***Assessing Fitness to Drive* (AP-G56)**, Part B section 6.2,
> *Seizures and epilepsy* [S1]. **Criteria changed from 22 June 2022.**

## ⚠️ A different kind of document

**Every other source in this compendium advises. This one governs.** *Assessing Fitness to Drive* is the
national medical standard adopted by the driver licensing authorities of every Australian state and territory.
It is not a clinical guideline, and the clinician is not the decision-maker:

> **The responsibility for issuing, renewing, suspending or cancelling a person's driver licence (including a
> conditional licence) lies ultimately with the driver licensing authority** [S1].

The doctor's role is to **provide information** against stated criteria. The patient's role is separate and
legal:

> **Patients should be made aware of the effects of their condition on driving and should be advised of their
> legal obligation to notify the driver licensing authority where driving is likely to be affected. The health
> professional may themselves advise the driver licensing authority as the situation requires** [S1].

**This is the cross-cutting document the compendium has been pointing at without naming it.** *Assessing
Fitness to Drive* also carries chapters on **blackouts, cardiovascular conditions, diabetes mellitus, hearing
loss, musculoskeletal conditions, dementia, psychiatric conditions, sleep disorders, substance misuse and
vision** [S1] — intersecting a dozen guidelines already in this set. Stroke's care plan requires *"advice on
when the person may resume driving"*; stroke follow-up assesses *"fitness to drive"*; dementia, ADHD, alcohol,
opioid stewardship and delirium all touch it. **This is where that advice actually comes from.**

⚠️ **This is not the epilepsy guideline either.** Diagnosis, seizure classification, drug choice, status
epilepticus and drug-resistant epilepsy remain absent from this compendium. Together with the **sodium
valproate** guideline, epilepsy is now covered from two edges and not the middle.

## The epidemiology, as the standard states it

- **Epilepsy refers to the tendency to experience recurrent seizures. Not all people who experience a seizure
  have epilepsy** [S1].
- **Cumulative incidence of 2 per cent of the population, with 0.5 per cent affected and taking medication at
  any one time** [S1].
- **Most cases respond well to treatment, with a terminal remission rate of 80 per cent or more. The majority
  suffer few seizures in a lifetime, and about half will have no further seizures in the first one or two
  years after starting treatment** [S1].
- **People with epilepsy are twice as likely to be involved in a motor vehicle crash compared with the general
  driving population** — though **the size of the risk varies considerably across the studies** [S1].
- ⚠️ **More recent studies have found that drivers who do not take antiseizure medication as prescribed are at
  an increased risk for experiencing a crash** [S1].

## ⚠️ Twelve months, or ten years

**The default standard, applying to everyone who has had a seizure** [S1]:

| | **Private** | **Commercial** |
|---|---|---|
| **Unconditional licence** | **Cannot be held if the person has experienced a seizure** | **Cannot be held if the person has experienced a seizure** |
| **Conditional licence** may be considered with **at least annual review**, on information from… | **the treating doctor** | **a specialist in epilepsy** |
| Seizure-free period required | **at least 12 months** | **at least 10 years** |
| EEG | *(not required)* | **an EEG in the last 6 months showing no epileptiform activity, and no other EEG in the last 12 months showing epileptiform activity** — *initial grant only, not annual review* |
| Plus | **the person follows medical advice, including adherence to medication if prescribed or recommended** | same |

**A tenfold difference between driving a car and driving a truck, stated as policy.** The reasoning is given:

> **Commercial vehicle driving exposes the driver and the public to a relatively greater risk because of the
> increased time spent at the wheel, as well as the generally greater potential for injury… For this reason,
> the acceptable risk of a seizure-related crash for commercial driving is much less, and the requirements
> applied are much stricter; in addition, sleep deprivation is a common provoking factor in epilepsy and may
> be experienced in long-distance transport driving and amongst drivers doing shift work** [S1].

**Who may speak to the licensing authority is also split:** > **the treating doctor or general practitioner
may liaise with the driver licensing authority about whether the criteria are met for driving a private
vehicle, but only a specialist may do so for a commercial vehicle driver** [S1].

**Extended remission relaxes review, not the standard:** more than **10 years** seizure-free (private) or
**20 years** (commercial) while on treatment may allow **reduced review requirements — at least once every
three years** [S1].

## The hierarchy, and the eight reductions

The standard is built as **a default plus defined reductions plus an escape hatch** [S1]. Each reduction
below applies to **private drivers only** unless stated.

| Situation [S1] | Private | Commercial |
|---|---|---|
| **Benign childhood seizure/epilepsy syndrome** (febrile seizures, benign focal epilepsy, childhood absence epilepsy) | **Unconditional licence**, provided **no seizures after age 11** | **Unconditional licence**, same condition |
| **First seizure (of any type)** | conditional after **6 months** seizure-free, with or without medication | conditional after **5 years** + EEG |
| **Epilepsy treated for the first time** (treatment started within the preceding 18 months) | conditional after **6 months treated and 6 months seizure-free** | **no reduction** |
| **Acute symptomatic seizures** (transient brain disorder or metabolic disturbance, no prior seizures) | conditional after **6 months** | conditional after **12 months** + EEG |
| **'Safe' seizures** | conditional if **'safe' seizures for ≥2 years and no other seizure type for ≥2 years** | **no reduction** |
| **Sleep-only seizures** | conditional if **no previous waking seizures and first sleep-only seizure ≥12 months ago**; *or* **no waking seizures in 2 years and sleep-only seizures for ≥2 years** | **no reduction** |
| **Seizure in previously well-controlled epilepsy** | **4 weeks** if provoked by an identified, reliably avoidable factor; **3 months** if no cause identified | **no reduction** |
| **Exceptional cases** | conditional if a **medical specialist experienced in managing epilepsy** advises and the authority judges **the risk of a crash caused by a seizure is acceptably low** | same, via **a specialist in epilepsy** |

**Resumption of an unconditional licence** (private): **no seizures for at least 5 years and no antiseizure
medication for at least the preceding 12 months** [S1]. **For commercial drivers, resumption of an
unconditional licence will not be considered** [S1].

## ⚠️ One hard gate: the crash

> **If a person has experienced a crash or has lost control of the vehicle as a result of a seizure, the
> default seizure-free non-driving period applies, even if they fall into one of the seizure categories that
> allow a reduction** [S1].

**Every reduction in the table above is void if a seizure has already caused a crash.** It is the only
provision in the chapter that admits no exception, and it is stated three separate times.

## ⚠️ Refusal, and doubt

> **If a patient refuses to follow a treating doctor's recommendation, the patient should be assessed as not
> fit to drive. The treating doctor may consider reporting this information to the driver licensing authority
> so it can be considered in the event that another doctor certifies the person as fit to drive without
> therapy** [S1].

**The stated reason for reporting is to pre-empt a second opinion.** That is unusually explicit, and it sits
alongside an equally unusual provision:

> **If the treating doctor doubts the reliability of the relevant clinical information (e.g. unreported
> seizures, likely due to the person not recognising the occurrence of seizures or deliberately not reporting
> seizures), the person is not fit to drive** [S1].

**Doubt itself is disqualifying.** Nothing else in this compendium makes the clinician's uncertainty, rather
than a finding, the operative fact. The standard gives the reason: **some people with epilepsy are unable to
reliably report the occurrence of seizures because their awareness is impaired by their seizure**, and
**corroboration by people in regular contact with the person may decrease any uncertainty** [S1].

## ⚠️ Switching drugs to protect a pregnancy costs three months of driving

**Planned withdrawal of antiseizure medication**, private drivers — **the person should not drive during the
period in which the dose is being tapered, and for 3 months after the last dose** [S1]. And then:

> **If a drug is being withdrawn as part of a switch from one drug to another (e.g. to reduce teratogenic
> risk), the 3-month non-driving period still applies** [S1].

**This lands exactly on the sodium valproate guideline in this compendium**, which advises switching women of
childbearing potential from valproate to lamotrigine or levetiracetam. **The switch that reduces the
teratogenic risk removes the licence for three months.** Both documents are correct; neither mentions the
other; the patient carries the collision. It belongs in the consent conversation, and this is the most
concrete cross-guideline consequence the compendium has surfaced.

**If seizures recur during withdrawal**, a conditional licence may be considered once **the previously
effective regimen is resumed and there have been no seizures for 4 weeks** [S1].

**Dose *reduction* is treated separately**, and driving may continue if [S1]:

- **the dose reduction is due only to the presence of current dose-related side effects and is unlikely to
  affect seizure control**; **or**
- **the dose is being reduced after an increase due to a temporary situation that has now resolved (e.g.
  pregnancy) to the dose that was effective before the increase**

Otherwise the same rule applies — **no driving during the reduction and for 3 months after**. **For commercial
drivers, withdrawal means the criteria for a conditional licence are no longer met**, full stop [S1].

## Definitions that do real work

**Two or more seizures in a 24-hour period are considered a single seizure** [S1] — for the first-seizure
standard.

⚠️ > **For the purpose of these standards, sleep deprivation is not considered a provoking factor** [S1].

**A definitional exclusion that closes the most available excuse.** The standard also notes generally that
**some provocative factors (e.g. sleep deprivation), unless severe, cannot be reliably avoided** [S1].

**'Well controlled'** is defined as **no seizures during the 12 months leading up to the last seizure** [S1].

**'Safe' seizures** must be **well established without exceptions and corroborated by reliable witnesses or
video-EEG recording** [S1], because:

> **people may believe their consciousness is unimpaired when it is not. For example, some 'auras' are
> associated with impaired consciousness that the person does not perceive** [S1].

> **Any seizures that involve confusion/vagueness, automatisms, difficulty speaking or emotional features or
> memory loss are not considered safe seizures** [S1].

On the prolonged aura defence — that a driver could pull over in time — the standard is sceptical and says why:
**it may be impossible to stop immediately and safely because of traffic conditions**, and **even if the person
is able to stop the vehicle before the seizure, they may then be in a confused state and not appreciate the
danger of resuming their journey** [S1]. It is allowed **only in exceptional circumstances** and **case by
case**.

**Isolated infrequent myoclonic jerks (without impaired awareness) may be considered safe in the context of no
seizures of any other type for more than 12 months** [S1].

## The six-month window after starting treatment

For **epilepsy treated for the first time**, the standard builds in tolerance for titration [S1]:

> **When treatment with an antiseizure drug is started in a previously untreated person, enough time should
> pass to establish that the drug is effective before driving is recommenced. However, effectiveness cannot be
> established until the person reaches an appropriate dose. For example, if a drug is being gradually
> introduced over three weeks and a seizure occurs in the second week, it would be premature to declare the
> drug ineffective.**

**Seizures in the first six months after starting treatment do not lengthen the required seizure-free period;
seizures after six months do.** The standard works its own example [S1]:

| Seizure occurs | Fit to drive |
|---|---|
| **3 months after starting therapy** | **6 months after the most recent seizure** (9 months after starting therapy) |
| **8 months after starting therapy** | default standard — **12 months after the most recent seizure** |

**If the patient has received no treatment in the last five years or more, resumption of treatment is managed
as if treated for the first time** [S1].

## EEG's limited place

> **Because there is a wide range of clinical situations, with varying utility of EEG, it does not form part
> of the driving standards except for the initial fitness assessment of commercial drivers** [S1].

But where an incidental finding appears — **generalised spike-wave bursts lasting longer than three seconds,
or photoparoxysmal response** — in someone who otherwise meets the standard, **their fitness should be
assessed on a case-by-case basis**, possibly requiring **more detailed testing** [S1].

## What to tell every licence holder

> - **The person must continue to take antiseizure medication regularly as recommended.**
> - **The person should ensure they get adequate sleep and not drive when sleep-deprived.**
> - **The person should avoid circumstances, or the use of substances (e.g. excessive alcohol), that are known
>   to increase the risk of seizures.** [S1]

And: **it is good medical practice for any person with initial seizures to be referred to a specialist, where
available, for accurate diagnosis of the specific epilepsy syndrome so that appropriate treatment is
instituted and all the risks associated with epilepsy, including driving, can be explained** [S1].

**Conditional licences rely on individual responsibility for management of the condition, including compliance
with treatment, in conjunction with the support of a health professional and regular review** [S1].
