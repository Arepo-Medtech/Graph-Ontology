# Gout

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-21 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults with crystal-proven or clinically diagnosed gout. Acute flare and long-term urate lowering.

> Every clinical sentence carries a source tag. Claims no retrieved source supports are listed under
> *Unresolved* rather than written. **No free Australian guideline exists for gout** — the sources here are
> American and European, and the Australian layer is PBS listing only.

## Treatment aims

Two separable goals, and conflating them is the common error: **terminate the flare**, and **lower serum
urate below the saturation point so crystals dissolve and flares stop recurring**.

Both American and European guidance set a **serum urate target below 6 mg/dL (360 µmol/L)** [S1][S2], with
EULAR setting a tighter target **below 5 mg/dL (300 µmol/L) in severe gout** [S2]. ACR frames this as a
treat-to-target strategy with dose titration guided by serial serum urate measurement, and grades it a
*strong* recommendation [S1].

## Drug treatment — acute flare

Colchicine, NSAIDs, oral or intra-articular corticosteroids, or a combination [S2].

Where flares are frequent and colchicine, NSAIDs and corticosteroids are all contraindicated, an
interleukin-1 blocker should be considered [S2].

## Drug treatment — urate-lowering therapy

**Allopurinol is first line**, including in moderate-to-severe chronic kidney disease (stage >3) — a *strong*
ACR recommendation [S1]. That CKD carve-out is worth noting, because it is where allopurinol is most often
wrongly withheld.

**Start low.** Allopurinol ≤100 mg/day, lower in CKD; febuxostat <40 mg/day. Strong recommendation [S1].

**Titrate to target, not to dose.** Serial serum urate guides titration to <6 mg/dL [S1].

### When to start

ACR strongly recommends initiating urate-lowering therapy for **tophaceous gout, radiographic damage due to
gout, or frequent flares** [S1]. EULAR goes further, recommending it be *considered from the first
presentation* [S2] — a real difference between the two, recorded rather than averaged.

### Flare prophylaxis on initiation

Concomitant anti-inflammatory prophylaxis for **at least 3–6 months** when starting urate-lowering therapy.
Strong recommendation [S1].

## Australian context

PBS Schedule 4333, checked directly [S3]:

| Agent | PBS | Benefit type |
|---|---|---|
| Allopurinol | listed — 100 mg, 300 mg tablets | **unrestricted** (also R) |
| Colchicine | listed — 500 microgram tablets | **unrestricted** |
| Probenecid | listed — 500 mg tablets | unrestricted (also R) |
| Prednisolone | listed | unrestricted (also R) |
| Indometacin | listed | unrestricted (also R) |
| Febuxostat | listed — 80 mg tablets | **authority required (streamlined)** |

The first-line agent is unrestricted, as is flare treatment. Febuxostat — the alternative when allopurinol
fails or is not tolerated — carries an authority requirement, so the step from first to second line is an
administrative step as well as a clinical one.
