# Procedural sedation in children

## Key points

- **Use non-pharmacological methods (play therapy, distraction) as alternatives or adjuncts, and topical, local or regional anaesthesia where appropriate** (eg Bier block). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Ensure adequate analgesia and watch for the combined effect of sedation and opioid.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Only clinicians trained and approved in paediatric sedation**, experienced in paediatric resuscitation, airway management and monitoring, should use sedation agents. [S1]
- **Sedatives can cause airway compromise, respiratory depression and CNS depression**, so careful monitoring and skilled staff are essential. [S1]

## Before sedation

- **Pre-sedation:** review need and urgency, risk factors and contraindications · consent from the child (if appropriate) and parents · **fasting per local guidelines** · analgesia for painful procedures · **sedation checklist with role assignment** · prepare equipment · **agree a plan B for sedation failure.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Resuscitation equipment and staff competent in paediatric resuscitation and airway management must be readily available before starting.** [S1]
- **Do not proceed** if child, clinician or environment factors make sedation or the procedure unsafe. [S1]

## High-risk children — get senior or specialist advice first

- ⚠️ **Higher risk of severe adverse effects: seek senior clinician or specialist advice on risk versus benefit.** [S1]
- **Airway:** severe obstructive airway disease (croup, foreign body, head and facial trauma); previous airway surgery, laryngomalacia, craniofacial abnormality. **Breathing:** severe respiratory distress (pneumonia, bronchiolitis, asthma); sleep apnoea. [S1]
- **Circulation:** shock; congenital heart disease. **Neurological:** altered consciousness, seizure, meningitis, trauma, space-occupying lesion; unstable epilepsy, neuromuscular disease. **Other:** unstable psychiatric disorder; previous sedation failure or anaesthetic reaction. [S1]

## Choosing an agent

- **Choice depends on the aim** (less movement, analgesia, anxiolysis, amnesia), the child (age, compliance, development), the setting (time, staffing, load, GA availability) and the procedure (emergency vs planned, length, intensity). [S1]
- **Chloral hydrate:** oral, but **narrow therapeutic index** and no analgesia. [S1]
- **Midazolam:** oral, intranasal, buccal, IM or IV; amnestic, anxiolytic, quick and short acting — but **can cause a paradoxical effect after discharge**, may fail, and gives no analgesia. [S1]
- **Intranasal fentanyl:** synergistic with nitrous oxide and avoids IV access, but no sedative effect. **Nitrous oxide:** quick on and off, anxiolysis and amnesia; vomiting is common and analgesia limited. [S1]
- **Ketamine:** airway reflexes kept, cardiovascular stability, IM or IV, excellent analgesia and sedation — **can be a sole agent**; agitation and emesis in recovery, rarely laryngospasm. [S1]
- **Propofol:** ultrashort-acting, good muscle relaxation (eg ankle dislocation) — but ⚠️ **narrow therapeutic window, respiratory and cardiovascular depression, and no analgesia.** [S1]
- **Chloral hydrate, midazolam and propofol may need extra analgesia** (eg fentanyl) for painful procedures. ⚠️ **Ketamine and propofol only by clinicians trained and approved in their use, with advanced airway experience.** [S1]

## Monitoring and discharge

- ⚠️ **Monitor pulse oximetry, cardiac rhythm and blood pressure, watch airway and chest movement closely, and keep skilled staff present until recovery is well established.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Do not discharge until back to premorbid neurological baseline** — walking and talking at the pre-treatment level. [S1]
- **If sedation fails:** try an alternative if safe; involve the paediatric team if a general anaesthetic is needed; **transfer if needs exceed local capability.** [S1]
