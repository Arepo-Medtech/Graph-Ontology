# Foreign body ingestion in children (including button batteries)

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** assessment, imaging, and specialist referral for ingested foreign bodies in children — button batteries, magnets, sharp and lead objects, water beads, oropharyngeal impaction, oesophageal food bolus, coins — plus discharge criteria. Toxic ingestions are in `poisoning-initial-management-children`. No adult or other-source counterpart exists in the corpus (the AMH `gastrointestinal-decontamination` guideline records button batteries as not addressed).

> ✅ **OPEN AND QUOTED.** 22 of 22 claims quoted verbatim from the **RCH Melbourne Clinical Practice Guideline *Foreign body ingestion*** (Last updated February 2024); **22 fragments re-checked by machine. 1 quoted doses.**

> ⚠️ **An oesophageal button battery must be removed within two hours** to avoid serious complications including death. While awaiting theatre, give honey to children over 1 year within 12 hours of ingestion.

---

## Key points

- **Most ingested foreign bodies are low risk** and need no imaging or intervention. ⚠️ **High-risk objects can cause significant harm or death — discuss with a senior clinician or specialist.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Oesophageal button batteries must be removed within two hours.** [S1]
- **Large objects, magnets and objects impacted in the oropharynx need surgical review.** Ingested objects may be toxic — consider Poisons Information (13 11 26). [S1]
- **Peak incidence 6 months to 3 years**; developmental, behavioural problems and pica raise the risk. **Older children who ingest intentionally need a mental health review.** [S1]

## Assessment

- **Ask about:** object type and timing · coughing, drooling, painful swallowing, reduced intake, abdominal pain or vomiting, melaena or GI bleeding · **choking or respiratory distress (consider inhalation).** [S1]
- ⚠️ **High-risk foreign bodies:** oesophageal button battery · a magnet plus metal, or more than 1 magnet · sharp objects in the oesophagus · lead objects that fail to leave the stomach · large objects (over 6 cm long and/or over 2 cm wide) · expandable objects (water beads) · multi-component toys. [S1]
- **Higher impaction risk:** pre-existing GI abnormalities or repaired/unrepaired malformations · eosinophilic oesophagitis · neuromuscular disease. [S1]
- **Examine for** airway compromise, drooling or oropharyngeal injury, respiratory distress, and abdominal tenderness, peritonitis or obstruction. [S1]

## Imaging

- ⚠️ **Image:** suspected or known button battery · magnet(s) or other high-risk radio-opaque object · unknown object · high-risk or unwell child. **In smaller children, one X-ray covering neck, chest and abdomen.** [S1]
- **Imaging may be skipped only if ALL apply:** asymptomatic · normal examination · no known GI abnormality · reassuring observation · eating and drinking · object under 2 cm diameter and under 6 cm long, **not a magnet or battery**, non-expandable and non-toxic. [S1]

## Button batteries and magnets

- ⚠️ **Critically unwell children and high-risk objects (button battery, magnets, sharp or lead objects) need time-critical care.** The referral team (ENT, general surgery or gastroenterology) depends on local service, object and site. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Oesophageal button battery: honey for children over 1 year, within 12 hours of ingestion, while awaiting theatre — 10 mL every 10 minutes, up to 6 doses.** [S1]
- **Batteries in the stomach or beyond without signs of GI injury:** discuss with the relevant specialist for a plan and timeframe. [S1]
- ⚠️ **One magnet on X-ray may still be high risk** — two stuck together can look like one. [S1]

## Other objects

- **Water beads (superabsorbent polymers)** can pass the pylorus, then expand enough to obstruct the bowel — **discuss early with Poisons Information** about growth potential and endoscopic removal. [S1]
- **Objects impacted in the oropharynx need urgent ENT evaluation**; fish bones may lodge in the tonsils. [S1]
- **Oesophageal food bolus:** ⚠️ **acutely symptomatic (drooling, neck or chest pain) → urgent endoscopy**; if managing secretions, discuss early with paediatric gastroenterology for retrieval within 12–24 hours. [S1]
- **Carbonated drinks may help mobilise a food bolus** (may cause vomiting). ⚠️ **Glucagon and hyoscine butylbromide are not recommended in children.** [S1]
- **Coins and blunt objects:** symptomatic → urgent endoscopy; **asymptomatic oesophageal object → endoscopic removal within 24 hours**; over 6 cm long → gastroenterology or surgical opinion. **A diameter over 2 cm (Australian 10c, 20c, 50c, $1) is unlikely to pass a young child's pylorus.** [S1]

## Disposition

- **Consult the relevant specialty when:** symptomatic · immediate removal needed · FB lodged in the oesophagus · toxicity concern · significant past history or behavioural issues. [S1]
- **Discharge when** the child looks well, is pain-free, has no respiratory distress, can eat and drink, and has a reliable history of a low-risk FB. [S1]
- **Return if:** breathing problems · abdominal pain · fever · vomiting · unable to tolerate food and drink · melaena or haematemesis. [S1]
