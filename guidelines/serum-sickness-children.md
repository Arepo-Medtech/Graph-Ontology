# Serum sickness and serum sickness-like reactions in children

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** recognition, triggers, differential diagnosis and management of true serum sickness and serum sickness-like reactions (SSLRs) in children. No adult counterpart exists in the corpus. > ⚠️ **Rash, fever and polyarthritis days to weeks after a drug or antiserum: stop the offending agent and avoid it afterwards.** Mucous membrane involvement points away from SSLR (think erythema multiforme or SJS).

---

## Key points

- **Key features: rash, fever and polyarthritis.** [S1]
- **Timing:** true serum sickness one to two weeks after first exposure; SSLRs usually after 5 to 10 days. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Identify and remove the causative agent** — note it may already have been stopped before the rash appears. [S1]

## Triggers

- **True serum sickness** is a delayed hypersensitivity reaction triggered by snake antivenom, equine and rabbit anti-thymocyte globulin (ATG), monoclonal antibodies (rituximab most often), insect stings such as bees, and rabies and tetanus antiserum. [S1]
- **SSLRs are more common in children** and milder, without measurable immune complexes. **Commonest drug triggers: cefaclor and amoxicillin**; illness, immunisation and occasionally chemicals can also trigger them. [S1]

## Assessment

- **History:** illness, immunisation, antibiotics and other medicines in the two weeks before onset. [S1]
- **Rash:** in SSLRs usually urticarial, starting in the flexures then generalising; **unlike acute urticaria, lesions persist for days in the same area**; pruritic. [S1]
- **Fever:** over 38.5 in true serum sickness; may be absent or low-grade in an SSLR. [S1]
- **Arthralgia:** acute joint pain, limp or inability to walk; in up to two-thirds of patients, most often the metacarpophalangeal joints, knees, wrists, ankles and shoulders. [S1]
- **Examination:** lesions gradually expand and may leave central clearing or slight purpura; erythema and oedema of the hands and feet are common; lymphadenopathy; facial and periorbital oedema. [S1]
- In true serum sickness the rash varies (urticarial, palpable purpura, morbilliform, papules, maculopapular); urticaria is the commonest rash in SSLRs. [S1]
- ⚠️ **The mucous membranes are not involved in either condition.** Erythema multiforme and Stevens-Johnson syndrome typically do involve them. [S1]
- **Differentials:** acute viral exanthem · urticaria · systemic-onset juvenile idiopathic arthritis · erythema multiforme · Stevens-Johnson syndrome · Kawasaki disease · acute rheumatic fever. [S1]
- **SSLR is a clinical diagnosis; laboratory investigation is not necessary.** Finding the culprit can be hard if the child is on several medicines and has been recently unwell. [S1]

## Management

- ⚠️ **Identify and stop the offending drug** — fever and arthralgia usually resolve and new lesions stop forming within 48 hours. [S1]
- **Symptomatic treatment: NSAIDs and/or antihistamines.** Systemic glucocorticoids may help with more severe symptoms and high fevers, though the evidence is low. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Once diagnosed, avoid the offending drug** — re-exposure may cause a more rapid and severe serum sickness. [S1]
- **Consult the paediatric team** if ongoing or future treatment with the drug is desirable (lifesaving, or no suitable alternative) — refer to clinical pharmacology or the paediatric drug allergy clinic — or for advice on alternative antimicrobials. [S1]
- **Discharge** with adequate symptom relief and pain control, and information about serum sickness. [S1]
