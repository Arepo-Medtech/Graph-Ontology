# Acute rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart disease

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-21 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults and children, primary care.

> **Australian primary source.** Ralph AP, Currie BJ. *Therapeutics for rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart
> disease.* **Aust Prescr**, published 1 August 2022 [S1] — a peer-reviewed synthesis of the national
> guideline. The national guideline itself: RHDAustralia (ARF/RHD writing group), *The 2020 Australian
> guideline for prevention, diagnosis and management of acute rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart disease,
> Version 3.2* [S2] — **not retrieved**.

## The disease Australia has not finished having

> **At least 8000 people in Australia currently have acute rheumatic fever or rheumatic heart disease** [S1].

> **In Australia, nearly 90% of acute rheumatic fever cases and 70% of rheumatic heart disease diagnoses are
> in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people** [S1].

> **In up to 75% of people with rheumatic heart disease, previous acute rheumatic fever was
> unrecognised** [S1].

Three numbers that set the task. The disease is concentrated, it is preventable at the point of the
streptococcal infection, and **three-quarters of the people who end up with damaged valves were never
diagnosed with the illness that damaged them.**

**Notifiable in the Northern Territory, Western Australia, Queensland, South Australia and New South
Wales** [S1] — five jurisdictions, not all.

**Peak incidence is 5–14 years of age**, but **approximately 7% of notifications in Australia are in 35–44
year olds** [S1]. It is not only a paediatric diagnosis.

**Most cases occur when exposure risk is high — crowded living conditions, or inadequate access to sanitation
facilities and health care** [S1].

## ⚠️ There is no diagnostic test

> **There is no dedicated diagnostic test, and instead it is diagnosed using the Jones criteria. These
> factors make the diagnosis highly challenging** [S1].

**Diagnosis requires actively excluding alternative diagnoses, then applying the Jones criteria** [S1].

What it actually looks like in Australia [S1]:

- **Approximately 50% of cases involve a fever with joint pain.**
- **Joint pain may be subtle** — no heat, effusion or erythema; **only pain and limping** — **or florid**,
  with classical migratory polyarthritis predominantly affecting large joints.
- **Carditis with arthritis is next most common**, then chorea, then carditis alone.
- **Erythema marginatum and subcutaneous nodules are reported in less than 1% of local cases** — the
  textbook signs are the ones you will not see.
- **Carditis alone may comprise only fever with evidence of valve disease** on echocardiography, **with or
  without a murmur**, and with or without a conduction abnormality such as first-degree heart block.

> **Acute rheumatic fever should therefore be considered in a child with a high risk of streptococcal
> exposure presenting with unexplained fever** [S1] — because **fever can be the only sign** [S1].

**Sydenham chorea** may occur **weeks to months** after the streptococcal infection, and **fever, elevated
inflammatory markers and elevated streptococcal serology may all be absent** [S1].

**Hospitalisation for rheumatic fever is recommended**, to confirm the diagnosis and facilitate prompt access
to an echocardiogram [S1].

## ⚠️ Do not give anti-inflammatories before the diagnosis is made

> **Initial analgesia is preferred during diagnostic uncertainty to avoid the masking effect that
> anti-inflammatory use can have on migratory joint symptoms, fever and concentrations of inflammatory
> markers** [S1].

| While awaiting diagnostic confirmation | Drug [S1] |
|---|---|
| **Mild to moderate pain** | **Paracetamol 1000 mg (children 15 mg/kg) orally every 4 hours as needed, max 60 mg/kg/day or 4000 mg/day** |
| **Severe pain** | **Tramadol immediate-release 50–100 mg (children 1–2 mg/kg) orally every 4 hours as needed, max 400 mg/day** — **usually avoided in children under 12 due to variable metabolism**; use only when strong analgesia is essential and cautious monitoring is available |

The NSAID that would relieve the arthritis also erases the evidence for the diagnosis. This is the clearest
"treat the symptom later" instruction in thirty-one guidelines.

## What changed in the 2020 national guideline

The source names four changes [S1]:

1. **Duration of secondary prophylaxis is now shorter for some people without cardiac involvement.**
2. **NSAIDs (naproxen or ibuprofen) are now first-line for arthritis instead of aspirin.**
3. **Lidocaine (lignocaine) is no longer contraindicated with intramuscular injections of benzathine
   benzylpenicillin G.**
4. **Endocarditis prophylaxis is now recommended for all patients with rheumatic heart disease, not just for
   Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people.**

Change 3 is small in print and large in practice: the injection is monthly for years, and pain is a
documented reason people stop coming. Change 4 removes a race-based distinction from a prophylaxis rule.

## Treating the episode

### Eradicating the inciting infection

| Order | Regimen [S1] |
|---|---|
| **1** | **Benzathine benzylpenicillin G 1,200,000 units (child <20 kg: 600,000 units; ≥20 kg: 1,200,000 units) intramuscularly, single dose** |
| 2 | **Phenoxymethylpenicillin 500 mg (child 15 mg/kg up to 500 mg) orally every 12 hours for 10 days** |
| 3 | **Non-severe penicillin hypersensitivity: cefalexin 1 g (child 25 mg/kg up to 1 g) orally every 12 hours for 10 days** |
| 4 | **Immediate penicillin hypersensitivity: azithromycin 500 mg (child 12 mg/kg up to 500 mg) orally daily for 5 days** |

**Streptococcal infection may not be evident by the time acute rheumatic fever manifests — cultures are often
negative — but eradication therapy is still recommended** [S1]. **Intramuscular penicillin is preferred**
because of better adherence **and its subsequent ongoing use in secondary prophylaxis** [S1]: the first
prophylactic dose and the eradication dose are the same injection [S1].

### Arthritis

| Order | Regimen [S1] |
|---|---|
| **1** | **Naproxen immediate-release 250–500 mg (children 10–20 mg/kg/day) orally twice daily, max 1250 mg daily** |
| 2 | **Ibuprofen 200–400 mg (children 5–10 mg/kg) orally three times daily, max 2400 mg daily** |
| 3 | **Aspirin 50–60 mg/kg/day orally in 4–5 divided doses, escalating to max 80–100 mg/kg/day** |

**Naproxen may be safer than aspirin, and is convenient for twice-daily dosing and the availability of an
oral suspension** [S1]. **There are fewer data with ibuprofen in acute rheumatic fever than with
naproxen** [S1].

> **The dose of NSAIDs needed for acute rheumatic fever is generally higher than the dose recommended for
> other conditions; therefore it may be appropriate to start at the higher dose range** [S1].

> **Due to the rare possibility of Reye's syndrome in children, aspirin may need to be discontinued during
> intercurrent acute viral illness; thus influenza vaccination is strongly recommended** [S1].

**Initial high-dose NSAID therapy, weaned after 1–2 weeks, is usual.** **Most episodes resolve within six
weeks and 90% within 12 weeks. A rebound in inflammatory symptoms can occur on ceasing treatment, requiring
the drugs to be re-introduced** [S1]. **Consider a proton pump inhibitor for prolonged
anti-inflammatory treatment** [S1].

### Carditis

> **There is no targeted drug therapy available for cardiac valve damage during the acute inflammatory
> stage** [S1].

**Corticosteroids are recommended for severe carditis — prednisolone 1–2 mg/kg up to 80 mg orally once
daily** [S1] — but the evidence is explicitly contested:

> **Meta-analyses have suggested their lack of benefit in preventing subsequent rheumatic heart disease,
> although the studies were mostly performed before the availability of echocardiography. Expert opinion
> recommends corticosteroids for carditis associated with heart failure** [S1].

**If NSAIDs were prescribed for pericarditis or arthritis, they can be discontinued when corticosteroids are
started** [S1].

> **Screening for and management of latent infections (e.g. hepatitis B, strongyloidiasis, tuberculosis) are
> required before or on starting immunosuppressive corticosteroid doses** [S1].

That instruction connects to the hepatitis B guideline in this set, which records that **resolved hepatitis
B can flare under immunosuppression.**

**Heart failure from acute carditis is managed on the same principles as acute heart failure** [S1], with
frusemide, spironolactone, nitrates and an ACE inhibitor (**perindopril or ramipril in moderate or severe LV
systolic dysfunction**) in adults [S1]. **Digoxin is rarely used** [S1]. **Seek specialist cardiologist
advice early** [S1].

**Hydroxychloroquine has been used as a targeted disease-modifying agent based on promising in vitro
findings, but clinical trial data are not yet available** [S1].

### Chorea

**Pharmacotherapy is not needed for mild chorea.** **Treatment should be considered if movements interfere
substantially with normal activities** [S1].

| Order | Regimen [S1] |
|---|---|
| **1** | **Carbamazepine 3.5–10 mg/kg per dose orally twice daily** — first line for its safety profile |
| 2 | **Sodium valproate 7.5–10 mg/kg per dose orally twice daily** |
| **Very severe chorea or chorea paralytica** | **Add prednisolone 1–2 mg/kg up to 80 mg orally daily** |

> **A treatment response may not be observed for 1–2 weeks, and drugs may only reduce, not eliminate,
> chorea. Treatment should be continued for 2–4 weeks after chorea has subsided, and then be withdrawn** [S1].

**Corticosteroids are recommended if the response to carbamazepine or sodium valproate is insufficient**;
**IV immunoglobulin and plasmapheresis might be beneficial experimental immunotherapies** [S1].

## Secondary prophylaxis — the whole of prevention

> **Secondary antibiotic prophylaxis is the only treatment confirmed to be associated with a long-term
> reduction in the severity of rheumatic heart disease** [S1].

| Order | Regimen [S1] | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Benzathine benzylpenicillin G by deep intramuscular injection — 1,200,000 units (≥20 kg) or 600,000 units (<20 kg)** | **Every 28 days**; **every 21 days for selected groups** |
| 2 | **Phenoxymethylpenicillin (penicillin V) 250 mg orally twice daily** | |
| 3 | **Penicillin hypersensitivity (non-severe or immediate): erythromycin 250 mg orally twice daily** | |

**Intramuscular penicillin is preferred due to greater effectiveness in head-to-head trial and better
adherence** [S1]. **Regular oral penicillin is not as effective**, potentially due to the serum
concentrations achieved and problems with adherence [S1].

**Every 21 days may be considered for** [S1]:

- **patients who have breakthrough acute rheumatic fever despite complete adherence to a 28-day regimen**
- **patients at high risk of adverse consequences if acute rheumatic fever occurs** — severe rheumatic heart
  disease, or a history of heart valve surgery.

> **Patients on 28-day regimens can be recalled from 21 days to help ensure that injections are given by day
> 28** [S1].

A recall window rather than a due date — the schedule is designed around the reality of missed appointments,
not around the pharmacology alone. **Increasing adherence to benzathine benzylpenicillin G is associated with
improved rheumatic fever outcomes** [S1].

**For children under 10 kg, 600,000 units is still generally recommended, but seek paediatric advice for
careful planning of the regimen** [S1].

### Duration

| Diagnosis | Duration [S1] |
|---|---|
| **Possible ARF (no cardiac involvement)** — incomplete features, normal echo and normal ECG throughout | **12 months, then reassess** |
| **Probable ARF (no cardiac involvement)** | **Minimum 5 years after the most recent episode, or until age 21 — whichever is longer** |
| **Definite ARF (no cardiac involvement)** | **Minimum 5 years after the most recent episode, or until age 21 — whichever is longer** |
| **Borderline RHD (people ≤ 20 years only, no documented ARF)** | **In a high-risk setting: minimum 2 years from diagnosis. If still present at 2 years, continue another 2 years and reassess. Seek specialist input** |
| **Mild RHD** | **With documented ARF: minimum 10 years after the most recent episode, or until age 21 — whichever is longer.** Without documented ARF and aged < 35: minimum 5 years from RHD diagnosis or until 21, whichever is longer |
| **Moderate RHD** | **With documented ARF: minimum 10 years after the most recent episode, or until age 35 — whichever is longer.** Without documented ARF and aged < 35: minimum 5 years from diagnosis or until 35, whichever is longer |
| **Severe RHD, or previous valve repair or prosthetic valve** | **With documented ARF: minimum 10 years after the most recent episode, or until age 40 — whichever is longer.** Without documented ARF: minimum 5 years from diagnosis or until 40, whichever is longer |

**If diagnosed with mild or moderate RHD at age ≥ 35 without acute rheumatic fever, secondary prophylaxis is
not required** [S1]. **If diagnosed with severe RHD at ≥ 40 without ARF, specialist input is required** [S1].

**The risk of recurrence is low in people ≥ 40 years, however lifelong secondary prophylaxis is usually
recommended for patients who have had, or are likely to need, heart valve surgery** [S1].

**Prophylaxis may be considered for longer in women considering pregnancy who live in high-risk circumstances
for acute rheumatic fever** [S1].

**Echocardiography after cessation:** at 1, 3 and 5 years for probable/definite ARF and mild RHD; **initially
every 12 months** for moderate; **initially every 6 months** for severe [S1].

> **All people receiving secondary prophylaxis require a comprehensive clinical assessment and echocardiogram
> before cessation. Risk factors including future exposure to environments with a high burden of group A
> streptococcus must be considered** [S1].

**A March 2022 update recommends secondary prophylaxis for people ≤ 20 years of age living in high-risk
settings without a documented history of acute rheumatic fever but who have an echocardiogram showing
borderline rheumatic heart disease** [S1].

**Moderate or severe RHD may rarely improve on echocardiography without valve surgery; the cessation
conditions then follow the most recent severity category** [S1].

## Penicillin allergy, and why penicillin still works

> ***S. pyogenes* remains susceptible to penicillin, as it is unable to genetically express resistance to
> penicillin** [S1].

**An estimated 3.2% of people have an allergic reaction to penicillin and 0.2% have anaphylactic
reactions** [S1].

**Macrolides cover approximately 88% of *S. pyogenes* isolates** (Northern Territory Top End antibiogram
data), **due to the development of class resistance to macrolides and clindamycin in some isolates.** The
proportion resistant in any region **is related to local prescribing practices.** **As long as most isolates
remain susceptible, macrolides are an acceptable second-line option** [S1]. **Between 3% and 30% of group A
streptococcus isolates internationally are resistant to macrolides** [S1].

**Non-severe penicillin hypersensitivity: cefalexin for the inciting infection, erythromycin for secondary
prophylaxis. Immediate penicillin hypersensitivity: azithromycin for the inciting infection, erythromycin for
secondary prophylaxis** [S1].

## Endocarditis prophylaxis

> **The weight of evidence now favours antibiotic use for infective endocarditis prophylaxis in all patients
> with rheumatic heart disease undergoing high-risk dental or other surgical procedures** [S1].

| Indication | Drug [S1] | Before procedure |
|---|---|---|
| **Endocarditis prophylaxis** | **Amoxicillin 2 g (children 50 mg/kg up to 2 g)** | **60 minutes** |
| **Delayed non-severe penicillin hypersensitivity** | **Cefalexin 2 g (children 50 mg/kg up to 2 g)** | 60 minutes |
| **Immediate (severe or non-severe) or delayed severe penicillin hypersensitivity** | **Clindamycin 600 mg (children 20 mg/kg up to 600 mg)** | **60–120 minutes** |
| **Recently received a treatment-dose course of a beta-lactam** | **Clindamycin 600 mg (children 20 mg/kg up to 600 mg)** | 60–120 minutes |

**Amoxicillin is first-line even in patients already receiving benzathine benzylpenicillin G for secondary
prophylaxis** [S1] — the monthly injection does not cover the dental procedure.

**But if the patient has recently had a treatment course of penicillin, amoxicillin or another beta-lactam,
clindamycin is first-line**, because **the treatment course may have reduced the amoxicillin susceptibility of
viridans streptococci** [S1].

## ⚠️ Cross-reference: the benzathine benzylpenicillin supply

**Secondary prophylaxis depends on a single injectable product.** The **syphilis guideline in this
compendium** records, from ASHM, **a shortage of both strengths of Bicillin L-A prefilled syringes with
supply dates into 2027 and s19A-approved overseas-registered substitutes**. That is the same active drug.

The ARF/RHD source retrieved here **does not address supply** [S1], so no claim about the shortage's effect on
prophylaxis is made — but the dependency is real, the schedule is monthly for years to decades, and this is
the population least able to absorb a substitution or an interruption. **Flagged for the attester rather than
asserted.**

## Who is at risk

**At high risk** [S1]: people living in an ARF-endemic setting; **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people
living in rural or remote areas**; **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people, and Māori and Pacific
Islander people, living in metropolitan households affected by crowding or low socioeconomic status**;
patients with a personal history of ARF or RHD **and aged under 40**.

**May be at high risk** [S1]: family or household recent history of ARF/RHD; **household overcrowding
(> 2 people per bedroom)** or low socioeconomic status; migrants or refugees from low- or middle-income
countries, and their children.

**Additional factors** [S1]: previous residence in a high-risk setting; frequent or recent travel to one;
**age 5–20 years**.

**An ARF-endemic setting means communities with ARF incidence higher than 30 per 100,000 per year in those
aged 5–14, and RHD all-age prevalence higher than 2 per 1000** [S1].

## Rheumatic heart disease

**Patients with moderate to severe RHD require cardiology services and regular echocardiographic
follow-up** [S1]. **Women with RHD who are pregnant or of childbearing age require pre-conception counselling
and specialist care** [S1].
