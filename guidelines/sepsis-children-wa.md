# Paediatric sepsis — the Western Australian guideline

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-22 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** children presenting to or admitted at Perth Children's Hospital with sepsis or septic shock.

> **Australian primary source.** Perth Children's Hospital (Child and Adolescent Health Service, WA).
> ***Sepsis recognition and management*** — Emergency Department Guideline [S1].

**The third and last of the state pathways** the RCH guideline says must be followed. **All four Australian
paediatric sepsis documents are now in this compendium.**

## ⚠️ WA does not list ethnicity. It lists access.

Three documents in this set name **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander** (and in Queensland, **Pacific
Islander or Māori**) origin as a sepsis risk factor, **none with a stated basis, magnitude or cultural safety
guidance**. That is Finding 4 of `docs/unresolved-audit.md`.

**Western Australia's high-risk list does not include an ethnicity at all** [S1]:

> **Infants less than 3 months of age** · **Immunosuppression due to chemotherapy, long-term steroids, other
> immunosuppressants, asplenia and other chronic medical conditions** · **Unimmunised / incompletely
> immunised children** · **Children with central venous access devices (CVAD), indwelling medical devices** ·
> **Complex / chronic medical conditions** · **Recent surgery, burn or wound** ·
> ⚠️ **Children living in rural and / or remote locations or living in socioeconomically deprived settings or
> with delayed access to healthcare** ·
> ⚠️ **Culturally and linguistically diverse children** ·
> **Children with multiple presentations to healthcare providers (including GP) with the same infective
> illness** · **Family and/or clinician concern**

**The same populations are reached through remoteness, deprivation, delayed access and language** — the
mechanisms — **rather than through ethnicity as a category.** WA is the jurisdiction with the largest remote
population and the longest retrieval distances, so this is not a softening: it is a different and arguably
more actionable construction of the same risk, because **each item names something a service can change.**

**Four documents, two framings.** Recorded here and in the audit; **the compendium does not adjudicate
between them**, but a reader deciding house policy should see both.

WA also carries the practice through to the family: **consider cultural needs, and use an interpreter for
families with limited English proficiency** [S1].

## ⚠️ The number that should be on the wall

> **For every hour a child remains in septic shock the mortality risk doubles** [S1].

**No other document in this compendium states it that way.** Queensland gives mortality (**>80% untreated,
15–20% treated**); RCH says the first hour is crucial. **WA gives the slope.**

## Definitions and recognition

**Sepsis is a dysregulated host response to infection leading to life threatening end organ dysfunction.
Septic shock is sepsis with evidence of cardiovascular dysfunction** [S1].

> ⚠️ **Hypotension is generally a late sign and is not required to diagnose septic shock in children. However,
> the presence of hypotension is confirmatory of shock** [S1].

**Consider sepsis** with **suspected or proven infection AND/OR fever/hypothermia (temperature ≥38 °C or
<36 °C) AND one or more signs of impaired tissue perfusion** [S1]:

> **Tachycardia disproportionate to fever, anxiety, medications** · **Bradycardia** · **Cold shock: CRT
> ≥3 seconds, cool peripheries, cool or mottled skin, reduced peripheral pulses, narrow pulse pressure** ·
> **Warm shock: CRT <1 second, bounding pulses, wide pulse pressure** · **Altered level of consciousness /
> drowsiness / irritability** · **Hypotension (a late sign)** · **New onset end organ dysfunction** ·
> **Evolving petechial or purpuric rash** · **Unexplained pain**

**"CRT <1 second" is the sharpest definition of warm shock in the set** — RCH and Queensland describe brisk or
flash refill; WA puts a number on it.

### Sepsis mimics

> **Children presenting with a fever may appear unwell with abnormal vital signs, however most children with a
> fever do not have sepsis.** **A period of observation may help differentiate children with fever due to
> self-limited viral infection (abnormal vital signs that normalise over time) and those that require further
> work-up… (abnormal vital signs that persist or worsen over time, clinical deterioration or persistently
> unwell appearance)** [S1].

**Time as a diagnostic test** — the only document here to name observation itself as the discriminator.

> **Several serious neonatal conditions can mimic sepsis, including… congenital cardiac lesions, bowel
> obstruction and other abdominal emergencies, metabolic disorders, seizures, and non-accidental injury.**
> ⚠️ **Always remember to check femoral pulses are palpable in an unwell neonate** [S1].

## The six key points

> 1. **Early recognition and initiation of treatment**
> 2. ⚠️ **Rapid vascular access, within 5 minutes of recognition of septic shock**
> 3. **Empiric antibiotic therapy as soon as possible and within 60 minutes of septic shock recognition** —
>    **if unable to gain vascular access rapidly (e.g. within 15 minutes), give initial dose of antibiotics
>    intramuscularly**
> 4. **Rapid, judicious, fluid resuscitation** — **neonates <4 weeks: 10 mL/kg; child ≥4 weeks:
>    10–20 mL/kg**
> 5. **Early initiation of inotropes / vasoactive support, via peripheral access for fluid refractory shock
>    (≥40 mL/kg fluid without shock reversal)**
> 6. **Source control (if possible)** [S1]

**Access**: **if unable to gain intravenous access within 5 minutes or after two attempts, insert intraosseous
access** — and **it may be appropriate to opt for intraosseous access initially in critically unwell
patients** [S1]. **Consider an umbilical venous catheter in neonates <7 days of age** [S1].

## ⚠️ Antibiotics — the only one of the four with a full empiric table

**Within 60 minutes of recognition of septic shock, ideally within 15 minutes** [S1] — **the tightest target
of the four documents.**

> **Cefepime, cefotaxime, ceftriaxone and gentamicin can be given as an IV push over 5 minutes or be given
> intramuscularly, and administration of these antibiotics should be prioritised over others which have a
> longer infusion time and cannot be given intramuscularly. Vancomycin should be infused over 60 minutes OR
> at a rate of 10 mg/minute whichever is longer** [S1].

**Children ≥4 weeks postnatal age** [S1]:

| Scenario | Regimen |
|---|---|
| **Community acquired sepsis or septic shock** | **IV/IO ceftriaxone 50 mg/kg/dose (max 2 g) 12 hourly.** ⚠️ **If shocked, add IV/IO gentamicin 7 mg/kg (max 560 mg) once daily AND IV/IO vancomycin 15 mg/kg/dose (max 750 mg) 6 hourly** |
| **Fever >38 °C without a source and suspicion of bacteraemia** *(no haemodynamic instability)* | **IV/IO ceftriaxone 50 mg/kg/dose (max 2 g) 24 hourly** |
| **Healthcare-associated sepsis** *(incl. community acquired with a CVAD)* | **IV/IO cefepime 50 mg/kg/dose (max 2 g) 8 hourly AND vancomycin 15 mg/kg/dose (max 750 mg) 6 hourly.** **If shocked, add gentamicin 7 mg/kg (max 560 mg) once daily** |

**Neonates (birth to <4 weeks)** [S1] — **doses deferred to neonatal guidelines**: **early-onset (<3 days):
gentamicin AND benzylpenicillin**; **late-onset community, not severely unwell: gentamicin AND
benzylpenicillin, and consider HSV testing and IV aciclovir**; **neonatal septic shock, severely unwell, or
confirmed/suspected meningitis: cefotaxime AND benzylpenicillin, and consider aciclovir**; **late-onset
healthcare associated: gentamicin AND vancomycin**.

**"Severely unwell with sepsis"** is defined [S1] as **end-organ dysfunction or elevated lactate · apnoeas or
airway compromise requiring airway management · respiratory failure or need for respiratory support · shock or
need for circulatory support · reduced conscious state · coagulopathy · need for intensive care input or
admission** — **or senior clinician concern that the child is "severely unwell"**.

> **Do not delay antibiotic administration in severely unwell children if microbiological sample collection is
> delayed or unsuccessful** [S1].

## ⚠️ Fluid as a drug

> **Fluid resuscitation should be titrated carefully. Both inadequate and excessive fluid resuscitation may be
> harmful.**
> ⚠️ **Fluids should be considered in the same way as any other intravenously administered medication, with
> the potential benefits and harms for the individual patient considered prior to administration** [S1].

**Sodium chloride 0.9% is the default**; **balanced fluids may be preferred in already acidotic or
hyperchloraemic patients due to the potential for sodium chloride 0.9% to cause a hyperchloraemic
acidosis** [S1].

- **10–20 mL/kg boluses rapidly (10 mL/kg for neonates) as a push or via pressure bag** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Discussion with critical care is mandatory if needing ≥40 mL/kg fluid resuscitation** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Fluid volumes of up to and over 60 mL/kg may be needed in the first 60 minutes in some instances of
  septic shock** [S1]

**The ceiling and the permission sit in the same section.** 40 mL/kg triggers a mandatory conversation; >60
mL/kg is explicitly foreseen.

## ⚠️ The adrenaline infusion question, resolved

> **Using adrenaline 1:1000 (1 mg/mL) ampoules, add 6 mg of adrenaline to a 1 L bag of sodium chloride 0.9%.
> 1 mL/kg/hr of this infusion = 0.1 micrograms/kg/minute. Birth to 18 years: usually start at
> 0.1 microgram/kg/minute i.e. 1 mL/kg/hour** [S1].

**This is identical to the RCH sepsis flowchart.** Earlier in this compendium I flagged a **sixfold**
difference between RCH sepsis (**6 mg in 1 L**) and RCH anaphylaxis (**1 mg in 1 L**) as a cross-guideline
hazard. **WA settles what kind of difference it is**: two of three sepsis documents independently use 6 mg/L,
so **6 mg/L is the paediatric sepsis concentration and 1 mg/L is the anaphylaxis one.** It is a considered
distinction between conditions, **not an inconsistency** — but it remains a hazard for a clinician moving
between the two protocols, and **neither RCH document mentions the other.**

**Noradrenaline** is prepared the same way — **6 mg in 1 L**, **1 mL/kg/hr = 0.1 microgram/kg/min**, starting
at **0.1 microgram/kg/min** [S1]. **Adrenaline or noradrenaline are appropriate first line options for most
patients**, and **it may be appropriate to exceed 0.3 micrograms/kg/minute on the advice of the PCC/NICU
Consultant** [S1]. **The site of administration should be monitored regularly for signs of extravasation.
Central access should be obtained as soon as practical** [S1].

## Investigations

> ⚠️ **Normal blood test results (e.g. a normal CRP, white cell count or lactate) do not exclude
> sepsis** [S1].

**Blood cultures**: ⚠️ **even if antibiotics already given — preferably two BACTEC Peds Plus/F bottles from
two different sites** [S1]. **A fourth rule on blood cultures across four documents.**

**Also**: **procalcitonin** — the only one of the four to include it in the routine set — and an **additional
EDTA tube for PCR testing e.g. Meningococcal, Pneumococcal, Group A Streptococcal PCR** [S1].

**Hypoglycaemia**: **blood glucose <3 mmol/L → 2 mL/kg glucose 10%**, then **repeat blood glucose after
treatment** [S1]. **Identical to NSW. RCH uses <3.3 mmol/L.**

**Lactate**: **a normal lactate does not exclude sepsis**; **>4 mmol/L… requires urgent (within 5 minutes)
senior clinician review** [S1].

**Lumbar puncture** [S1]:

> **A cerebrospinal fluid sample is recommended for all children with suspected meningitis including all
> neonates (<4 weeks postnatal age) with sepsis / suspected sepsis. Lumbar puncture should not [be] performed
> on an unstable, shocked or coagulopathic child or if there is evidence of raised intracranial pressure or
> any other contraindication.**

⚠️ **A fourth LP position — and the only one that makes CSF a recommendation for a whole age group.**

## Intubation, steroids, follow-up

> **Intubation in sepsis / septic shock is a high risk procedure** [S1]

**Prepare for potential cardiorespiratory arrest with resuscitation doses of adrenaline and fluid boluses
drawn up and ready**; **if appropriate commence inotropes prior to RSI**; **all induction agents have the
potential to worsen / precipitate shock in unstable septic patients**; **choose medications with a relatively
stable cardiovascular profile**; **reduced doses of induction agents may be sufficient** [S1].

**Compared with Queensland**, WA states the principle where Queensland names the drugs — *avoid midazolam,
propofol, metaraminol; halve the dose; ketamine and/or fentanyl with rocuronium*. **Read together they are
complete; separately, each is half the answer.**

**Steroids**: **IV hydrocortisone should be considered for those with catecholamine resistant shock**;
**children on long-term steroid therapy or with known adrenal insufficiency should receive stress dose
steroids** [S1].

**Follow-up**: **document time for next medical review (within 4 hours of PCH Sepsis Pathway commencement)**;
**antibiotic prophylaxis for household contacts (Meningococcal and Group A Streptococcal sepsis)**; and ⚠️
**for patients with a ≥7-day hospital admission due to sepsis, refer to the PCH Post-Sepsis Follow-Up
Program** [S1] — **the only post-sepsis service named in this compendium.**
