# Urinary incontinence

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-22 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation

*nocturnal enuresis* guideline.

## ⚠️ Before reaching for a drug, check what you already prescribed

> **Consider drugs as a cause of incontinence, eg selective alpha-blockers (eg prazosin), diuretics,
> anticholinesterases (eg donepezil), sedatives. Opioids and drugs with anticholinergic effects may cause
> urinary retention and overflow incontinence.** ⚠️ **Drug-induced incontinence is particularly common in
> the elderly** [A1].

⚠️ **Two of those are drugs this compendium recommends elsewhere.** **Selective alpha-blockers** are
first-line for BPH — and **may precipitate or worsen incontinence in women** [A1]. **Donepezil** is an
anticholinesterase, and ⚠️ **the anticholinergics used to treat incontinence antagonise the therapeutic
effects of anticholinesterases** [A1].

⚠️ **In a patient with dementia on donepezil who develops urge incontinence, the standard treatment works
against the standard treatment for the other condition.** **Anticholinergics are best avoided in
dementia — they may worsen symptoms** [A1].

## Three types

| Type | Definition [A1] | First non-drug measure [A1] |
|---|---|---|
| **Urge** | **involuntary loss of urine accompanied or preceded by a sudden urge to void (bladder overactivity)** | ⚠️ **bladder training** |
| **Stress** | **involuntary leakage on effort, exertion, sneezing or coughing** | ⚠️ **pelvic floor exercises** |
| **Overflow** | **urinary retention and bladder distension caused by outlet obstruction (eg prostatic enlargement) or detrusor underactivity** | see *overflow* below |

**They may occur separately or in combination** [A1].

## Assessment

> **Ask the patient to keep a bladder diary for three 24-hour periods; this may help identify the type of
> incontinence and possible aetiology. Tests (eg urinalysis, post-void residual volume) may be
> necessary** [A1].

**Manage contributing factors: UTI, constipation, excessive fluid intake, unstable diabetes, obesity;
reduce intake of caffeinated or alcoholic drinks** [A1].

⚠️ **Overactive bladder is a diagnosis of exclusion** [S1]. **A neuropathic bladder from diseases of the
central nervous system may result in neurogenic detrusor overactivity, a disease process that is high risk
and requires complex urological input.** ⚠️ **Urothelial carcinoma of the bladder can result in irritative
voiding, therefore symptoms similar to OAB may be the first signs of high-grade bladder cancer** [S1].

**S1's definitions** [S1]: **urinary urgency — inability to defer voiding; urge incontinence — urgency
causing non-voluntary urinary incontinence; urinary frequency — eight or more voids per 24 hours;
nocturia — awakening to void ≥1 instance per night.**

⚠️ **Many patients downplay their symptoms of OAB and do not raise the issue with health
practitioners** [S1] — and **untreated urinary incontinence may increase the risk of UTI and cause
significant surrounding inflammatory changes**, with **increased burden felt by the caregiver** [S1].

## ⚠️ Non-drug first

**Pelvic floor exercises and bladder training — particularly useful for stress and urge incontinence
respectively** [A1]. **Weight reduction, a decrease in exposure to bladder stimulants, fluid optimisation
and pelvic floor exercises** are **first-line** [S1].

**Protective pads and scheduled voiding may be helpful, especially in people with cognitive impairment or
limited mobility** [A1].

⚠️ **Drug treatment is for incontinence RESISTANT to non-drug measures** [A1].

## Urge incontinence — the drugs

### ⚠️ How much do they do? Two sources, two answers

| Source | Effect statement |
|---|---|
| ⚠️ **A1 (AMH 2026)** | **the benefit varies between individuals; ON AVERAGE THERE IS ONE FEWER EPISODE OF INCONTINENCE PER 48 HOURS compared with placebo** |
| ⚠️ **S1 (AJGP 2020)** | **non-selective agents are effective, with a SUCCESS RATE OF >65%** |

⚠️ **Both are given, because they answer different questions and averaging them would answer neither.**
A1 states an **absolute effect**; S1 a **responder rate**. ⚠️ **And S1 supplies the number that reconciles
them in practice: fewer than 35% of patients are adherent to anticholinergic medications at 12 months.**

### Doses and cost [S1]

| Class | Agent | Route, dose | PBS | Cautions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Anticholinergic, non-selective** | **Oxybutynin** | **oral, 5 mg three times daily** | ⚠️ **yes** | **dry mouth, constipation, blurred vision, drowsiness, delirium** |
| | **Oxybutynin** | **topical, 3.9 mg/24 hours** | ⚠️ **yes** | **skin reaction**, plus the above |
| **Anticholinergic, M3 selective** | **Solifenacin** | **oral, 5–10 mg daily** | ⚠️ **no — $50–65 per month** | **severe hepatic impairment** |
| **Beta-3 agonist** | **Mirabegron** | **oral, 25–50 mg daily** | ⚠️ **no — $50–65 per month** | **hypertension, long QT syndrome** |

### ⚠️ Where the sources disagree: is the expensive one better tolerated?

| Source | Says |
|---|---|
| ⚠️ **A1** | ⚠️ **EVIDENCE IS LIMITED to support that adverse effects are less likely with transdermal oxybutynin or selective agents (eg solifenacin, darifenacin)**, and **there is no clear evidence that any anticholinergic is the most effective** |
| ⚠️ **S1** | **more-selective agents have an IMPROVED TOLERABILITY PROFILE at an increased expense**… **fewer systemic side effects and remarkable efficacy at the level of the bladder** |

⚠️ **This is a $50–65 per month question and the sources do not agree on the answer.** A1 also states
directly: ⚠️ **no evidence of superior efficacy with newer agents compared to oxybutynin** [A1].
⚠️ **This guideline states both positions and does not choose.**

**S1's practical middle path:** ⚠️ **if there is good therapeutic response to oral oxybutynin but it is
poorly tolerated because of systemic side effects, a common solution is topical oxybutynin patches, which
are better tolerated because of the lack of hepatic metabolites** — though **they can cause a bothersome
local skin reaction** [S1]. **Oxybutynin is the only one available as a patch** [A1], **and the patch is
PBS listed** [S1].

### Safety

⚠️ **Avoid in urinary retention or significant bladder outlet obstruction. Measure post-void residual volume
in men BEFORE starting treatment; monitor for urinary retention especially during initial treatment when
risk appears highest** [A1]. **Anticholinergics may increase voiding dysfunction, causing hesitancy and
retention in susceptible people** [A1].

⚠️ **Contraindicated in glaucoma** [S1] — see the **glaucoma** guideline, where **drugs causing pupil
dilation may precipitate an acute angle-closure crisis**.

⚠️ **Elderly people may be more sensitive to urinary retention, blurred vision, dry mouth, constipation and
confusion. Adverse effects are usually dose related; start with low dosage and increase cautiously to the
lowest effective dose** [A1]. **Use cautiously in older patients because of effects on cognitive
function** [S1].

⚠️ **Avoid combination with other drugs having anticholinergic effects — the additive effect increases both
therapeutic and adverse effects** [A1].

⚠️ **Anticholinergics may reduce or prevent sweating, which may affect the body's ability to cool down,
especially in hot weather** [A1].

**Solifenacin may increase the QT interval, especially at high doses** [A1].

⚠️ **Propantheline is no longer recommended for treatment of urinary incontinence** [A1] — though it
remains listed in AMH's genitourinary anticholinergic class.

### Review at 4 weeks

> **After 4 weeks, monitor for adverse effects (including changes in cognitive function) and assess for
> improvement in symptoms. STOP IF THERE IS NO OVERALL BENEFIT; an alternative anticholinergic could be
> tried** [A1].

### Mirabegron

**Similar efficacy to anticholinergics; an option in people with urge incontinence who cannot tolerate
anticholinergic adverse effects or when anticholinergics are not effective or contraindicated** [A1].

**Relaxes the detrusor muscle, allowing higher bladder volumes before the need for urination. Significant
improvements are typically seen at week four and maintained at 12 months** [S1]. ⚠️ **Use cautiously in
difficult-to-control hypertension and prolonged QT interval** [S1].

⚠️ **S1 adds a combination A1 does not mention: studies have shown it is both safe and efficacious to
prescribe an antimuscarinic agent and β3 adrenoceptor agonist synergistically for patients refractory to
monotherapy** [S1].

### Vaginal estrogen

⚠️ **Vaginal estrogen may be effective for incontinence in postmenopausal women with urogenital symptoms;
SYSTEMIC estrogens may WORSEN incontinence** [A1]. **The route decides the direction of effect.**

## Beyond drugs

### Intravesical botulinum toxin A

| Source | On it |
|---|---|
| **S1** | **typically effective for 6–12 months and can be repeated**; ⚠️ **half of patients reported improvement**; **needs a general anaesthetic**, and ⚠️ **5% risk of iatrogenic urinary retention requiring temporary catheterisation** |
| **A1** | **consider if other treatments are not tolerated or ineffective.** ⚠️ **Only use in people able to self-catheterise (if needed)**; ⚠️ **do NOT use in men with bladder outlet obstruction**; **there were few men in clinical trials and about 60% reported no change or worsening of symptoms** |

⚠️ **Read the two response figures together: about half improve overall, and about 60% of the men in trials
did not.**

### Neuromodulation [S1]

**Sacral nerve stimulation** — **electrode in the S3–S4 sacral foramen**; **90% of patients showed >50%
improvement** by voiding diary, but ⚠️ **one-third may require surgical revision within the first year**,
and **the battery will require replacing**. ⚠️ **Implants are expensive and public hospital accessibility is
limited.**

**Peripheral tibial nerve stimulation** — **electrode 5 cm above the medial malleolus**, **30 minutes,
weekly for up to 12 sessions**, with **response typically observed within six treatments**.
⚠️ **Contraindicated with pacemakers/defibrillators, in pregnancy, or with prior nerve damage affecting the
tibial nerve or pelvic floor.**

## Overflow incontinence and special cases

**Selective alpha-blockers block receptors in the bladder neck and urethra, which may help reduce outflow
obstruction and overflow incontinence in males** — ⚠️ **but may precipitate or worsen incontinence in
women** [A1].

⚠️ **Intermittent catheterisation is preferred in most cases of overflow incontinence occurring
postoperatively, postpartum or after neurological damage, when improvement with time can be
expected** [A1]. ⚠️ **Indwelling catheters should only be used as a last resort** [A1].

**Neurological disease:** ⚠️ **complex and best managed by a specialist.** **Mainstays are intermittent
self-catheterisation, behavioural modification, and anticholinergics**; **botulinum toxins, surgery and
urinary appliances may be useful** [A1].

**Specialists sometimes use anticholinergics with selective alpha-blockers in men with coexisting urge
incontinence and bladder outlet obstruction** [A1] — **the same combination the BPH guideline records from
the other direction.**
