# Donovanosis

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-21 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults, primary care.

> **Australian primary source.** ASHM *Australian STI Management Guidelines for use in primary care* —
> *Donovanosis* [S1], modified 30 June 2026.

## The ulcer may be a cancer

This is the only infection in twenty-three guidelines where the recurring clinical question is oncological
rather than microbiological. The source raises it four separate times:

> **In non-healing genital lesions, carcinoma needs to be considered** [S1].

> **Neoplastic transformation is possible** [S1].

> **Biopsy if any concern about malignant change** [S1].

> **Recurrence of lesions may represent development of skin cancer in previous lesions and requires
> biopsy** [S1].

A lesion that comes back after treatment is not assumed to be relapse. It is biopsied.

## Two laboratories in the country

| Site/specimen | Test | Note [S1] |
|---|---|---|
| **Dry swab or punch biopsy of lesions** | **NAAT** | **Highly sensitive and specific, but only available at Pathwest laboratories in WA and the Molecular Diagnostics Unit at Royal Brisbane and Women's Hospital. Discuss with your laboratory before sending the specimen** |
| **Punch biopsy of lesion** | **Histology** | **Low-to-moderate sensitivity but highly specific.** Requires an experienced histopathologist, as **classic Donovan bodies may be sparse**. Biopsy if any concern about malignant change |

The source names the two laboratories because there is no general pathway. Ordering the test means arranging
it first — a harder version of the LGV problem, where the referral at least existed by default.

**Seek specialist advice before treating this rare condition** [S1].

## Treatment

| Situation | Recommended [S1] | Alternative [S1] |
|---|---|---|
| **Anogenital lesions** | **Azithromycin 500 mg PO daily for 7 days**<br>**OR**<br>**Azithromycin 1 g PO once weekly for at least 4 weeks, until complete resolution of lesions** | **Doxycycline 100 mg PO BD for a minimum of 4 weeks, until complete resolution of lesions** |

**Azithromycin is highly effective and well tolerated** [S1]. **In pregnancy, azithromycin is the
recommended treatment** [S1].

### A common practice the source declines to endorse

> **Many guidelines recommend treating with azithromycin until lesions have completely healed, but there is
> no evidence that longer treatment is beneficial. Non-azithromycin regimens should be continued until
> complete resolution of lesions** [S1].

The distinction is easy to miss and worth holding: **azithromycin does not need extending past its course
for the sake of it; doxycycline does run to resolution.** The source names the widespread practice,
states that the evidence does not support it, and then keeps the "until resolution" rule only where it
applies.

### Adherence is treated as a clinical risk

> **Adherence to treatment is essential to ensure cure — consider directly-observed therapy** [S1].

> **Patients may require hospital admission if adherence to treatment is poor or disseminated disease is
> present** [S1].

The only mention of directly-observed therapy in the set, and the only place where **poor adherence alone**
is given as a reason to admit. Given a 4-week-plus course for a condition that scars and can transform,
the source treats a missed course as a clinical event rather than a compliance failure.

## Immediate management

- **No sexual contact for 7 days after treatment is commenced, or until the course is completed and symptoms
  resolved — whichever is later** [S1].
- **No sex with partners from the last 6 months** until those partners have been reviewed and treated if
  necessary [S1].
- **Notify the state or territory health department** [S1] — and this is the sole auditable outcome:
  **100% of diagnoses are notified to the state or territory health departments** [S1].

## Transmission is not only sexual

**Primarily sexually transmissible, but may be transmitted vertically and by casual contact** [S1].

**Vertical transmission to a neonate can occur during vaginal delivery** [S1], and:

> **Neonates born to a mother with untreated donovanosis at time of delivery should be followed closely for
> the development of lesions** [S1].

## Presentation and complications

**Relatively painless anogenital ulceration** [S1] — the painlessness is what delays presentation.
Lesions may be [S1]:

- **ulcerative** — shallow ulcers which **bleed on contact**
- **proliferative** — raised lesions with a **beefy appearance**
- **ulcero-proliferative** — a combination

Lesions may involve the **genitals, perineum and perianal area**, and **secondary anaerobic bacterial
infection may produce an offensive odour** in association with the primary lesions [S1].

**Complications** [S1]:

- **Extra-genital disease is uncommon** but may occur by **auto-inoculation**, **contiguous spread**
  (uterus, fallopian tubes) or **haematogenous spread** (long bones, psoas muscle).
- **Chronic untreated ulcers may lead to lymphatic destruction with subsequent pseudo-elephantiasis of the
  genitalia.**
- **Neoplastic transformation is possible.**
- **Increased HIV transmission risk.**

**Extra-genital disease should be considered in patients with current genital infection, and in patients
with a past history of donovanosis who present with unusual symptoms** [S1].

**Complicated or disseminated infection may require prolonged treatment** [S1].

## Where it is found

- **A rare cause of genital ulceration, but should be considered in patients returning from areas where the
  disease may be endemic — e.g. PNG, Southern Africa, India and parts of South America** [S1].
- **Incident cases were found as recently as the mid-2000s in northern and tropical Australia among
  Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people, but it is now very rare in these areas** [S1].

That second sentence records a near-elimination within Australia. It is the reason the test now sits in two
laboratories, and the reason the source says to seek specialist advice: the clinicians who saw this
routinely are no longer the clinicians most patients will meet.

## Contact tracing and follow-up

> **Contact tracing of sexual partners in the last 6 months is recommended, but yield is low** [S1].

An unusually candid line — the source recommends the action and simultaneously tells you what to expect
from it.

**Review in 1 week** to confirm adherence and symptom resolution, confirm or support contact tracing, and
educate about condoms, contraception, HIV PrEP/PEP, safe injecting, consent, cervical screening and
HAV/HBV/HPV vaccination [S1].

**Test of cure:** **relapse and re-infection can occur**; review **at completion of the treatment course and
at 3 months** [S1]. **Recurrence requires biopsy** [S1].

**Retesting is not required**, but offers the opportunity to retest past the window period for other STIs if
not done at first presentation [S1].
