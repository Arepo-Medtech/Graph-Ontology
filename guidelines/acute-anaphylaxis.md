# Acute anaphylaxis

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-22 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** all ages, all healthcare settings — primary care, community and hospital.

> **Australian primary source.** Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care (ACSQHC).
> ***Acute Anaphylaxis Clinical Care Standard*** [S1]. **Six quality statements**, referenced throughout to
> the **ASCIA *Acute Management of Anaphylaxis*** guidelines.

**This closes the allergy thread running through the antimicrobial stewardship guideline** in this compendium,
whose quality statement 3 sets out how a reaction must be documented. **This is the standard for the reaction
itself.**

## ⚠️ An entire quality statement about not standing up

> **Quality statement 3 — Correct patient positioning.** A patient experiencing anaphylaxis **is laid flat, or
> allowed to sit with legs extended if breathing is difficult. An infant is held or laid horizontally. The
> patient is not allowed to stand or walk during, or immediately after the event until they are assessed as
> safe to do so, even if they appear to have recovered** [S1].

> **Fatality can occur within minutes if a patient stands or sits up suddenly while they have inadequate
> perfusion** [S1].

Forty-eight guidelines in, this is the only national standard that devotes a numbered quality statement to
**body position** — because position is the intervention.

- **Ensure the patient is in a supine position; do not allow them to stand or walk. Monitor blood pressure and
  elevate their legs if blood pressure is low** [S1].
- **A semi-reclined position sufficient to relieve respiratory distress is allowed while the patient is
  monitored carefully for any circulatory collapse** [S1].
- **Patients who are pregnant or vomiting should be placed on their side in the left lateral (recovery)
  position** [S1].
- > **Infants should be held horizontally (across the body). They must not be held upright or over a
  > shoulder** [S1].
- **Ensure the patient understands that they are not to stand up suddenly or walk until after they have been
  treated and assessed, and why this is important. Communicate this to other staff caring for the
  patient** [S1].

**"Even if they appear to have recovered."** The apparent recovery is the moment of danger.

## ⚠️ Adrenaline first, and there is nothing that stops you

> **A patient with anaphylaxis, or suspected anaphylaxis, is administered adrenaline intramuscularly without
> delay, before any other treatment including asthma medicines. Corticosteroids and antihistamines are not
> first-line treatments for anaphylaxis** *(Quality statement 2)* [S1].

> **Delayed administration of adrenaline is a risk factor for fatal anaphylaxis. If anaphylaxis is suspected in
> the presence of an allergy or anaphylaxis history, or following exposure to a potential allergen, it is safer
> to administer adrenaline early than to wait for progression, which may be hard to reverse. **There are no
> absolute contraindications to adrenaline administration in anaphylaxis**** [S1].

**"Before any other treatment including asthma medicines"** is the clause that matters in the moment — the
patient with wheeze reaches for a puffer, and the clinician must not.

### Route

**Administer adrenaline via intramuscular injection into the mid-anterolateral thigh using a needle of
appropriate length** [S1].

> **Subcutaneous or inhaled routes for adrenaline are not recommended as they are less effective** [S1].

> **In most situations, IM adrenaline is preferred and is safer than the intravenous (IV) route. Adverse events
> have been reported in adult patients who received overdoses of IV adrenaline, but these are rare with IM
> adrenaline** [S1].

**An IV adrenaline infusion should only be administered when clinically appropriate, and by clinicians
trained** to do so [S1].

> **Pregnant women experiencing anaphylaxis require the same dose of IM adrenaline as other patients** [S1].

### Doses [S1, Table 3]

| Age (years) | Weight (kg) | Volume of adrenaline **1:1,000** ampoules | Adrenaline injector (instead of ampoules) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **~ < 1** | **< 7.5** | **0.1 mL** | **Not available** |
| **~ 1–2** | **10** | **0.1 mL** | 7.5–20 kg (~< 5 years) — **150 microgram device** |
| **~ 2–3** | **15** | **0.15 mL** | 7.5–20 kg (~< 5 years) — **150 microgram device** |
| **~ 4–6** | **20** | **0.2 mL** | 7.5–20 kg (~< 5 years) — **150 microgram device** |
| **~ 7–10** | **30** | **0.3 mL** | > 20 kg (~> 5 years) — **300 microgram device** |
| **~ 10–12** | **40** | **0.4 mL** | > 20 kg (~> 5 years) — **300 microgram device** |

**Note the gap:** **no adrenaline injector is available for infants under 7.5 kg** — the ampoule is the only
option in that group.

## Recognition

**Quality statement 1** [S1]: rapid assessment for anaphylaxis in **acute-onset clinical deterioration with
signs or symptoms of an allergic response**, especially with an allergic trigger or history of allergy.

> **Assess patients immediately for possible anaphylaxis if they present with rapid development of airway,
> breathing and/or circulation problems, with or without skin or mucosal changes. The presence of an allergic
> trigger or a history of allergy should heighten suspicion even if the patient is not in severe
> distress** [S1].

- **Symptoms typically occur in two or more body systems, but this can be variable** [S1].
- **The most common triggers are food, insect venom and medicines** [S1].
- > **After exposure to a trigger, the time until onset of signs and symptoms of anaphylaxis may range from
  > minutes to several hours** [S1].
- **Obtain a history noting recent exposure to substances known to cause an allergic reaction, any known
  allergies including previous reactions and treatment, and any history of anaphylaxis** [S1].
- > **Document the time of symptom onset in the patient's healthcare record** [S1].
- **Consider patient risk factors that potentially contribute to fatal anaphylaxis — for example, older age
  and cardiovascular and respiratory diseases** [S1].

**"With or without skin or mucosal changes"** and **"even if the patient is not in severe distress"** are the
two clauses that prevent the diagnosis being missed. **No rash does not mean no anaphylaxis.**

## ⚠️ The patient keeps their own injector

**Quality statement 4** [S1]:

> A patient who has an adrenaline injector **has access to it for self-administration during all healthcare
> encounters. This includes patients keeping their adrenaline injector safely at their bedside during a
> hospital admission.**

**A national standard instructing hospitals to let patients keep and self-administer their own medicine.** The
reasoning is speed:

> **For adrenaline to be given as soon as possible after the onset of symptoms of anaphylaxis, it is important
> for the patient (or their carer) to be able to immediately administer their own adrenaline injector,
> regardless of the setting. A readily accessible adrenaline injector may also be used by a clinician if
> necessary** [S1].

**As soon as possible following presentation or admission, assess their capacity to safely use it** [S1]:

- **checking their capacity, physical capability and willingness to use their personal adrenaline injector and
  their ability to recognise the symptoms of anaphylaxis**
- > **considering medicines administered during the healthcare visit that may impair the patient's usual
  > ability to recognise and treat anaphylaxis**
- **involving a parent, guardian or carer in the assessment when in the paediatric setting**
- **involving a family member or carer when a patient is cognitively impaired or lives with a disability**

**Indicator 4** audits **evidence of a locally approved policy to ensure patients maintain access to their
personal adrenaline injectors** [S1] — a policy, not a case-by-case favour.

## ⚠️ Four hours minimum, because of biphasic reactions

**Quality statement 5** [S1]: observation **for at least 4 hours after their last dose of adrenaline, or
overnight as appropriate** per the ASCIA guidelines, with timeframes **determined based on assessment and risk
appraisal after initial treatment**.

> **Prolonged and biphasic reactions may occur. Biphasic reactions are estimated to occur following 3–20% of
> anaphylactic reactions, and cannot be predicted** [S1].

**"Cannot be predicted"** is why the four hours is a floor rather than a judgement call.

> **When the initial injection of adrenaline for anaphylaxis is administered in general practice or another
> primary care location, where observation for four hours is not possible, arrange ambulance transfer to an
> appropriate facility for clinical observation** [S1].

**Giving the adrenaline in general practice creates an obligation to transfer** — the treatment is not the end
of the episode.

**Observe overnight if the patient** [S1]:

- **had a severe reaction (hypotension or hypoxia)**
- **required repeated doses of adrenaline**
- **has a history of severe asthma or protracted anaphylaxis**
- **has other concomitant illnesses, such as asthma, chest infection or arrhythmia**
- > **lives alone or is remote from medical care**
- **has known systemic mastocytosis**
- > **presented for health care late in the evening**
- > **cannot easily replace their adrenaline injector on discharge and have no other adrenaline injector**

**Three of the eight criteria are social or logistical**, not physiological — living alone, presenting late at
night, and being unable to replace the injector. The standard treats *what happens after they leave* as part
of the clinical risk.

## Discharge

**Quality statement 6** [S1]: before leaving, the patient is **advised about the suspected allergen, allergen
avoidance strategies and post-discharge care**. The discharge care plan is **tailored to the allergen** and
includes **details of the suspected allergen, the appropriate ASCIA Action Plan, and the need for prompt
follow-up with a general practitioner *and* clinical immunology/allergy specialist review**. **Where there is a
risk of re-exposure, the patient is prescribed a personal adrenaline injector and is trained in its use.**
**Details of the allergen, the anaphylactic reaction and discharge care arrangements are documented in the
patient's healthcare record.**

In most cases discharge will include [S1]: **an ASCIA Action Plan**; **advice about the allergen or suspected
allergen and how to avoid it**; **advice about follow-up visits with their GP and a clinical
immunology/allergy specialist**; **the Commission's Anaphylaxis Discharge Checklist and Discussion Guide**; and
**prescribing personal adrenaline injector(s) where there is** risk of re-exposure.

**Trained in its use** — the prescription alone does not satisfy the statement.

| **6a** | **Evidence of local arrangements that ensure patients treated for anaphylaxis receive tailored discharge planning prior to separation from hospital** |
| **6b** | **Proportion separated from hospital with a completed ASCIA Action Plan for Anaphylaxis or ASCIA Action Plan for Drug (Medication) Allergy** |
| **6c** | **Proportion who require adrenaline injectors who are supplied or prescribed an adrenaline injector prior to separation from hospital** |

**Statements 3 and 5 — positioning and observation time — carry no indicator**, despite positioning being the
one the standard says can kill within minutes.

**No benchmarks are set by the Commission**; definitions are at **METEOR** [S1].
