# Hepatitis C

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-21 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults, primary care.

> **Australian primary source.** ASHM *Australian STI Management Guidelines for use in primary care* —
> *Hepatitis C* [S1], modified 30 June 2026.

## It is curable, and that changes every rule on the page

> **Almost all patients can be successfully cured with direct-acting antiviral (DAA) therapy** [S1].

The only curable chronic viral infection in twenty-nine guidelines. Everything else here follows from it —
including who is eligible, which is the part that changed.

> **Current injecting drug use does *not* exclude a patient from treatment initiation** [S1].

The most direct removal of an eligibility barrier anywhere in the set, italicised in the source. It is
stated because the barrier was historically real, and it is stated in the same table as the clinical
exceptions rather than in a policy note.

> **All HCV PCR positive patients should be given the opportunity to be treated** [S1].

The two auditable outcomes measure exactly that pathway, and nothing else:

> **100% of hepatitis C antibody positive patients have a hepatitis C RNA performed** [S1].
> **100% of hepatitis C antibody positive / RNA positive patients are offered HCV treatment** [S1].

**Re-infection? Treat again** [S1]. **Over 65? No dose adjustment required** [S1]. **Not yet confident
prescribing?** — **treatment advice and initiation in collaboration with HCV treatment providers until you
are comfortable with the decision-making process and prescribing** [S1].

## ⚠️ Antibody positive is not infection, and cure does not clear the antibody

| Test | Meaning [S1] |
|---|---|
| **Hepatitis C antibody** | **Detected: infection with hepatitis C — past or current.** Not detected: no evidence of infection |
| **HCV RNA (NAAT) / HCV PCR** | **Detected: active infection.** Not detected: past/cleared infection, or never infected. **Use for test of cure or test of re-infection** |

> **Following successful treatment or natural clearance of hepatitis C virus, HCV RNA will be negative
> although hepatitis C antibody will remain positive** [S1].

> **Never repeat an HCV antibody test if initially positive** [S1].

> **Only use HCV PCR on patients with previous HCV infection** [S1], and **HCV PCR is required for patients
> with previously cleared or treated infection who require ongoing testing** [S1].

A cured patient stays antibody-positive for life. Re-testing the antibody answers a question that has
already been answered, and the only test that speaks to their current status is the PCR.

## Reflex testing — diagnosing without recalling the patient

> **Reflex testing is an HCV PCR test done if an HCV antibody test is positive. The opportunity to diagnose
> HCV status is done without having to recall the client. Two EDTA samples need to be collected with the
> initial serum sample. Request HCV PCR if HCV antibody (anti-HCV) confirmed positive — ensure with the
> local pathology service if this test is possible, as sample preparation will differ in metropolitan,
> regional and rural areas** [S1].

Two extra tubes at the first visit remove an entire recall step. For a population that may not return, that
is the difference between a diagnosis and a lost result — and the source is explicit that **availability
varies by location**, so it must be arranged with the local laboratory rather than assumed.

**A finger-prick point-of-care HCV RNA test gives a result in 60 minutes — useful for test-and-treat models
of care** [S1].

## ⚠️ Clearance does not confer immunity

> **Past cleared hepatitis C infection does not provide immunity against re-infection** [S1].
> **Retesting is required if there is a continued risk of re-exposure** [S1].

Set this beside the other two hepatitides in the same source and the three answers are all different:

| | Immunity after infection [S1 and the hepatitis A and B guidelines] |
|---|---|
| **Hepatitis A** | **Lifelong immunity** |
| **Hepatitis B** | **Lifelong immunity to re-infection** — but resolved infection may flare if immunocompromised |
| **Hepatitis C** | **No immunity. Re-infection possible; treat again** |

**If the patient has ongoing risk factors: annual HCV RNA test. If re-infected, offer re-treatment and harm
reduction strategies** [S1]. **Ensure your patient uses new equipment if they continue to inject
drugs** [S1].

## Management

| Situation | Recommended [S1] | Alternative [S1] |
|---|---|---|
| **Acute hepatitis** | **Test for HCV PCR 6 months after the possible infection date — the patient may have cleared the infection** | **Speak to a specialist if the patient is unwell** |
| **Chronic infection** | **All positive patients should be treated** (hepcguidelines.org.au) | — |
| **Complications** | **Referral to a specialist if complications are suspected, e.g. cirrhosis.** The Hepatitis C Consensus Statement states: **all people with cirrhosis should be referred to, and managed in consultation with, a specialist familiar with the management of this condition** | **Commence treatment and send the patient to a specialist, or speak to a specialist** |

The alternative in the complications row is the notable one: **start treatment and refer**, rather than
refer and wait.

**Specific DAA regimens are not on this page** — the source directs to the Australian Hepatitis C consensus
guidelines. They are recorded as `input_unavailable` below rather than written from memory.

## Assessment

| Test | Consideration [S1] |
|---|---|
| **Liver function test** | **Upper limits of normal ALT: male sex 30 IU/L; female sex 19 IU/L. AST 40/L for the APRI calculator** |
| **Full blood count** | **Platelet count is important for the APRI score** |
| **APRI (online calculator)** | **Above 1: increased likelihood of cirrhosis** |
| **AFP** | **Increasing levels may indicate hepatocellular carcinoma. Upper limit of normal ≤ 10. May be elevated in other conditions** |
| **HCV genotype** | **Not required for treatment, but recommended as part of clinical assessment where possible** |
| **HCV resistance testing** | **Done by a specialist to guide treatment if initial treatment has failed** |

**The sex-specific ALT ceilings are lower than many laboratory reference ranges** — 30 IU/L and 19 IU/L. A
result reported as "normal" by the lab may exceed the threshold used here.

**Check the MBS schedule for pre-treatment, on-treatment and post-treatment item numbers and testing amounts
per 12-month period** [S1] — the only place in the set where the *number of tests permitted per year* is
flagged as a planning constraint.

## Other immediate management

- **Vaccinate for hepatitis A and B if susceptible** [S1].
- **Screen for STIs and other blood borne viruses (HIV and syphilis)** [S1].
- **Notify the state/territory health department if required — only if HCV PCR detected** [S1]. Antibody
  positivity alone is not notifiable.

## Special situations

| Situation | Recommended [S1] |
|---|---|
| **Pregnancy** | Refer to antenatal care and blood borne viruses guidance |
| **Babies and children** | **All babies of HCV RNA positive women should be tested with an HCV PCR at 8 weeks of age and then 4–6 weeks later.** If positive, the baby needs specialist management. **Treatment with DAA for HCV PCR positive children from ≥ 12 years old** |
| **Over 65 years old** | **No dose adjustment required** |
| **Past failed treatment or current treatment failure** | **Refer to a specialist** |
| **Re-infection** | **Treat again** |
| **Co-infection with HIV or HBV** | **Refer to specialist services** |
| **Current injecting drug use** | **Does not exclude patient from treatment initiation** |

## Follow-up and test of cure

> **Test of cure — HCV PCR at 4 weeks after completion of treatment. If negative, counsel about possible
> risk behaviour and re-infection; if positive, retest at 12 weeks post treatment** [S1].

**SVR (sustained viral response) is defined on this page as test of cure 12 weeks after the completion of
treatment** [S1].

**Patients with chronic hepatitis C should be assessed for other causes of hepatitis — e.g. alcohol, fatty
liver — and counselled to reduce these factors if relevant** [S1].

## Who is at risk

**People with current or a history of injecting drug use; previous incarceration; born in HCV endemic
countries and regions; received blood products before 1990 or in developing countries; engage in condomless
anal sex with a partner with HCV infection; participate in group sex; and current HIV PrEP use** [S1].

**About 75% of people with hepatitis C infection will progress to chronic hepatitis** [S1]. **Acute hepatitis
is uncommon; asymptomatic infection is common** [S1]. **Chronic HCV is defined as an HCV viral load detected
in the absence of clinical features of acute hepatitis infection** [S1].

**Disease progression is usually slow** but may cause **cirrhosis, liver failure and hepatocellular
carcinoma**, and is affected by **age at infection, duration of infection, alcohol and other drug use,
co-infection with HIV or HBV, male gender, stage of fibrosis, higher ALT levels, and other comorbid
conditions such as diabetes** [S1].

## Contact tracing

- **Low risk for sexual exposure — contact tracing is not generally performed for sexual partners** [S1].
- **Contacts via parenteral exposure (shared needles, injecting equipment) should be tested if
  possible** [S1].
- **Children of mothers who are HCV PCR positive and not tested in the post-natal period should be
  tested** [S1].
