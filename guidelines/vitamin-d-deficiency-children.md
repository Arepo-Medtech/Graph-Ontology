# Vitamin D deficiency in children

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** risk factors, screening, colecalciferol treatment and prevention of low vitamin D and nutritional rickets in infants, children and adolescents. Related: `hypocalcaemia-children` (vitamin D deficiency is its commonest cause). The adult bone-health counterpart is `osteoporosis`, which does not address calcium and vitamin D.

## Background and definitions

- **Sunlight is the most important source of vitamin D at all ages.** In Australia, nutritional rickets is generally only seen in infants and children with dark skin. [S1]
- **Breastmilk contains almost no vitamin D**; infant formula is fortified. [S1]
- **Recommended intake without sun exposure:** 0–12 months 400 units daily; 1–18 years 400–600 units daily. [S1]
- **Status by 25-OH-D:** severe deficiency under 12.5 nmol/L · moderate 12.5–29 · mild 30–49 · **sufficient 50 or more (the target at all ages)** · elevated 250 or more. **Toxicity = over 250 nmol/L with hypercalcaemia and PTH suppression.** [S1]

## Recognise it

- **Risk factors:** little sun exposure (time indoors, covering clothes), dark skin, conditions affecting vitamin D metabolism (obesity, liver/renal failure, severe malabsorption, medications); **infants: exclusive breastfeeding plus** any of these, maternal deficiency or prematurity. [S1]
- **Low-calcium symptoms:** muscle cramps, tetany, stridor, seizures (rare beyond 6–12 months of age). [S1]
- **Signs of rickets:** long-bone deformity, widened wrists/ankles, delayed anterior fontanelle closure (normally closed by 2 years), frontal bossing, rachitic rosary, increased fracture risk; also delayed dentition (no teeth by 9 months, no molars by 14 months). [S1]

## Investigate

- ⚠️ **Consider admission for symptomatic hypocalcaemia (tetany, stridor, seizures) or severe rickets.** Specialist review for infants with symptoms/signs, clinical rickets, abnormal serum calcium, or deficiency not responding to high-dose supplements. [S1]
- **Breastfed infants under 12 months with other risk factors can usually start 400 units daily without tests**, provided they have no symptoms or signs. [S1]
- **Screen children/adolescents with one or more risk factors: 25-OH-D, calcium, phosphate, ALP**; add PTH if low calcium intake, symptoms or multiple risk factors. **Rickets:** add Mg, PTH, UEC, urine studies and a left wrist X-ray. [S1]

## Treat (oral D3 / colecalciferol; 1 microgram = 40 units)

- **Treat low vitamin D to restore levels to 50 nmol/L or more**, ensure adequate calcium intake, and educate on sun exposure. Evidence for high-dose treatment under 3 months is limited. [S1]
- **Preterm:** mild (30–49 nmol/L) 200 units/kg/day, max 400 units/day (same for maintenance); moderate–severe (under 30) 800 units/day, review after 1 month. [S1]
- **Term, under 3 months:** mild 400 units/day for 3 months (then 400 units daily); moderate–severe 1,000 units/day for 3 months. [S1]
- **3–12 months:** mild 400 units/day for 3 months (then 400 units daily); moderate–severe 1,000 units/day for 3 months **or** 50,000 units stat, review after 1 month and consider repeating. [S1]
- **1–18 years, mild (30–49):** 1,000–2,000 units/day for 3 months **or** 150,000 units stat. **Maintenance:** 400–600 units daily, **or** 3000–4000 units weekly, **or** 150,000 units at the start of autumn. [S1]
- **1–18 years, moderate–severe (under 30):** 1,000–2,000 units daily for 6 months, **or** 3,000–4,000 units daily for 3 months, **or** 150,000 units stat repeated at 6 weeks. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Nutritional rickets: specialist-guided; minimum vitamin D 2000 units daily for at least 3 months plus oral calcium 500 mg daily** (diet or supplement); phosphate may also be needed. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Check which product the family uses** — some preparations reach 1000 units per drop and **overdose is possible**; most adult tablets are 1000 units. [S1]

## Prevention and follow-up

- **At-risk breastfed infants: 400 units vitamin D daily for at least the first 12 months.** Fully formula-fed infants usually need no supplement. [S1]
- **Repeat bloods:** not usually needed in mild deficiency; otherwise at 1 month in infants, 3 months if older. [S1]
- **Self-management plan:** consider 400–600 units daily **or** 3000–4000 units weekly over the cooler months (May–August) to avoid blood tests and high-dose therapy. [S1]
- **Sun:** normal sunscreen use does not cause low vitamin D; children with dark skin can tolerate intermittent sun without sunscreen (hat and sunglasses still recommended). In winter in southern Australia, sun alone may not maintain levels. [S1]
