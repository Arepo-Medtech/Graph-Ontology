## ⚠️ The threshold is arbitrary and the risk is continuous

> **The risk of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality rises as BP increases FROM 115/75 mmHg** [H1].
>
> ⚠️ **"The distinction between normotension and hypertension is arbitrary"** — AMH's own framing.
> **Normotension is generally taken as a clinic BP of 120/80 mmHg and below, and hypertension as above
> 140/90 mmHg** [H1].

**The decision to treat should incorporate assessment of BP, overall cardiovascular risk AND end-organ
damage** [H1] — three inputs, not one number.

---

## ⚠️ Measure it properly before you treat it

| [H1] | |
|---|---|
| **Confirm with MULTIPLE readings on DIFFERENT occasions** | ensure correct cuff size; consider white coat effect |
| ⚠️ **Method changes the number** | **ambulatory and home BP are generally LOWER than clinic BP — and are a STRONGER PREDICTOR OF OUTCOMES** |
| ⚠️ **Automated office BP** | **substantially lower systolic — BY ABOUT 10–20 mmHg — than conventional clinic readings**; **used in SPRINT**; ⚠️ **currently not often practised in Australia** |

> ⚠️ **That 10–20 mmHg gap is the single most important number on this page**, because the strongest
> evidence for a lower target (SPRINT) was generated with the method Australia mostly does not use. It
> reappears under *Elderly* below.

## Before any drug [H1]

- **Look for SECONDARY CAUSES**, including **drugs — NSAIDs, corticosteroids** — and **end-organ damage**.
- **Identify and treat other modifiable risk factors** (obesity, dyslipidaemia); **encourage smoking
  cessation**.
- **Lifestyle change can avoid or delay drug treatment:** ⚠️ **regular aerobic exercise can reduce daytime
  systolic BP BY UP TO 3.2 mmHg**, and **10 kg of weight loss in an overweight person may reduce systolic
  BP by 6–10 mmHg** [H1].

---

## ⚠️ Treat the risk, not the number

> **In adults without known cardiovascular disease, consider drug treatment ACCORDING TO ESTIMATED RISK
> RATHER THAN TARGETING BP ALONE — although treat those with systolic BP >160 mmHg or diastolic >100 mmHg**
> regardless [H1].

**Risk assessment is UNNECESSARY in people clinically determined to be high risk:** **established
cardiovascular disease (MI, stroke, vascular disease), familial hypercholesterolaemia, or
moderate-to-severe chronic kidney disease** [H1].

**Assess risk in all adults without known CVD aged 45–79 — from 35 in people with diabetes, and ⚠️ from 30
in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people.** **Calculator: cvdcheck.org.au** [H1].

| 5-year risk [H1] | Action |
|---|---|
| ⚠️ **High — ≥10%**, or clinically determined high risk | **start antihypertensive AND lipid-lowering therapy** unless clinically inappropriate |
| **Intermediate — 5% to <10%** | **consider drug treatment** |
| **Low — <5%** | **drug treatment NOT routinely recommended** |

⚠️ **These thresholds match this compendium's `cardiovascular-disease-risk` guideline**, which carries them
from the 2023 Australian guideline. **Two independent sources, the same numbers.**

## ⚠️ BP targets — and a real disagreement

| Source | Target |
|---|---|
| **AMH** [H1] | **uncomplicated hypertension: BELOW 140/90 mmHg, or lower if tolerated** |
| **AJGP, August 2026** [S1] | **"aim for BP ≤130/85 mmHg in patients at normal risk"**, individualised on comorbidities |

> ⚠️ **RECORDED, NOT RESOLVED.** Two current Australian sources, one month apart, give different targets —
> **140/90 and 130/85** — for what reads as the same population. **S1 is a menopause cardiovascular
> article, not a hypertension guideline**, and its row cites a reference this compendium did not retrieve;
> **that is stated so the reader can weigh it**, not as a reason to discard it. **AMH's number is the one
> attributed to "Australian guidelines"** [H1]. ⚠️ **Neither was checked against the Heart Foundation
> source directly.**

**Both sources agree on the direction of travel:** **BP can be lowered as far as it is tolerable, and even
if targets are not reached, ANY reduction in BP reduces cardiovascular morbidity and mortality** [H1].
**S1 directs stepwise pharmacotherapy for primary prevention according to AusCVDRisk recommendations**
[S1] — the same risk-based framing as [H1].

### The lower target, and where it came from

**A target of systolic BP <120 mmHg can be considered in selected high-risk cardiovascular populations,
with close monitoring for hypotension and electrolyte abnormalities** [H1].

> **SPRINT: patients at high cardiovascular risk had fewer cardiovascular events and reduced mortality —
> BUT MORE TREATMENT-RELATED ADVERSE EVENTS — at a target systolic <120 mmHg versus <140 mmHg.**
> ⚠️ **Generalisability may be limited by its use of AUTOMATED OFFICE BP and its exclusions: age under 50,
> diabetes, heart failure, history of stroke** [H1].

## Drug choice [H1]

> **Generally start with a SINGLE drug at the LOWEST recommended dose — this will satisfactorily reduce BP
> in about 25–50% of people** [H1].

**For uncomplicated hypertension, unless contraindicated or another drug is specifically indicated, first
consider:**

| [H1] |
|---|
| **an ACE inhibitor (or sartan)** |
| **a DIHYDROPYRIDINE calcium channel blocker** |
| ⚠️ **if 65 or older — a low-dose THIAZIDE diuretic** |

**Choose an agent given ONCE DAILY.** **Allow 4–6 weeks to assess response** — shorter for marked
hypertension or high risk. ⚠️ **Not all patients respond to all drugs: if the first is not tolerated or has
no effect, CHANGE CLASS rather than persisting** [H1].

> **It is the REDUCTION IN BP that matters most for reducing cardiovascular events, rather than which
> antihypertensive is chosen** [H1].

### What each class brings [H1]

- **ACE inhibitors** — first-line, **especially in chronic kidney disease, diabetes with micro- or
  macroalbuminuria, heart failure, or LV dysfunction particularly after MI**. **Sartans** are the
  alternative, **especially for those intolerant of ACE inhibitors**.
- **Dihydropyridines** (amlodipine, felodipine, lercanidipine, nifedipine) — suitable first-line. ⚠️ **In
  meta-analyses they REDUCED STROKE but INCREASED RATES OF HEART FAILURE** versus other classes;
  cardiovascular events and mortality were similar.
- ⚠️ **Verapamil and diltiazem are CONTRAINDICATED where heart failure coexists**; dihydropyridines may be
  used cautiously.
- **Thiazides** — well tolerated, **first-line over 65**. ⚠️ **Because of their association with NEW-ONSET
  DIABETES they are NO LONGER first-line monotherapy in younger patients.**
- ⚠️ **Beta-blockers — LESS effect on stroke than other classes and an INCREASED RISK OF DIABETES.**
  Recommended **when there is a compelling indication such as angina**. AMH's beta-blocker page adds that
  they are **not usually recommended first line for uncomplicated essential hypertension**, and that **most
  comparative studies used ATENOLOL, which is associated with higher cardiovascular mortality and stroke
  than other antihypertensives** [H2].
- ⚠️ **Selective alpha-blockers are still used but there is NO RELIABLE RANDOMISED EVIDENCE about their
  effects on cardiovascular morbidity or mortality** [H1].

### Comorbidity steers the choice [H1]

| Favourable | Drugs |
|---|---|
| **Diabetes with proteinuria/microalbuminuria, CKD** | **ACE inhibitors (or sartans)** |
| **Heart failure with reduced EF** | **ACE inhibitors (or sartans); carvedilol, CR metoprolol, bisoprolol, nebivolol** |
| **Post MI** | **beta-blockers; ACE inhibitors (or sartans)** |
| **Angina** | **beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, ACE inhibitors** |
| **AF** | **ACE inhibitors or sartans** (verapamil, diltiazem, beta-blockers may help rate control) |

| ⚠️ Unfavourable | Drugs |
|---|---|
| **Asthma, COPD** | **beta-blockers** — cardioselective agents may be used cautiously in COPD or well-controlled asthma |
| **Bradycardia, 2nd/3rd-degree AV block** | **beta-blockers, diltiazem, verapamil** |
| **Heart failure with reduced EF** | **calcium channel blockers, especially verapamil and diltiazem** |
| ⚠️ **GOUT** | **thiazide diuretics** |

## ⚠️ Add, do not escalate

> **If the effect is inadequate, ADD A SECOND ANTIHYPERTENSIVE RATHER THAN INCREASING THE DOSE OF THE
> FIRST** — which may cause adverse effects without improving control [H1].

| Preferred combinations [H1] |
|---|
| **ACE inhibitor (or sartan) + calcium channel blocker** |
| **ACE inhibitor (or sartan) + thiazide diuretic** |
| **calcium channel blocker + thiazide diuretic** |

**Also effective:** **beta-blocker with an ACE inhibitor/sartan, or with a dihydropyridine.**
⚠️ **Beta-blocker + thiazide is effective but INCREASES THE RISK OF DIABETES** compared with other
combinations [H1].

> ⚠️ **AVOID unless there are specific benefits** [H1]:
> **diltiazem or verapamil + beta-blocker** — severe bradycardia and heart block
> **ACE inhibitor or sartan + potassium-sparing diuretic** — hyperkalaemia
> **ACE inhibitor + sartan** — questionable benefit, may increase renal complications, specialist use only

**If still above target on two well-tolerated drugs:** **increase the dose of one NON-THIAZIDE agent
towards the maximum recommended; if still inadequate, increase the other** [H1].

### Resistant hypertension [H1]

**If BP remains above target on maximum doses of at least 2 appropriate agents, consider: POOR COMPLIANCE ·
HIGH SALT INTAKE · SECONDARY HYPERTENSION including drug-induced · VOLUME OVERLOAD · SLEEP APNOEA** [H1].

> **Resistant hypertension — uncontrolled BP despite 3 or more optimally tolerated antihypertensives
> INCLUDING A DIURETIC — occurs in MORE THAN 10% of patients.** ⚠️ **Consider PRIMARY ALDOSTERONISM,
> especially if hypokalaemia is present.** Seek specialist advice after ruling out compliance and other
> secondary causes [H1].

**SPIRONOLACTONE is an effective add-on** for resistant hypertension uncontrolled by first-line
combinations — ⚠️ **increased risk of HYPERKALAEMIA with an ACE inhibitor or sartan, particularly with
kidney disease** [H1].

**Combination products** may be convenient and less expensive: AMH lists **more than thirty** marketed in
Australia — most commonly **a sartan with hydrochlorothiazide or amlodipine**, plus **perindopril with
amlodipine or indapamide, trandolapril with verapamil, ramipril with felodipine, enalapril with
lercanidipine, and amlodipine with atorvastatin** [H1].

## Special cases [H1]

### Elderly
**Start at the lowest dose and titrate slowly.**

> **In a SPRINT subgroup of selected ambulatory patients aged over 75 — excluding diabetes, heart failure
> and history of stroke — lowering systolic BP to <120 mmHg significantly reduced cardiovascular and
> all-cause mortality.** ⚠️ **NOTE THE MEASUREMENT: the study used automated office BP, where a target
> <120 mmHg is comparable to a CLINIC systolic of 130–140 mmHg** [H1].

⚠️ **Aim for lower targets ONLY when treatment is well tolerated and with close monitoring — orthostatic
hypotension, electrolyte abnormalities, acute kidney injury** [H1]. **Orthostatic hypotension has its own
guideline here, and diuretics are on its exacerbating-drug list.**

### Coronary artery disease
> ⚠️ **THE CURVE IS J-SHAPED.** In an international cohort study of hypertensive patients with stable
> coronary artery disease, **BOTH high BP (systolic >140 or diastolic >80 mmHg) AND low BP (systolic <120
> or diastolic <70 mmHg) were associated with increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality** [H1].

### Diabetes
**Target <140/90 mmHg.** **If preventing stroke is a priority, consider lowering to systolic <120 mmHg
provided the patient is closely monitored for hypotension and acute kidney injury** [H1].
**ACE inhibitors or sartans are preferred — they delay progression of renal disease with micro- or
macroalbuminuria.** ⚠️ **Thiazides and beta-blockers have beneficial cardiovascular effects in diabetes but
TEND TO WORSEN GLYCAEMIC CONTROL** [H1].

### ⚠️ Hypertensive crisis
> ⚠️ **AVOID PRECIPITOUS BP REDUCTION — MORE THAN 25% OVER 2 HOURS** — to prevent MI and stroke. **Monitor
> BP continuously. Seek specialist advice. The aim is to limit acute end-organ damage** [H1].

**Rapid reduction is RARELY required** — exceptions are **aortic dissection, severe pre-eclampsia,
intracerebral haemorrhage**. **Where there is no end-organ damage, gradual reduction over several days is
sufficient and safer** [H1].

**To reduce over 2–6 hours: IV labetalol, sodium nitroprusside, glyceryl trinitrate, hydralazine,
clevidipine. Beta-blockers may be needed to control reflex tachycardia.** ⚠️ **In most cases aim towards
160/100 mmHg rather than normal BP, THEN WAIT 24 HOURS before lowering further** [H1].
**For less urgent reduction, use oral drugs and follow up within 24–72 hours** [H1].

### Pregnancy — summary only
**Stable chronic, gestational or non-severe pre-eclampsia: target BP <135/85 mmHg. First-line oral drugs:
METHYLDOPA, LABETALOL, NIFEDIPINE; oral hydralazine can also be considered** [H1].
⚠️ **AVOID ACE INHIBITORS AND SARTANS DURING PREGNANCY** [H1].
**Severe — systolic >160 or diastolic >110 mmHg: oral nifedipine (conventional tablet, available through
the SAS) and parenteral labetalol and hydralazine, per local protocols** [H1].
**Covered in full by the separate `hypertensive-disorders-of-pregnancy` guideline.**
