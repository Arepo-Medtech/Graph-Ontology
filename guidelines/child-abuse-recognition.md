# Child abuse: recognition and initial management

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** recognising possible physical abuse, sexual abuse and neglect in children presenting to hospital: when to suspect it, history, top-to-toe examination, consent, documentation, forensic opinion, treatment, escalation and safe discharge. No dedicated child-protection guideline exists in this corpus; abuse is flagged within `head-injury-children`, `burns-acute-management-children` and `functional-somatic-symptoms-children`. Forensic investigation protocols and sexual assault management are deferred to specialist services.

## Key points

- ⚠️ **Discuss all concerns with a senior clinician. Report all suspected cases in line with mandatory reporting obligations.** [S1]
- **Abuse can underlie many presentations. Assess every child with injury or suspected abuse top-to-toe.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Time-critical: suspected inflicted head injury, recent (under 72 hours) sexual assault, and poisoning — consult specialist services urgently.** Child sexual assault needs trained medical forensic examiners. [S1]
- **Abuse is common and can be missed or misdiagnosed** — diagnosis needs a high index of suspicion. Categories: physical assault, sexual assault, emotional maltreatment, neglect. [S1]

## When to suspect it

- ⚠️ **Consider abuse when:** no history accounts for the injury · unwitnessed trauma · family violence · a mechanism incompatible with age or development · inconsistent or changing histories · unreasonable delay in seeking care. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Also consider it for:** any injury in a non-ambulatory infant · another child said to have caused significant injury · **high-specificity injuries (ear bruising, posteromedial rib fractures, immersion-pattern scald, genital injury)** · an infant with unexplained encephalopathy · problematic or harmful sexual behaviours. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Unexplained encephalopathy:** any altered conscious state, collapse or arrest — consider abusive head trauma, ingestion or poisoning, toxins and suffocation. [S1]
- **Other red-flag injuries:** immersion (near drowning in a bath) · strangulation or suffocation · harm from fabricated or induced illness · harm from ingested poisons or medications · female genital mutilation. [S1]
- **Inflicted injuries can be occult** and may not be reported or detected by the carer. [S1]

## History and examination

- **Take history from multiple sources, including the child, with open-ended questions limited to what is clinically necessary.** Formal interviewing of the child is for the specialist service. [S1]
- **Ask about** personal and family bleeding disorders, connective tissue disease and developmental disorders, prior child protection or police contact, and protective orders. **Identify and document every child in the household and their ages.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Top-to-toe for every child:** height, weight and head circumference on percentile charts · ENT including ear drums, nostrils, frenulum, teeth · **fundoscopy** · a complete skin check including neck and joint creases, palms, soles, inside the mouth and under the nappy. [S1]
- **Document child–carer interactions**, and where possible examine with a colleague present (e.g. social worker). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Genital inspection only once, with the child's cooperation, when needed for medical care.** Forensic genital examination to decide whether sexual abuse occurred **only by an appropriately trained clinician**; seek specialist consultation for perineal injuries or sexual abuse concerns. [S1]

## Consent and documentation

- **Consent must be voluntary, informed, specific and given by someone with capacity** — a legal guardian and/or a Gillick-competent child. Child protection services may help if consent is refused. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Without consent, examine only in a medical emergency**, with senior advice. [S1]
- **Documentation has significant legal weight — use a proforma.** Clinical photographs (respecting legal and privacy rules) and body diagrams help; **always describe injuries.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Discuss with a senior clinician or specialist service before giving opinions** on whether abuse occurred, injury causation or timing. **Do not label injuries 'old' or 'new'.** [S1]

## Treat and escalate

- ⚠️ **Do not delay urgent treatment** (e.g. bleeding, fracture) while seeking specialist consultation. Give analgesia and psychological support. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Discuss any infant with unexplained encephalopathy or head injury with a senior clinician and specialist services** — shaking or direct head trauma can cause delayed deterioration; urgent investigation and ICU monitoring may be needed. [S1]
- **Early discussion with specialist services is recommended, e.g. for suspected poisoning** — some forensic investigations are time-critical. Hold a **multidisciplinary, multiagency meeting, if possible within 24 hours of admission.** [S1]
- **Consult the paediatric team for:** all children with unexplained encephalopathy or head injury · children admitted under surgeons with suspected abuse · children or siblings needing assessment for cumulative harm. **Transfer to a tertiary hospital for suspected abusive head injury.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Discharge only with a safe discharge plan approved by a senior clinician** — the joint responsibility of the health service and child protection — and ongoing supports in place. [S1]
