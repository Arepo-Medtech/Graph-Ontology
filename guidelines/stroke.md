# Stroke — acute care, rehabilitation and follow-up

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-22 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults with ischaemic stroke or intracerebral haemorrhage, pre-hospital to six-month review.

> **Australian primary source.** Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care (ACSQHC).
> ***Stroke Clinical Care Standard*** [S1]. **Last updated 11 June 2026** — **the most current document in this
> compendium**, three months old at the time of writing.

**Eight quality statements. Twenty-five indicators** — the densest measurement set of the fifty guidelines here.

## ⚠️ The rename is the finding

> **Change of title, previously the 'Acute Stroke Clinical Care Standard', to include aspects of care extending
> beyond the acute and hyperacute phase** [S1].

The Standard was **first released in 2015 with a minor update in 2019**, and the June 2026 revision
**incorporates changes in the relevant evidence-based guidelines since 2019** [S1]. The word *acute* was
deleted from the title on purpose. Alongside it: **a new quality statement about follow-up**, **greater
emphasis on rehabilitation**, **inclusion of time-critical care for intracerebral haemorrhage**, **greater
emphasis on ambulance services and pre-hospital notification**, **broader inclusion of stroke telehealth**,
and **addition of cultural safety and equity considerations** [S1].

**Set that against the rest of this compendium.** The acute coronary syndromes standard states it *has not
been updated against the 2025 guidelines*, with a replacement due late 2027. The dementia guideline's NHMRC
approval **expired 1 February 2021**. COPD-X paused surveillance. The RACGP osteoarthritis guideline is
*currently being updated*. **Stroke is what currency looks like** — and the compendium can now show both
states side by side.

Indicators changed too: new indicators for **time-critical care, stroke unit care, multidisciplinary
assessment of rehabilitation needs, referral for ongoing rehabilitation and participation in follow-up**, plus
**the retiring of four indicators** and **revisions to several** [S1].

## ⚠️ TIA is explicitly out of scope — and this compendium already covers it

**This Standard does not cover transient ischaemic attack (TIA)**, subarachnoid/extradural/subdural
haemorrhage, ICH secondary to a structural brain abnormality, or **the care of children with stroke** [S1].

**The TIA guideline in this set is the matching half.** Between them the two documents cover the warning and
the event, and each names the boundary the other starts at — the cleanest pairing in the compendium. (The TIA
guideline also carries the compendium's first explicit "do not": **ABCD² should not be used** to triage TIA.)

The Standard **does not offer specific guidance on end-of-life or palliative care** for stroke patients,
referring instead to the **National Consensus Statement: Essential elements for safe and high-quality
end-of-life care** [S1].

## Statement 1 — recognise, and drive past the nearest hospital

**A person with suspected stroke is assessed using a validated stroke screening tool, such as F.A.S.T.**, and
**transported immediately to a hospital capable of providing appropriate time-critical therapy**, with **the
hospital pre-notified** [S1].

> **Note that F.A.S.T. is a useful tool for identifying stroke, particularly for non-clinicians. However,
> other tools may have greater accuracy in detecting acute ischaemic stroke** [S1].

**The Standard recommends the famous tool and immediately qualifies it.** The alternatives it names: **BE-FAST,
CRESST, MASS, ROSIER**; and for specific purposes **ACT-FAST** and **RACE** (large vessel occlusion),
**HUNTER-8 or NIHSS-8** (impairment), **modified Rankin Scale** (premorbid function) [S1].

- **Once a patient is suspected of having an acute (clinical signs less than 24 hours) stroke, expedite primary
  emergency transport (usually by ambulance)** to a capable hospital [S1].
- > **Pre-hospital clinicians**, including ambulance services, **should use pre-notification systems and bypass
  > to access hospitals capable of providing appropriate time-critical stroke therapies (including
  > thrombolysis) when required** [S1].
- **Ambulances should have agreed bypass protocols to transport people to a Comprehensive Stroke Centre (CSC)
  in the case of patients with suspected large vessel occlusion** [S1].
- Document **symptom discovery, last known 'well' time, time of ambulance arrival on scene and medication
  history** [S1].
- Hospital teams on pre-notification: **obtain medical records and a best possible medication history, contact
  next of kin, activate a 'Code Stroke' alert system, and facilitate access to CT imaging** [S1].
- > **More detailed stroke severity scales (for example, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale [NIHSS])
  > may be used in hospital but are not recommended in the pre-hospital setting** [S1].
- **Exceptions include patients for whom transfer would not be consistent with their goals of care or advanced
  care plan, such as palliative care patients** [S1].

## Statement 2 — time-critical therapy, for both kinds of stroke

**A patient with ischaemic stroke suitable for reperfusion therapy receives timely thrombolysis and/or
endovascular thrombectomy. A patient with intracerebral haemorrhage receives urgent blood-pressure-lowering
therapy and/or anticoagulation reversal where appropriate** [S1].

**Including ICH in the time-critical statement is new in 2026** [S1] — the haemorrhagic half now carries the
same urgency framing as the ischaemic half.

- **Consider reperfusion treatment for all patients with ischaemic stroke** [S1].
- **Use multimodal imaging (Non-Contrast CT, and both CT angiogram and CT perfusion) when possible** to
  identify candidates for thrombectomy or surgery [S1].
- **Ensure all imaging (including any CT angiogram) is transferred to the treating hospital rapidly to
  facilitate treatment on arrival** [S1].
- **In hospitals that do not provide 24-hour, on-site access to stroke specialists, use telestroke systems** to
  assist with assessment and decisions on thrombolysis and possible transfer for thrombectomy [S1].
- ICH: **promptly initiate control of blood pressure and reversal of coagulopathy** within the Living
  Guidelines' targets and timeframes; **arrange urgent vascular imaging** to identify bleeds secondary to
  vascular malformations; **refer for neurosurgical consultation** [S1].
- ⚠️ > **If time-critical therapy is not provided, document the reason in the patient's medical record** [S1].

**All thresholds are deferred, not stated.** Time windows, blood pressure targets, reversal agents and doses
live in the **Living Clinical Guidelines for Stroke Management** — see *Unresolved*. **They are not
reconstructed here.**

## ⚠️ Nine of the twenty-five indicators are median times

| Indicator [S1] | |
|---|---|
| **1b** | Median time from **first clinical contact to arrival** at a capable hospital |
| **2b** | Median time from **arrival to intravenous thrombolytic** |
| **2e** | Median time from **arrival to departure** for patients transferred for thrombectomy |
| **2f** | Median time from **arrival to endovascular thrombectomy** |
| **2g** | Median time from **arrival to antihypertensive** in ICH with elevated systolic BP |
| **2h** | Median time from **arrival to anticoagulation reversal** in ICH on an anticoagulant |
| **3b** | Median time from **arrival to admission to a stroke unit** |

Plus the rate indicators **2a** (proportion thrombolysed), **2c** (proportion receiving thrombectomy) and
**2d** (proportion transferred for thrombectomy) [S1].

**Indicator 2e is the one to notice** — *arrival to departure*. It measures the door-in-door-out interval of
the hospital that **cannot** perform the procedure, making the referring hospital's delay visible as its own
number rather than disappearing into the receiving centre's total.

## Statement 3 — the ward is the intervention

> **People with stroke who receive care in a dedicated stroke unit are more likely to be alive, living at
> home, and independently looking after themselves one year after their stroke** [S1].

Stroke unit care is **organised care within a specific ward**, by **a multidisciplinary team who specialise in
stroke management**, with **care quality recorded and guided by established protocols** [S1]. **ICUs, HDUs or
similar locations are appropriate alternatives** for patients requiring critical care [S1]. Where there is no
stroke unit, care goes to **the nearest similar unit able to meet the requirements**, which **may include
management on the ward where the patient is located, with access to a telestroke service and allied health
assessment** [S1].

**The protocolised early care set** [S1]:

- ⚠️ **Screening for swallowing difficulties within four hours of arrival to hospital using a validated
  screening tool — before giving patients food, fluids or oral medications**
- **Monitoring and prompt management of pyrexia and hyperglycaemia for the first 72 hours**
- **Screening for communication difficulties**
- **Ensuring appropriate venous thromboembolism (VTE) prophylaxis**
- **Escalation pathways** — speech pathology review where swallowing or communication difficulties are found;
  notifying medical officers on temperature or glucose outside parameters

This is the **FeSS (Fever, Sugar and Swallowing)** bundle, named in the Standard's own resources [S1] — and
VTE prophylaxis routes directly to the **Venous Thromboembolism Prevention Clinical Care Standard**, already
in this compendium. **Another reciprocal standard-to-standard citation**, alongside delirium ↔ hip fracture
and antimicrobial stewardship ↔ sepsis.

> **All clinicians should use supportive communication techniques with patients who have communication
> difficulties. This improves patient consent processes, patients' ability to express their needs and
> preferences, and patient engagement with care** [S1].

**Indicator 3d** audits the swallow screen: **proportion of patients who had a swallow screen or assessment
completed before they were given any food, fluids or oral medication** [S1].

## ⚠️ Rehabilitation — within 48 hours, but not within 24

**Initial rehabilitation needs assessed by a multidisciplinary team as early as possible and within 48 hours
of hospital admission**; individualised rehabilitation **begins as soon as clinically appropriate during the
admission**; needs **continually assessed and documented**; **arrangements for ongoing rehabilitation made
before discharge** [S1].

> **Note: Intensive early mobilisation within 24 hours of stroke onset is not recommended** [S1].

**A national standard telling clinicians to do less, sooner-than-optimal.** Earlier is better for *assessment*
and worse for *intensive mobilisation* — two clocks running in opposite directions in the same statement.
This is an evidence reversal, and it joins the compendium's growing "do not" list.

- The assessment **should include, but not be limited to, medical (including rehabilitation medicine),
  nursing, occupational therapy, physiotherapy, speech pathology, dietetics, and clinical psychology** [S1].
- **Record functional status using a validated tool such as the Functional Independence Measure (FIM),
  modified Rankin Scale (mRS), Barthel Index, or Functional Autonomy Measurement System (SMAF)** [S1].
- **Consider changes in the patient's mood and cognition such as difficulty with concentration, memory loss,
  and language issues** [S1].
- **Alternative arrangements may apply if the patient is unable or unwilling to participate in rehabilitation,
  or rehabilitation is not consistent with their goals of care** [S1].

**Three separate 48-hour discipline indicators** — **4a physiotherapist, 4b speech pathologist, 4c occupational
therapist** — plus **4d** (ongoing needs assessed and documented before discharge) and **4e** (referred for
ongoing rehabilitation before discharge) [S1]. **Naming each discipline separately** stops a single "MDT
review" tick from standing in for three assessments.

## Statement 5 — find the cause, then prevent the next one

- **Communicate with the patient's general practice team** for previous treatments and investigations —
  **previous response to relevant medicines and results of cardiac monitoring** [S1].
- **Offer testing and assessment to investigate the mechanism of the patient's stroke** — for example **cardiac
  assessment and monitoring to screen for atrial fibrillation**. **When assessment is not appropriate during
  the acute admission, refer or arrange for the assessments to be completed promptly after hospital
  discharge** [S1].
- **Prescribe appropriate medicines for secondary prevention, including anticoagulants for atrial fibrillation
  and antiplatelet, antihypertensive and lipid-lowering medicines, where indicated** [S1].
- **Other measures may include time-limited surgical interventions (for example, carotid endarterectomy) or
  closure of a patent foramen ovale in younger patients** [S1].
- Modifiable risk factors to discuss: **uncontrolled hypertension or diabetes, smoking, poor diet, insufficient
  physical activity and excessive alcohol or recreational drug use** — **and provide written
  information** [S1].

**Three discharge-prescribing indicators**: **5a** antihypertensive, **5b** lipid-lowering (ischaemic stroke),
**5c** oral anticoagulant (ischaemic stroke **and** atrial fibrillation) [S1] — the last landing squarely on
this compendium's **atrial fibrillation** guideline.

## Statement 6 — the family is given training, not just information

**Practical assistance includes advice, guidance and training for families and support people on how to safely
support the patient with their new daily needs** [S1], across four domains:

| Domain [S1] | |
|---|---|
| **Physical care** | personal care techniques, **use of new assistive equipment**, **modification of food and drink to ensure safe swallowing**, safe mobilisation and transfer |
| **Communication and cognition** | **communication partner training**, strategies for changes in memory, concentration and behaviour |
| **Psychosocial wellbeing** | fatigue management, emotional support, **carer self-care**, return to community life |
| **System navigation** | **respite, carer financial aid, NDIS or My Aged Care**, health and financial decision-making responsibilities, contact details for support services |

> **Documenting all care given in the 24-hour period before discharge can help in identifying the patient's
> support needs** when they are discharged [S1].

**A concrete method, not an exhortation** — write down everything the ward did in the last day, because that
is the workload about to transfer to someone at home.

⚠️ > **If a patient is assessed as able to manage independently but does not have an identified carer or
> support person, offer the patient information, support and advice (in a way they can understand) to help
> them safely and confidently manage their daily needs** [S1].

**The statement names the person with nobody** and does not leave them out of its own indicator's shadow.

## Statement 7 — the care plan, and one item that is easy to forget

The individualised care plan is **jointly developed** with the patient and family and must include **results
from relevant rehabilitation assessments**; **secondary prevention recommendations, and where possible a
medication list and education provided by a pharmacist**; practical assistance information; and **details of
referrals and follow-up appointments** [S1] — with, listed among them:

> **advice on when the person may resume driving a vehicle** [S1].

**A copy goes to the patient, to their general practice, and where appropriate to the ongoing rehabilitation
team or aged care provider, before or at the time of discharge** [S1]. **The patient must receive a copy of
the individualised care plan to take with them before being discharged** [S1].

## Statement 8 — the new one

**A patient who has had a stroke receives a follow-up assessment and review, with appropriate multidisciplinary
team input, within six months of their stroke diagnosis. This is arranged before discharge** [S1].

> **It may be face-to-face or via telehealth, and may not necessarily be the first or only follow-up with a
> healthcare provider** [S1].

Five domains to address [S1]:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Medical and secondary prevention** | medication adherence and indication, progress with lifestyle modifications, **any pending investigation results** |
| **Rehabilitation** | re-assess against goals and progress; **record functional status using a validated tool**; **review the choice of rehabilitation pathway** |
| **Functional status** | **cognition, vision, fitness to drive, continence and equipment needs** |
| **Psychosocial and vocational** | **re-assess mood and, if appropriate, sexual dysfunction**; **review or develop a return-to-work plan, which may include returning to voluntary work, community roles or ongoing carer responsibilities for partners, parents, children or grandchildren**; community reintegration |
| **Care coordination** | review effectiveness of existing referrals; assess need for further appointments |

**"Ongoing carer responsibilities for partners, parents, children or grandchildren" is doing real work** —
it treats the stroke survivor as someone other people may still depend on, not only as someone now dependent.

**Give the patient, their GP and their ongoing rehabilitation team an updated care plan at the end of the
appointment** [S1].

## The size of it

- **In 2023, there were an estimated 45,785 stroke events in Australia, including 34,793 first-ever strokes,
  which equates to one stroke every 11 minutes** [S1]
- **The lifetime costs associated with strokes that occurred in 2023 exceeded $15 billion, which equates to
  almost $350,000 per person** [S1]
- **In 2022, stroke was the underlying cause of 8,400 deaths in Australia** [S1]

**The Standard has been endorsed by 23 key organisations** [S1], and is **supported** by the RACGP, the
Queensland Aphasia Research Centre and **Therapeutic Guidelines Ltd** [S1].
