# Migraine

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-21 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults. Acute treatment and pharmacological prevention.

> Every clinical sentence carries a source tag. Claims no retrieved source supports are listed under
> *Unresolved* rather than written. **No free Australian guideline exists for migraine** — the sources are
> international, and the Australian layer is PBS listing only.

## Treatment aims

Two separate decisions: **abort the attack**, and — where attacks are frequent or disabling enough —
**reduce how often they occur**. The evidence for each is of different quality and they should not be
reasoned about together.

## Acute treatment

The International Headache Society's global practice recommendations deliberately give **two tiers** for
every question: an *optimal* recommendation to use whenever possible, and an *essential* one for settings
where the optimal cannot be attained [S2]. Seventeen questions are covered this way. That structure is
worth preserving in any local adaptation: it is designed for exactly the situation where the best-supported
agent is unavailable or unaffordable.

A 2025 Italian Society for the Study of Headache / IHS guideline gives GRADE-assessed pharmacological
recommendations, and explicitly flags **the paucity of head-to-head drug comparisons** and the need for
long-term outcome studies as gaps in the evidence base [S1].

## Prevention

The strongest comparative evidence is a network meta-analysis of **74 trials, 32,990 patients** [S3]:

| Certainty | Agents | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **High** | CGRP / CGRP-receptor monoclonal antibodies, gepants, topiramate | increase the proportion achieving ≥50% reduction in monthly migraine days vs placebo |
| **Moderate** | beta-blockers, valproate, amitriptyline | same outcome |
| **Low** | gabapentin | may not differ from placebo |

Note what this does and does not say. It ranks agents against **placebo** by certainty of evidence, not
against each other — which is the same gap S1 names [S1]. High certainty for topiramate and for CGRP mAbs
does not mean they are equivalent, nor that either is first choice.

## Australian context

PBS Schedule 4333, checked directly [S4]:

| Agent | PBS | Benefit type |
|---|---|---|
| Amitriptyline | listed | **unrestricted** |
| Propranolol | listed | **unrestricted** (also R) |
| Candesartan | listed | unrestricted (also R) |
| Pizotifen | listed — 500 microgram tablets | unrestricted (also R) |
| Sumatriptan, rizatriptan, zolmitriptan, eletriptan | listed | **restricted** |
| Naratriptan | listed | **authority required** |
| Topiramate | listed | authority required (streamlined) |
| Galcanezumab, fremanezumab | listed | authority required (streamlined) |
| **Erenumab** | **not on Schedule 4333** | — |
| **Rimegepant** (gepant) | **not on Schedule 4333** | — |

The Australian access gradient runs opposite to the evidence gradient. The agents with **high**-certainty
evidence [S3] are the least accessible: gepants are not PBS-listed at all, CGRP mAbs require authority, and
topiramate requires authority. The **moderate**-certainty agents — amitriptyline, propranolol — are
unrestricted. Every acute triptan is restricted or authority-required.

So the practical first line in Australian general practice is a moderate-certainty agent, and that is a
funding decision rather than a clinical one. It should be visible when it is made.
