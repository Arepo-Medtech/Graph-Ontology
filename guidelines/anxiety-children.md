# Anxiety in children

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** recognition, assessment, psychoeducation, psychological therapy, SSRI initiation and titration, follow-up and referral for anxiety disorders in children and adolescents. The adult counterpart is `generalised-anxiety-disorder`.

> ✅ **OPEN AND QUOTED.** 25 of 25 claims quoted verbatim from the **RCH Melbourne Clinical Practice Guideline *Anxiety: identification and management*** (Last updated April 2024); **36 fragments re-checked by machine. 6 quoted doses.**

> **Psychological therapy is first line and ongoing.** ⚠️ **Medication is not first line except in exceptional circumstances, and always alongside continued psychological therapy.** SSRIs are used off label in Australian children.

## Key points

- **Consider whether anxiety contributes to every child's presentation.** **Psychological therapy is first-line and ongoing**; any medication goes alongside it. [S1]
- **Anxiety disorders differ from normal anxiety by intensity, duration and impact on daily function.** [S1]

## Recognise and assess

- **Look for:** regular avoidance (school, social events, play, sport, eating, sleeping) · frequent tummy aches and headaches · outbursts or meltdowns · poor sleep · appetite change · frequent reassurance-seeking · poor concentration · overplanning and overthinking. [S1]
- **Check against DSM-5 or ICD-11 criteria**; screening tools (Spence, SCARED, Social phobia inventory) help but **must be supplemented by clinical interview and observation.** [S1]
- **Risk factors include** neurodevelopmental disorders (autism, ADHD, intellectual disability), other mental health conditions, chronic illness, sensory impairment, gender-related concerns, trauma, family history of anxiety, depression, OCD or substance use, and substance misuse. [S1]
- **Examine:** baseline cardiac exam, BP, heart rate and growth; targeted exam to rule out mimics such as **hyperthyroidism**. **No investigations** unless considering an alternative diagnosis. [S1]

## Psychoeducation and therapy

- **Always start with psychoeducation**, then the psychological and medication options acceptable to child and family. [S1]
- **CBT is first line for ages 8–18 with mild–moderate symptoms**, before considering medication. **Internet CBT** (eg BRAVE, Cool Kids) suits children 8 and over. [S1]
- **Play therapy** for under-8s, procedural anxiety or medical trauma, or under-12s who struggle with CBT; **ACT** for 12–18-year-olds, especially with chronic health conditions. [S1]

## When to consider medication

- ⚠️ **Medication is not first line except in exceptional circumstances**, and is best started by a clinician experienced in paediatric psychotropic prescribing. [S1]
- **Add medication to therapy if anxiety is** too severe for meaningful therapy · significantly limiting school attendance · carrying moderate or greater self-harm risk · a significant risk to a family member's wellbeing. **Continue therapy alongside.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Starting medication may not reduce self-harm risk and may increase agitation at first** (activation syndrome). [S1]

## SSRIs

- **SSRIs are first-line medication**, prescribed **off label** — none is TGA-licensed for anxiety or depression in children. **Fluoxetine and escitalopram are sensible choices** (FDA-approved for depressive episodes). [S1]
- **Example doses — fluoxetine** (tab, liquid): start 5–10 mg, change by 5–10 mg, typical 20–40 mg. [S1]
- **Sertraline** (tab): start 12.5–25 mg, change by 12.5–25 mg, typical 50–100 mg. [S1]
- **Escitalopram** (tab): start 5 mg, change by 5 mg, typical 10–20 mg. [S1]
- **Start low and titrate slowly** to the minimum effective dose; **effect takes several weeks** and keeps building over months. Unfamiliar prescribers should discuss with a senior clinician. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Activation syndrome** — more activity, impulsivity, disinhibition, restlessness, irritability, insomnia. **Use caution, low doses and slow up-titration** in highly aroused/ADHD children, family history of bipolar disorder, and younger children. [S1]
- **Common adverse effects:** nausea, vomiting, poor appetite, dry mouth, agitation, insomnia or sedation, headache, dizziness, sweating, sexual dysfunction. **Taper SSRIs gradually**; prefer a long half-life such as fluoxetine if adherence is a concern. [S1]
- **Adjust:** partial effect — consider increasing; intolerable side effects — reduce or stop; **ineffective at tolerated doses after a reasonable trial (eg 8 weeks) — stop and change**, first to another SSRI. [S1]

## Other drugs

- **Short-acting benzodiazepines may be cautiously considered for an acute crisis in high-risk settings** (eg ED). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Not recommended for anxiety disorders alone:** antipsychotics, clonidine, guanfacine, atomoxetine, reboxetine, tricyclic antidepressants. [S1]

## Follow-up, referral, discharge

- **Use rating scales (eg RCADS) throughout; on starting medication, review response and adverse effects every 2 weeks** until stable. [S1]
- **Involve Child and Adolescent Mental Health** if anxiety is complicated by other mental health problems, carries significant risk to the child or others, or is beyond the clinician's comfort. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Discharge only after a risk assessment for harm to self or others deems it safe**, with follow-up arranged — usually ongoing. [S1]
