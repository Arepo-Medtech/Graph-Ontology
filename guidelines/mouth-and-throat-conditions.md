# Mouth and throat conditions

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-22 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults and children in general practice, covering the mouth, teeth and throat.

> **Mixed sources.** **S1** and **S2** are **open** RACGP *AJGP* articles (**September 2020**), **quoted
> verbatim** — 20 claims. **A1** is the **Australian Medicines Handbook**, **paraphrased** — 21 claims, 6
> doses, **no passage stored**.

**AMH topics closed by this guideline (7):** *Mouth and throat conditions*, *Angular cheilitis*,
*Dental abscess*, *Dry mouth*, *Gingivitis*, *Herpes stomatitis*, *Ulcers, oral*.

## ⚠️ Two things that kill, and one threshold the sources disagree on

### 1 · The non-healing ulcer

| Source | Threshold |
|---|---|
| ⚠️ **S1 (AJGP 2020)** | **mucosal turnover should occur in <10 days; therefore ANY persistent ulcer present for ≥2 WEEKS should be referred** to an oral and maxillofacial surgeon or oral medicine specialist **for biopsy** |
| ⚠️ **A1 (AMH 2026)** | **consider neoplasia in non-healing ulcers (present for >3 WEEKS)** |

⚠️ **A week apart, on a cancer referral.** ⚠️ **This guideline states both and does not split the
difference** — but note that **S1 gives its reasoning (mucosal turnover under 10 days) and A1 does not**,
and that **the shorter threshold is the safer one for this purpose.** ⚠️ **That last judgement is the
reader's to make with both numbers in front of them.**

**Why the threshold matters** [S1]:

> ⚠️ **Oral SCC may not necessarily be painful, and PAIN IS NOT USED to differentiate between a potentially
> malignant or benign cause of an ulcer.**
>
> ⚠️ **Up to 10% of SCCs are diagnosed in patients who do not smoke or drink alcohol. Therefore, THE
> ABSENCE OF RISK FACTORS DOES NOT RULE OUT the possibility of malignancy** — and **oral SCC is found
> across virtually all age groups**.

**Risk factors: smoking, alcohol consumption of >3 standard drinks per day, and betel quid (paan)
consumption** [S1].

**Appearance:** **most commonly a non-healing ulcer, which can be indurated/firm with irregular margins and
raised, rolled edges.** **As it invades, it may cause neurosensory change (paraesthesia or anaesthesia) and
tooth mobility; in late stages, alteration of speech and swallowing** [S1]. **Lip cancer typically presents
as a non-healing ulcer on the vermillion of the lower lip** [S1].

⚠️ **Also refer:** **a bleeding lesion, or an area of mucosa that persistently appears red or white.**
**Leukoplakia and erythroplakia** — white and red patches **that cannot be scraped off and cannot be
ascribed to any disease** — ⚠️ **referral for biopsy is MANDATORY, as a significant proportion will be
dysplastic or malignant.** **Any tissue excised from the oral cavity should be sent for histopathological
examination, as clinical appearance alone is insufficient** [S1].

⚠️ **If any suspicious malignant features: immediate referral** [S1].

### 2 · The dental abscess

> ⚠️ **Once the infection has spread beyond the confines of the jaws, there is an increasing risk of airway
> obstruction and septicaemia. IF TREATED WITH ANTIBIOTICS ALONE, THE INFECTION WILL NOT RESOLVE AND WILL
> BECOME PROGRESSIVELY WORSE** [S2].
>
> **Antibacterial treatment should not be used alone; it cannot replace the need for dental or surgical
> treatment** [A1].

⚠️ **Treatment by a dentist is crucial — to remove infected tissue, drain the abscess and, if necessary,
extract the tooth. An antibacterial is ADJUNCTIVE** [A1].

⚠️ **Patients with dental abscesses commonly initially present to their primary health providers,
particularly if they have dental phobia or ARE FINANCIALLY CONSTRAINED. It is easy to underestimate their
condition** [S2] — **a script is the cheapest thing to offer and the one that does not work.**

**The anatomy that matters** [S2]: ⚠️ **the most dangerous space is the submandibular space.** **When the
masseter is irritated by inflammation, TRISMUS or difficulty in jaw opening ensues.** ⚠️ **The submandibular
space is in direct contact with the pharyngeal spaces and down through the neck to the mediastinum, and also
connects to the contralateral side.** **Swelling can extend over a wide area of the airway in both length
and breadth.**

⚠️ **Prompt assessment and referral to a tertiary hospital is required for cases at risk of airway
compromise** [S2].

**On the name:** ⚠️ **strictly, Ludwig's angina refers only to cases in which the whole neck bilaterally
from the mandible to the clavicle is involved — such cases have a poor prognosis.** **The term is commonly
misinterpreted to apply to any localised neck infection** [S2].

**Why S2 exists:** ⚠️ **in the pre-antibiotic era, dental infection was a common cause of death, with
fatality rates of 10–40%. With antibiotics, odontogenic infections responded well to penicillin. This may
have created a FALSE SENSE OF SECURITY**, while **antibiotic-resistant bacteria were generally on the
increase** [S2]. ⚠️ **A patient died of airway obstruction a few hours after operation at the Royal Adelaide
Hospital in 2002**, prompting review and audit; **more than 1000 cases of severe odontogenic infection were
managed there between 2002 and 2019** [S2].

## Oral ulceration

**Most cases are idiopathic and heal spontaneously in 10–14 days; they often recur** [A1].
**Causes may include oral trauma, poor nutrition, drugs (eg methotrexate, NSAIDs, nicorandil), infection and
some diseases** [A1].

⚠️ **Nicorandil is an antianginal — and a PDE5 inhibitor is contraindicated with it.** See **erectile
dysfunction**.

**The commonest ulcer is traumatic** — **mechanical (eg biting), thermal, radiation or chemical.**
⚠️ **Address the cause and review to ensure mucosal healing within two weeks; if it persists beyond that,
refer** [S1].

### Recurrent aphthous ulceration [S1]

**Affects 20–50% of the population**, **painful, recurrent, almost always on non-keratinised oral mucosa**.

| Subtype | Size | Course |
|---|---|---|
| **Minor** (most common) | **oval, <10 mm** | **5–10 days, heals without scarring** |
| ⚠️ **Major** | **variably shaped, >10 mm** | **up to six weeks, can scar** — ⚠️ **can resemble an ulcer of an early malignancy** |
| **Herpetiform** | **numerous, 1–2 mm, may coalesce** | **1–2 weeks**; ⚠️ **not restricted to non-keratinised mucosa** |

⚠️ **Check the bloods in the right subgroup:** **aphthous-like ulceration can be associated with haematinic
deficiency or gastrointestinal or rheumatological disease. Vitamin B12, folic acid or iron deficiency can
be associated in a small proportion of patients, and these nutritional markers should be checked and
corrected** [S1].

### Symptomatic treatment [A1]

**Saline mouthwash — half a teaspoon of salt in a glass of warm water — to soothe mild ulcers.**
**Non-alcoholic chlorhexidine mouthwash short-term; it may hasten healing and prevent secondary infection.**

⚠️ **There is limited evidence for topical agents to reduce ulcer severity and duration, and THEY DO NOT
PREVENT RECURRENCE** [A1]:

- **local corticosteroids, eg triamcinolone paste** — **symptomatic relief, may promote healing of mild to
  moderate ulcers**; ⚠️ **best applied in the PRODROMAL phase (tingling or swelling), or when ulcers first
  appear**
- **local anaesthetics (lidocaine, benzocaine) or anti-inflammatories (benzydamine)** — **temporary
  analgesia**
- **protectants (eg paste)** — **stick to the mucosa and protect the ulcer from further abrasion**

## Angular cheilitis

**Redness and maceration of the skin at the angle of the mouth, USUALLY ASSOCIATED WITH ORAL CANDIDIASIS.
Treat with topical miconazole** [A1].

⚠️ **Crusting may suggest infection with *Staphylococcus aureus*.** **Other causes include ill-fitting
dentures, dermatitis, vitamin B or iron deficiency** [A1].

## Oral candidiasis

**More common in elderly people.** **Treatment with broad-spectrum antibacterials or inhaled
corticosteroids, immunosuppression and diabetes all increase risk** [A1].

**Immunocompetent:** **a topical antifungal — miconazole, amphotericin B or nystatin — for 1–2 weeks.**
**Consider an oral antifungal (eg fluconazole) for severe or unresponsive disease, especially in
immunocompromised patients** [A1].

⚠️ **Denture-associated candida stomatitis: brush the upper surface of the dentures with oral miconazole
after cleaning teeth** [A1] — **treating the patient without the denture leaves the reservoir.**

## Herpes stomatitis

**Mild-to-moderate: adequate fluid intake, soft diet and analgesics may be all that is required.**
**Use non-alcoholic chlorhexidine mouthwash if tooth brushing is too painful.** ⚠️ **Severe herpes
stomatitis requires systemic antivirals** [A1].

## Gingivitis

**Caused by plaque and calculus deposits on the teeth; effective treatment requires their removal by a
dentist. Good oral hygiene, with effective brushing and flossing, prevents gingivitis and periodontal
disease** [A1].

**Non-alcoholic chlorhexidine mouthwash can inhibit plaque formation and may be used in moderate-to-severe
gingivitis, as an ADJUNCT to improved oral hygiene and teeth scaling by a dentist** [A1].

⚠️ **In ACUTE ULCERATIVE gingivitis antibacterial treatment is also needed, eg amoxicillin with clavulanic
acid or clindamycin for 5 days. More severe infections require combination antibacterial treatment** [A1].

## Dry mouth

**Causes include disease of the salivary glands, radiation therapy and medication — particularly drugs with
anticholinergic activity, eg TCAs** [A1].

⚠️ **This compendium prescribes several: the genitourinary anticholinergics for urge incontinence, the
ocular anticholinergics, sedating antihistamines, and prochlorperazine.**

**Simple measures:** **regular drinks (non-acidic, sugarless), sucking ice or lozenges (sugarless for those
with their own teeth), and sugarless chewing gum** [A1].

⚠️ **Artificial saliva products may help, and for patients with their own teeth, products with NEUTRAL pH
are recommended** [A1] — A1's table gives pH values **ranging from about 5 to 7** [A1]. ⚠️ **The acidic end
of that range is the part to avoid in a dentate patient.**

**Encourage regular dental checks to monitor dental and gingival health** [A1].

## Sore throat

**Commonly caused by a virus; occasionally bacterial, typically streptococcal** [A1].

⚠️ **Topical products — anaesthetic, anti-inflammatory or antiseptic in a lozenge, gargle or spray — have
LITTLE EVIDENCE OF BENEFIT** [A1].

> **In patients with SEVERE acute sore throat, where pain is not relieved by paracetamol or NSAIDs, consider
> adding to standard care a single dose of corticosteroid (eg prednisolone 1 mg/kg, maximum 50 mg). Repeat
> dose the next day if necessary** [A1].

### Streptococcal pharyngitis

**Group A beta-haemolytic streptococci are a more common cause in children than adults. Symptoms may present
abruptly: fever, tonsillar exudate, cervical lymphadenopathy and ABSENCE OF COUGH** [A1].

| Who | What [A1] |
|---|---|
| ⚠️ **High risk of rheumatic fever (eg rural/remote Aboriginal people)** | ⚠️ **treat with a systemic antibacterial** |
| **Not at high risk** | **consider antibacterials if symptoms are severe** — they **may reduce symptom duration and complications** |

**Where indicated: 10 days of phenoxymethylpenicillin (alternative cefalexin); a single dose of IM
benzathine benzylpenicillin may also be used** [A1].

⚠️ **The decision to treat is a rheumatic-fever-risk decision before it is a symptom decision.**

### Recurrent tonsillitis in children

⚠️ **Recurrent tonsillitis is often over-diagnosed.** **Recurrent sore throat is common in childhood and
usually has a viral cause. A throat swab during an acute episode can help guide diagnosis** [A1].

⚠️ **Although long-term low-dose penicillin and other approaches have been used for true recurrent
tonsillitis, THERE IS NO GOOD EVIDENCE OF BENEFIT. Consider specialist referral** [A1].

## Tooth decay

**Regular brushing and flossing and avoiding sugary foods and smoking — which reduces saliva — are
important.** **Fluoride encourages tooth enamel to repair itself**, and ⚠️ **fluoride mouth rinses may
reduce tooth decay in children even when fluoridated water and toothpaste are used** [A1].
