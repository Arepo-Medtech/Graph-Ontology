# GORD and dyspepsia — proton pump inhibitor therapy

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-22 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults. PPI indications, treatment duration, adverse events and deprescribing.

> **Australian primary source.** Turner J, Reeve E, Bell JS, et al. ***Deprescribing proton pump
> inhibitors.*** *Australian Journal of General Practice*, November 2022 [S1]. **Commissioned, externally
> peer reviewed.**

**AMH topics closed by this guideline:** *GORD*, *Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease*, *Dyspepsia*.

## ⚠️ The scale of the problem is the reason the guideline exists

> **More than 15 million PPI prescriptions were dispensed through the Australian Pharmaceutical Benefits
> Scheme in 2020–21** [S1].

> **Fifteen per cent of Australian adults receive at least one PPI prescription each year, suggesting a large
> number of Australians are exposed to the risk of adverse events without additional benefit** [S1].

> ⚠️ **Recent reports suggest that 22–63% of PPIs are continued longer than is likely necessary (ie continued
> despite no indication for long-term therapy)** [S1]. An international review reported **PPIs were overused
> or improperly used by 11–84% of patients** [S1].

**The Gastroenterological Society of Australia and The Royal Australian College of General Practitioners
recommend regular review of long-term PPI use with the goal of dose reduction and cessation** [S1].

## First-line therapy for GORD and dyspepsia

> **The Australian Therapeutic guidelines recommend PPIs be trialled for GORD and dyspepsia if antacids, diet
> and lifestyle modifications fail to resolve symptoms. This trial involves prescribing a PPI once daily, to
> be taken 30 minutes before a meal, for a period of 4–8 weeks to allow the mucosa time to heal** [S1].

**If symptoms persist after 8 weeks, it is recommended to check adherence and dosing, before reassessing the
need for continued treatment** [S1].

### ⚠️ Alarm symptoms — refer for gastroscopy

> **Patients should be referred for gastroscopy if they are experiencing alarm symptoms such as anaemia,
> dysphagia, odynophagia, haematemesis, melaena, vomiting or weight loss, particularly in older adults** [S1].

## Duration: eight weeks is the line

| Term | Indications [S1] |
|---|---|
| **Short-term (≤8 weeks)** | **Helicobacter pylori eradication** · **treatment of aspirin or NSAID-related gastric and duodenal ulcers in patients at low risk** · **GORD** · **dyspepsia** |
| **Long-term (>8 weeks)** | **Barrett's oesophagus** · **Zollinger–Ellison syndrome** · **clinically significant severe erosive oesophagitis (LA grade C/D)** · **peptic strictures** · **oesophageal scleroderma** · **eosinophilic oesophagitis** · **gastroprotection from NSAIDs or aspirin in patients at high risk of GI bleed** · **prevention of pulmonary fibrosis progression** |

**High bleeding risk** is defined by the cited American guidance as **age >60 years, severe medical
comorbidity, using multiple NSAIDs or aspirin, taking and antithrombotic or oral corticosteroid** [S1].

**When patients have a clear and current indication for long-term therapy, then the benefits are likely to
outweigh any risk of harm. These patients are not candidates for deprescribing** [S1].

⚠️ **Apart from patients with Zollinger–Ellison syndrome, there is a dearth of evidence to support the use of
double-dose PPIs when compared with maintenance-dose PPIs** [S1].

## Agents and doses available in Australia

| Tier | Agents [S1] |
|---|---|
| **Initial therapy, high-dose** | **Esomeprazole 20 mg, 40 mg** · **Lansoprazole 30 mg** · **Omeprazole 20 mg** · **Pantoprazole 40 mg** · **Rabeprazole 20 mg** |
| **Maintenance therapy, low-dose** | **Lansoprazole 15 mg** · **Omeprazole 10 mg** · **Pantoprazole 20 mg** · **Rabeprazole 10 mg** |

⚠️ Note the asymmetry: **esomeprazole appears only in the high-dose tier** in this source's list.

## Adverse events, with the numbers

**Short-term**: **headache, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, abdominal pain, constipation and flatulence** [S1].
**Observational studies report short-term PPI use is associated with an increase in the risk of acute
interstitial nephritis** [S1].

**Long-term**, from observational studies [S1]:

| Outcome | Association |
|---|---|
| **Enteric bacterial infections (eg *Clostridium difficile*)** | **OR 1.74; 95% CI 1.47, 2.85** |
| **Fractures** | **OR 1.33; 95% CI 1.15, 1.54** |
| **Community acquired pneumonia** | **HR 1.82; 95% CI 1.27, 2.54** |
| **Reduced magnesium** | **OR 1.43; 95% CI 1.08, 1.88** |
| **Reduced vitamin B12** | **HR 1.83; 95% CI 1.36, 2.46** |

⚠️ **However, it is not possible to directly infer causation from these studies** [S1].

**And the trial evidence is more modest than the observational signal**:

> **A large randomised controlled trial examined adverse events associated with PPI when compared with
> placebo use over three years. An increased risk of enteric infections was observed among PPI users
> (absolute risk = 0.4%); however, no other differences in adverse events were observed between groups.
> While this was a large study, it may have been underpowered for detecting rare adverse events** [S1].

⚠️ **Supplements including vitamin B12, calcium or vitamin D should be initiated in accordance with
evidence-based guidelines regardless of PPI use** [S1] — the PPI is not itself the indication to supplement.

**Patients with newly diagnosed vitamin B12 deficiency, enteric infections or fractures should have their
condition investigated, including a review of the necessity of their long-term PPI treatment** [S1].

## How to deprescribe

**Two different deprescribing approaches have shown success in clinical trials** [S1]:

1. **Gradual dose tapering to the lowest available dose for a defined period of time prior to discontinuation (eg 4–8 weeks or shorter depending on patient preference)**
2. **PPI discontinuation and switching to pro re nata (PRN; 'as needed') treatment**

**A third approach is discontinuing the PPI and switching to an H2 antagonist.** ⚠️ **The likelihood of
symptoms returning has been shown to be greater following a switch to a H2 antagonist when compared with dose
tapering or switching to PRN treatment** [S1].

**Some healthcare providers find it practical to commence gradual dose tapering prior to discontinuation
followed by switching to PRN treatment** [S1]. **Non-pharmacological interventions include changes in
lifestyle factors such as diet, weight and avoiding stress** [S1].

## ⚠️ Rebound acid hypersecretion

> **Approximately 80% of patients with GORD remain symptom free after gradual dose tapering or
> discontination** [S1].

> ⚠️ **However, abrupt cessation can precipitate rebound acid hypersecretion in 10–50% of patients, which can
> be mistaken for a return of upper gastrointestinal symptoms, even in healthy adults without
> gastrointestinal disorders** [S1].

**This is the trap the guideline is built to prevent**: the symptom that looks like relapse, and restarts the
drug, may be caused by stopping it.

**The clinical significance of rebound acid hypersecretion remains unclear, and while rebound acid
hypersecretion is not necessarily a failure of PPI deprescribing, it can be concerning for patients** [S1].

**Management**: **gradual dose tapering and switching to PRN use have been shown to be appropriate and
effective ways of managing rebound acid hypersecretion** [S1]. ⚠️ **Alternate-day low-dose PPI use before
discontinuation has been shown to be more effective than abrupt cessation for managing rebound acid
hypersecretion** [S1].

**Patients should be reassured that rebound acid hypersecretion may only last a few days and can often be
adequately managed with PRN PPIs or non-prescription antacids or H2 antagonists** [S1].

## The conversation

**When initiating a PPI, it is prudent to provide information about the intended treatment duration and
follow-up** [S1] — the deprescribing problem is created at the moment of prescribing.

**After confirming the patient's symptoms have resolved, it is important to explain that the gastrointestinal
mucosa is likely to have healed, and they are a good candidate for deprescribing** [S1].

**Engaging patients and carers in shared decision making increases deprescribing success** [S1]. **A useful
phrase to gain patient acceptance is to talk about 'a trial of deprescribing'** [S1].
