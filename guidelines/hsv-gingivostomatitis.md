# HSV gingivostomatitis in children

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** primary herpetic gingivostomatitis in infants and children beyond the neonatal period: analgesia, hydration, aciclovir and red flags. The general counterpart is `mouth-and-throat-conditions` (herpes stomatitis, no antiviral regimen); see also `dehydration-children` and `meningitis-and-encephalitis-children`.

> ✅ **OPEN AND QUOTED.** 20 of 20 claims quoted verbatim from the **RCH Melbourne Clinical Practice Guideline *HSV Gingivostomatitis*** (Last updated November 2025); **34 fragments re-checked by machine. 3 quoted doses.**

> **Mainly supportive: analgesia and hydration.** ⚠️ **Immunocompromised children get IV aciclovir.** Neonatal HSV is not covered.

---

## Recognise it

- **Usually self-limiting, resolving without complications**; management centres on hydration and analgesia. [S1]
- **Typical age 6 months–5 years**, though older children and adolescents are affected too. **Neonatal HSV is severe and not covered here.** [S1]
- **Onset is about one week after contact with a shedder**, after a prodrome of fever, anorexia, malaise and headache. **Lesions heal in about 10–14 days (up to 3 weeks if severe).** [S1]
- **Vesicles on the buccal mucosa, tongue, gums, hard palate, pharynx, lips and perioral skin**; yellow with a red halo once ruptured; they bleed easily and may crust black. [S1]
- **Differentials:** hand, foot and mouth disease · herpangina · aphthous ulcers · Stevens-Johnson syndrome · Behçet syndrome · RIME. [S1]

## Red flags and complications

- ⚠️ **Lethargy, drowsiness, focal neurology or altered behaviour: think HSV encephalitis.** [S1]
- **Dehydration is the most common complication and the usual reason for admission.** Painful vesicles can cause refusal to drink. [S1]
- **Other complications:** herpetic whitlow (thumb-suckers), herpetic keratitis, oesophagitis, pneumonitis, lip adhesions, secondary infection. **Examine the eyes and nervous system.** [S1]
- **Consider secondary infection if the child worsens (e.g. new fever) after a period of gradual recovery.** [S1]

## Diagnosis

- **Diagnosis is clinical.** Where confirmation is needed (immunocompromised host or contact), send a flocked lesion swab for PCR; **serology is rarely helpful.** [S1]

## Analgesia and hydration

- **Offer analgesia to all: paracetamol and ibuprofen.** Severe pain may need admission and oral opioids. [S1]
- **Topical anaesthetics are commonly used but unproven** (e.g. Xylocaine Viscous, lignocaine gel 2%, Mucosoothe); under 3 years, apply with a cotton swab. [S1]
- **Mild or no dehydration: most can go home without a fluid trial**, with advice and scheduled review. [S1]
- **Moderate dehydration: consider an ORS trial of 10–20 mL/kg over 1 hour**, in small frequent volumes after analgesia. Severe dehydration or failed rehydration may need NG or IV fluids. [S1]

## Antiviral treatment

- ⚠️ **Immunocompromised: aciclovir 10 mg/kg IV 8-hourly until no new lesions.** [S1]
- **Immunocompetent children generally need no antiviral.** Within 72 hours of onset, with severe pain or dehydration, **consider aciclovir 10 mg/kg (max 400 mg) orally 5 times a day until no new lesions.** [S1]
- **Topical aciclovir is not effective.** [S1]

## Other care, escalation and discharge

- **Exclude from childcare or school until the sores have dried** if the child cannot control oral secretions. Barrier cream on the lips can prevent adhesions. [S1]
- **Involve the paediatric team for:** inadequate hydration · immunocompromise · severe pain, keratitis, pneumonitis, eczema herpeticum or encephalitis · a need for IV aciclovir. [S1]
- **Discharge when drinking enough to stay hydrated**, or when follow-up is arranged. [S1]
