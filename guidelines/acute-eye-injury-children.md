# Acute eye injury in children

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** assessment and ED management of blunt, chemical, thermal and superficial eye trauma in children: globe checks, corneal abrasion and foreign body, chemical burn irrigation, lid laceration and referral. Open-globe care is in `penetrating-eye-injury-children`; the non-traumatic red eye is in `acute-red-eye-children`.

> ✅ **OPEN AND QUOTED.** 22 of 22 claims quoted verbatim from the **RCH Melbourne Clinical Practice Guideline *Acute eye injury*** (Last updated October 2022); **44 fragments re-checked by machine. 4 quoted doses.**

> ⚠️ **Suspected penetrating injury: stop examining, shield the eye, nil by mouth, refer urgently to ophthalmology.** Chemical burns, especially alkali, need immediate irrigation.

---

## Recognise the sight-threatening injury

- **Consider a serious eye injury in any child with a painful eye or blurred vision.** [S1]
- **Sight-threatening:** ruptured globe · retrobulbar haematoma · intraocular or corneal foreign body · large hyphaema · retinal detachment · chemical or thermal corneal burn. **Alkalis penetrate deeper** and can burn later. [S1]
- **Ask about high-velocity projectiles or chemicals** — air guns, lawn mowers, power tools, hammering, motor vehicle accidents. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Signs of globe rupture or laceration:** severe loss of vision or red reflex · loss of ocular motility · asymmetric irregular pupil · hyphaema · distorted globe · localised conjunctival haemorrhage or chemosis. [S1]
- **Manage life-threatening injuries first**, and consider head and C-spine injury before the eye. [S1]

## Examine

- **Give adequate analgesia**; the younger child may need procedural sedation. **Get senior or ophthalmology help early** if a distressed child cannot be examined. [S1]
- **Topical tetracaine 0.5% or 1%:** one drop anaesthetises after 20 seconds and lasts 10–20 minutes (longer with 1%). ⚠️ **Do not repeat doses** — epithelial toxicity delays corneal healing. [S1]

## Chemical and thermal burns

- ⚠️ **Urgent, copious irrigation after topical anaesthetic, including under the top lid.** Sedation or urgent GA may be needed; remove particulate alkali completely with a cotton bud. [S1]
- **Irrigate with 3 litres of 0.9% sodium chloride through an open giving set over about 15 minutes**, from 3–5 cm above the eye, while the child looks in each direction. [S1]
- **Continue until conjunctival pH is normal (6–8)** on universal indicator paper. **Discuss every eye burn with ophthalmology.** [S1]
- **Thermal burns: first aid comes before a complete examination** — local anaesthetic and copious irrigation. [S1]

## Corneal abrasion and foreign body

- **Refer to ophthalmology** embedded or metallic foreign bodies (do not remove them) and large, deep or central corneal foreign bodies. ⚠️ **A defect over 2 mm or deep: presume full thickness and manage as penetrating.** [S1]
- **Anaesthetise, evert the lids and stain with fluorescein**; remove a small foreign body with a moistened cotton bud (a 25 gauge needle, bevel up, only in older cooperative children at the slit lamp). [S1]
- **Defect over 2 mm:** chloramphenicol 1% ointment and double eye-pad overnight. **Under 2 mm:** chloramphenicol 0.5% drops four times a day for 5 to 7 days. [S1]
- **Review daily** (visual acuity and slit lamp) until healed; **pain beyond 48 hours or no improvement → ophthalmology.** [S1]
- **Contact lens red eye:** remove the lens, stain, swab any discharge — **Gram-negatives including *P. aeruginosa* are frequent**, so broader cover is needed. **Ulcer → urgent ophthalmology.** Start topical chloramphenicol (1% ointment or 0.5% drops) and arrange follow-up. [S1]

## Blunt orbital injury and lids

- ⚠️ **Retrobulbar haematoma** — tense proptosis, limited eye movements, reduced acuity, RAPD: **emergent ophthalmology referral; may need urgent decompression.** [S1]
- **Orbital blow-out fracture** (pain, diplopia especially on upward gaze, cheek numbness, lid crepitus): **CT orbit and ophthalmology/maxillofacial referral.** [S1]
- **Treat a lid laceration as penetrating until proven otherwise.** Superficial ones away from the margin can be sutured; **any lid-margin laceration is repaired by an ophthalmologist.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Subconjunctival haemorrhage:** localised may mean penetration; if its posterior edge cannot be seen, consider orbital or base-of-skull fracture; **in infants it may signal non-accidental injury.** [S1]

## Escalation and discharge

- **Consult ophthalmology for** a sight-threatening condition · a large or hard-to-remove corneal foreign body · a distressed child who needs examination · retinal photography for suspected abuse. [S1]
- **Discharge when** the eye examination is normal, **or** sight-threatening conditions are excluded **and** minor injuries are treated **and** follow-up or early-review criteria are given. [S1]
