# Allergic rhinitis

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-22 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults and children. Diagnosis, aeroallergen minimisation, pharmacotherapy, immunotherapy.

> **Australian primary source.** ASCIA (Australasian Society of Clinical Immunology and Allergy).
> ***Allergic Rhinitis Clinical Update***, content updated 2024 [S1]. **The peak professional body of
> clinical immunology/allergy specialists in Australia and New Zealand.**

**AMH topics closed by this guideline:** *Allergic rhinitis*, *Rhinitis*.

## The most common allergic disorder in the country, and undertreated

> **Almost one in four Australians (23.9% of the population) had allergic rhinitis as stated in the
> Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) National Health Survey 2023** [S1].

ASCIA is blunt about the care gap: allergic rhinitis **is often underdiagnosed, undertreated, and
sub-optimally self-treated** [S1]. It **can have a significant impact on sleep, concentration, learning and
daily function, and affect childhood behaviour and development** [S1].

**Most common between 15–54 years of age, peaking between 35 and 44. Children are less likely (10%)** [S1].

## ⚠️ Treat the nose to treat the lung

> **United airway disease is the concept that allergic rhinitis and asthma are upper and lower respiratory
> tract manifestations of the same inflammatory process** [S1].

> **50–80% of patients with asthma have allergic rhinitis. 20–30% of patients with allergic rhinitis have
> asthma** [S1].

**Allergic rhinitis is a risk factor for subsequent asthma development**, and **effective treatment of
allergic rhinitis may improve asthma severity/control** [S1]. ASCIA's instruction is reciprocal: **patients
with either asthma or allergic rhinitis should be assessed for coexistent disease** [S1].

### Thunderstorm asthma

> ⚠️ **Thunderstorm asthma is usually due to thunderstorms with rapid changes in wind, temperature and
> humidity. This causes pollen grains to absorb moisture, burst open and release large amounts of small
> pollen allergen particles. These particles penetrate the small airways of the lung, which can be fatal if
> medical treatment is delayed** [S1].

⚠️ **Not everyone affected by Australian thunderstorm asthma has previously experienced this condition.
However, they have usually had severe allergic rhinitis and are allergic to ryegrass pollen** [S1].

That is the reason a rhinitis consultation is also a risk-stratification opportunity: the people who die in a
thunderstorm asthma event are, characteristically, people whose presenting complaint was hay fever. The
advice follows — **if patients are allergic to pollen, recommend that they stay indoors during, before and
after thunderstorms in pollen seasons, or on windy days, and use preventer treatments** [S1].

## Classification drives the drug choice

**By timing of exposure** [S1]: **perennial** (indoor allergens — dust mite, animal dander, moulds);
**seasonal** (grass, weed or tree pollens, and moulds); **perennial with seasonal exacerbations**; and
**occupational**, where **symptoms improve when away from the workplace**.

**By duration and severity** [S1]:

| | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Intermittent** | **<4 days/week or <4 weeks** |
| **Persistent** | **>4 days/week or >4 weeks** |
| **Mild** | **Normal sleep, no impairment of daily activities, normal work, or school performance** |
| **Moderate–severe** | **One or more of: abnormal sleep, impairment of activities, abnormal work or school performance, troublesome symptoms** |

## Pharmacotherapy

> **Intranasal corticosteroids sprays or combined intranasal/antihistamine sprays are recommended first line
> treatments** [S1].

**Non-sedating antihistamines** — first line for **intermittent mild** disease or **in conjunction with other
therapies** [S1]. Oral onset is **1–2 hours**; intranasal is **within 30 minutes** and **may be used as a
rescue medication to provide immediate relief of symptoms** [S1]. ⚠️ **Non-sedating antihistamines are
recommended. Sedating antihistamines are not recommended** [S1]. Against nasal congestion the benefit is
**limited**, and **intranasal corticosteroids (INCS) are more effective in reducing nasal congestion** [S1].

**Intranasal corticosteroids** — first line for **persistent and/or moderate to severe allergic rhinitis and
treatment failures with antihistamines alone** [S1]. ⚠️ **Continuous use is needed for long term benefit** and
**use on an as-needed basis is less effective** [S1]. They reduce **itchy watery eyes, sneezing, itching,
runny nose and nasal congestion**, and are described as a **cost effective reduction of symptoms** [S1].

⚠️ **Different brands of INCS vary in strength and efficacy**, and **different intranasal corticosteroids
often have different minimum age restrictions** [S1].

### INCS safety — two cautions that are easy to miss

> **Topical corticosteroids such as INCS do not cause atrophy**, and there is **minimal potential for
> systemic absorption, when used in recommended doses** [S1].

But:

> ⚠️ **Whilst systemic absorption of INCS is negligible, growth of children and adolescents taking
> corticosteroids by any route should be monitored** [S1].

> ⚠️ **INCS must be used with caution in patients with pre-existing glaucoma and/or cataracts. Rare cases of
> cataracts, glaucoma and increased intraocular pressure have been reported following use of INCS** [S1].

This lands directly on the **cataract** guideline in this compendium: a first-line, over-the-counter,
long-term nasal spray carries a stated caution in exactly the population that standard is written for.

### Adjuncts, and what not to reach for

**Saline nasal irrigation** — **usually well tolerated, effective in reducing rhinitis symptoms, safe and
inexpensive**; it **clears aeroallergens and inflammatory mucus but does not replace pharmacotherapy**.
⚠️ **Large volume (>60 mL) and positive pressure devices are more effective than simple sprays (<1 mL)** [S1].

**Intranasal ipratropium** — **only decreases watery rhinorrhoea**; an adjunct **for persistent
rhinorrhoea** [S1].

⚠️ **Oral leukotriene antagonists** — **no additional benefit if used in combination with antihistamines**,
and **the combination of leukotriene antagonists (such as montelukast) and antihistamines are no more
effective than intranasal corticosteroids alone**. **There is no Australian or New Zealand government subsidy
for use of leukotriene antagonists for patients with allergic rhinitis alone** [S1].

⚠️ **Decongestants** — short term only, **3–5 days**, to reduce severe congestion so that INCS can be
administered effectively. **Chronic use of intranasal decongestants may lead to rebound nasal obstruction,
called rhinitis medicamentosa** [S1]. **Decongestants should not be used in patients with hypertension,
coronary artery disease, prostatism or glaucoma. They should not be used in pregnancy** [S1].

⚠️ **Systemic corticosteroids** — **brief courses of oral corticosteroids (3–7 days) are rarely indicated**;
if used as rescue, **only up to a maximum limit of 2 or 3 short courses in a 12-month period**.
**Depocorticosteroids are not recommended due to short duration of benefit and potential for local
(subdermal and dermal atrophy) and systemic side effects.** **Patients requiring oral corticosteroids for
allergic rhinitis should be referred to a clinical immunology/allergy specialist for assessment** [S1].

**Athletes**: ⚠️ **pseudoephedrine used in some decongestants is subject to certain restrictions** — check
with **Sport Integrity Australia** or **Drug Free Sport New Zealand** [S1].

### The four-week rule

> **Trial of pharmacotherapy initiated by primary care physicians and maintained for at least 4 weeks is
> recommended. Then consider referral to a specialist if no improvement** [S1].

**When symptoms improve, pharmacotherapy doses may be reduced** [S1].

## Eyes

**Allergic conjunctivitis presents with watery, itchy eyes and may occur in conjunction with allergic
rhinitis or in isolation** [S1]. Non-drug measures: **flush allergen from eyes (saline washes, liquid-tear
preparations)** and **cool compresses** [S1]. **Oral antihistamines or topical mast cell stabilisers may be
used to control itchy/watery eyes**, and **intranasal corticosteroids can reduce ocular symptoms of allergic
rhinitis** [S1].

⚠️ **Ocular corticosteroids should only be prescribed in consultation with, and regular review by an
ophthalmologist** [S1].

## Chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps

**Defined as inflammation of the paranasal sinuses for more than 12 weeks**, and **present in 2–4% of the
adult population** [S1].

⚠️ **Aspirin hypersensitivity is common in people with polyposis and asthma (Samter's triad)** [S1].
**Consider polyps if a patient presents with persistent nasal obstruction and/or anosmia** [S1].

Treatment [S1]: **nasal irrigation is a widespread first line treatment**; **intranasal corticosteroids are
safe and effective for long term use**; **a short course of antibiotics may have a role to play in non-Type 2
CRSwNP, but patient selection is important**; **biologics can have positive outcomes**; and **endoscopic
sinus surgery plays a significant role**, being **safe, reduces symptom burden, and improves quality of
life**.

## Surgery in rhinitis generally

⚠️ **Surgery plays a limited role in the management of rhinitis, except for CRSwNP** [S1]. **Turbinate
reduction and re-modelling of the nasal airway can improve medically refractory nasal obstruction**, while
**vidian neurectomy (division of autonomic nasal nerves) is not indicated for allergic rhinitis** [S1].

## Allergen immunotherapy

> **Allergen immunotherapy, also known as desensitisation** … **is usually administered for 3–5 years to
> produce durable effects** … and **should only be initiated by medical specialists with training in allergy,
> following a confirmed diagnosis** [S1].

**On average there may be a 50% reduction in symptoms and/or medication need** [S1], and the possible
additional benefits are the disease-modifying ones: **reduced risk of new sensitisation … in children**,
**reduced risk of progression from allergic rhinitis to asthma in children**, and **reduced asthma
exacerbation** [S1].

**Available aeroallergen preparations in Australia and New Zealand: house dust mite; pollens (grass, tree,
and weeds); animal dander; moulds** [S1].

**Refer** when symptoms are **severe and/or persistent**, when medications **are associated with intolerable
side effects** or **do not adequately control symptoms**, when the patient **desires to reduce use**, when
**allergen avoidance is difficult, as in the case of pollen**, or for **occupational allergy** [S1].

## ⚠️ Testing does not make the diagnosis

> **Results of allergy tests should always be considered with a patient's clinical history. Positive tests do
> not automatically prove the allergen is causing the symptoms** [S1].
