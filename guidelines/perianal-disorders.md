## ⚠️ Confirm the diagnosis — "haemorrhoids" is what patients call everything

> ⚠️ **It is important to confirm diagnosis as patients commonly describe any anorectal symptom as
> haemorrhoids** [A1].

**Anal fissure is the second most common anorectal problem after haemorrhoids, affecting both genders
equally**, with **frequency in the general population of 10–15%, although the actual number might be higher
due to patients not presenting** [S1].

### ⚠️ The honesty marker

> ⚠️ **A 2019 study of colorectal surgeons in Australia and New Zealand showed genuine clinical uncertainty
> in approximately two-thirds of clinical management questions** [S1].

**That is the specialists, about their own field.** It is the frame for everything below.

## Anal fissure

**A superficial tear in the anoderm distal to the dentate line** [S1].

**Presentation:** ⚠️ **severe tearing pain with defaecation, often accompanied by spots of bright red
blood**; ⚠️ **the pain can persist for one to two hours or more afterwards, causing many patients to fear
defaecation and worsen symptoms of constipation as a result** [S1] — **a self-reinforcing loop, and the
reason treating the constipation is not optional.**

### Examination

**Diagnosis is often achieved with visual inspection. The patient might lie in the lateral decubitus
position or prone over a table flexed at the hips** [S1], with **gentle parting of the buttocks** [S1].

> ⚠️ **Digital exam is contraindicated with suspected AF due to this pain** [S1].

**If a fissure is not apparent, gently pressing on the anterior or posterior anal sphincter might produce
the characteristic pain** [S1].

| | Acute [S1] | Chronic [S1] |
|---|---|---|
| **Duration** | **six weeks or less** | beyond that |
| **Looks like** | **a fresh laceration on the sphincter** | **raised edges and exposed internal anal sphincter**; **distal sentinel tags and hypertrophied anal papilla at the internal apex might also be present** |
| **Course** | **often heals spontaneously within 4–6 weeks or with medical management** | — |
| ⚠️ **Progression** | ⚠️ **approximately 40% will progress to chronic fissures** | — |

### ⚠️ Why the location matters

**Anal fissure occurs due to localised ischaemia augmented by internal anal sphincter hypertonicity** —
which explains why **90% of all presenting AF cases** are in **the posterior midline, an area that receives
less than half of perfusion compared to the rest of the anal canal** [S1].

> ⚠️ **Most treatments focus on reducing IAS resting tone. However, fissures anterior to the midline are
> usually related to occult external sphincter injury and often lack IAS hypertonicity. These fissures
> require alternative treatment methods** [S1].

⚠️ **An anterior fissure may not respond to the standard treatment, because the standard treatment targets
a mechanism it does not have.** ⚠️ **S1 says these need alternative methods and does not say what they
are** — see *Unresolved*.

### ⚠️ Secondary anal fissure — under 1%, and it changes everything

**Often characterised by multiple fissures or fissures off midline** [S1], and associated with:

- **previous anal surgeries**
- ⚠️ **inflammatory bowel disease, especially Crohn's disease**
- **granulomatous diseases, eg tuberculosis and sarcoidosis**
- **gastrointestinal malignancies, eg colon cancer**
- ⚠️ **sexually transmitted infections — which might present with AF in addition to discharge, perianal
  ulcers or fever** [S1]

> ⚠️ **If a secondary AF is suspected, surgical therapies should be postponed pending further investigation
> and multidisciplinary involvement** [S1].

## ⚠️ The treatment ladder — and what AMH does not carry

**AMH offers conservative measures, topical anaesthetics, and rectal glyceryl trinitrate** [A1].
⚠️ **AMH's perianal topic does not mention topical calcium channel blockers or botulinum toxin at all.**
**S1 has both, with ASCRS positioning and healing rates.** ⚠️ **This is a coverage gap in A1, not a
disagreement between the sources** — they are not saying opposite things, one simply stops earlier.

### Step 1 · Conservative — and it outperforms the drug

**Increased fibre intake, sitz baths and, if necessary, stool softeners as the first-line therapy for acute
AF or chronic AF in patients who elect or are unable to have surgery** [S1] (**ASCRS**). **Avoid straining,
increase fibre and fluid intake; bulk-forming laxatives or stool softeners may also be useful** [A1].

> ⚠️ **Conservative therapy has shown a healing rate of 87% with 15 g/day of fibre supplementation. This
> therapy reduced recurrence at 16% versus 68% with placebo** [S1].

⚠️ **Read that against the glyceryl trinitrate figure below. The fibre number is the better one.**

**Conservative measures are more effective in healing acute anal fissure than chronic anal fissure** [A1].

**For pain:** **topical anaesthetics applied before passing a stool may relieve pain but have no effect on
healing**; **oral paracetamol may help relieve prolonged burning pain after defecation** [A1].

### Step 2 · Topical glyceryl trinitrate

**Releases nitric oxide, relaxing the internal anal sphincter, reducing anal pressure — which is increased
in patients with anal fissure — and improving blood flow** [A1]; **acting as an inhibitory
neurotransmitter** [S1].

| | |
|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Dose** | **a 1–1.5 cm strip of ointment inserted into the anal canal 3 times daily for at least 2 weeks; up to 4 weeks of treatment may be required for anal fissure** [A1] — ⚠️ **licensed source, in the attestation queue** |
| ⚠️ **How well** | **a Cochrane review found GTN slightly but significantly better than placebo (49% vs 37%, P <0.004), although recurrence was widespread (>50%)** [S1]. **A modest effect on healing** [A1] |
| ⚠️ **Tolerability** | **headaches, light-headedness and local irritation** — ⚠️ **their severity causes up to 20% of patients to discontinue therapy** [S1]. **Headache is dose-related** and **common** [A1] |
| ⚠️ **Contraindicated** | ⚠️ **hypotension**, and ⚠️ **treatment with avanafil, sildenafil or tadalafil** [A1] |
| **Pregnancy / breastfeeding** | **safety not established** in pregnancy; **should be safe in breastfeeding — apply a small amount after breastfeeding** [A1] |
| ⚠️ **Do not stop early** | ⚠️ **pain relief often occurs before anal fissure healing is complete; ensure adequate treatment course** [A1] |

**Adjunct:** **co-administration of GTN and topical metronidazole decreased pain with defecation during the
early stages and significantly increased healing by the end of week 6 (97.1% vs 85.2% with GTN alone,
P <0.014)** [S1]. ⚠️ **One RCT; no metronidazole dose or formulation is given, and none has been supplied.**

### Step 3 · Topical calcium channel blockers — when GTN fails or is not tolerated

**Relax the internal anal sphincter by blocking calcium influx to the cytoplasm of smooth muscle cells.
ASCRS guidelines recommend CCB when GTN therapy has failed** [S1].

> **The two most used CCBs are topical nifedipine (0.2–0.5% gel) and diltiazem (2% cream)** [S1] —
> ⚠️ **a quoted strength, machine re-checkable.** ⚠️ **No frequency and no duration are given by either
> source.**

**As effective as GTN and favoured for patients with poor reactions to GTN, as reported adverse effect
rates are much lower, with only some experiencing mild headaches and anal pruritus. Reported healing rates
vary from 65% to 96%** [S1].

### Step 4 · Botulinum toxin — when topical therapy fails

**Prevents acetylcholine release from presynaptic terminals, relaxing both the external and internal anal
sphincter for up to three months. ASCRS guidelines recommend BTA when topical therapy has failed** [S1].

⚠️ **Strongly recommended in patients at high risk of developing incontinence from surgery, including those
with Crohn's disease or a history of anal surgery** [S1]. **Adverse effects include short-term incontinence
but at lower rates than surgical methods** [S1].

⚠️ **No botulinum toxin dose, product, or injection technique is given** — see *Unresolved*.

### ⚠️ Head to head

> **One prospective study showed comparable healing rates between GTN, diltiazem and BTA of nearly 55%.
> Additionally, diltiazem and BTA had lower reported adverse effects than GTN. Long-term recurrence might
> be up to 42%, depending on method** [S1].

⚠️ **Three treatments, the same ~55% healing, and the one with the worst adverse effects is the one AMH
carries and the only one on the Australian market as a dedicated anorectal product.**

### Step 5 · Surgery

**Typically recommended at least 6–8 weeks after attempting conservative treatment, although it might be
first-line if the fissure is hyperalgesic or infected. The best results are seen with a combination of
surgical and medical management** [S1]. **Consider referral for surgery if fissure fails to heal** [A1].

| Procedure | What | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Lateral internal sphincterotomy (LIS)** | **partial incision to the IAS to relieve hypertonia**, **most often used for chronic AF** [S1] | ⚠️ **transient incontinence of flatus or stool in up to 17% of patients, with 1% experiencing faecal incontinence five years after the procedure** [S1] |
| **Fissurectomy / advancement anoplasty** | **a Cochrane review favoured LIS in uncomplicated hypertonic AF but recommended combination fissurectomy/advancement anoplasty in patients with hypotonia, diagnostic doubt, or high risk for faecal incontinence with LIS** [S1] | — |

## Haemorrhoids

**Associated with constipation, diarrhoea and prolonged straining** [A1].

> **Most cases can be managed conservatively. Give advice to avoid prolonged straining and to increase
> fibre and fluid intake. Fibre (dietary or bulk-forming laxative) is effective in relieving symptoms and
> reducing episodes of bleeding** [A1].

**Topical anorectal medications often contain a corticosteroid and/or a local anaesthetic agent.**
⚠️ **Evidence for their efficacy is anecdotal** [A1] — though **topical corticosteroids may reduce
inflammation and local anaesthetics may relieve pain, burning and itching** [A1].

**Procedural:** **sclerotherapy, rubber band ligation or haemorrhoidectomy are indicated for more severe
haemorrhoids or those unresponsive to conservative management** [A1]. **Rectal glyceryl trinitrate may
assist in wound healing and relieve pain and discomfort associated with haemorrhoidectomy; it commonly
causes headache** [A1].

## Pruritus ani

⚠️ **Treat it as a symptom, not a diagnosis.** **Consider secondary causes such as haemorrhoids, anal
fissure, infections (eg worms) or skin conditions. Heat, moisture, stress or local irritants (eg faeces,
sweat, soap, perfumes, topical products) can also cause perianal itching** [A1].

**To reduce irritation keep the area clean and dry and avoid soap, rough toilet paper and nylon clothing.
Dietary alteration may be useful if itching is related to certain foods or drinks** [A1].

**Simple emollients (eg sorbolene cream, zinc oxide ointment) applied after cleansing may help. A short
course (5–7 days) of hydrocortisone ointment may relieve any inflammation** [A1].

## ⚠️ The 7-day rule on topicals

> ⚠️ **Limit use of topical anaesthetics and corticosteroids to <7 days, as local anaesthetics may
> sensitise perianal skin and topical corticosteroids may exacerbate local infection and cause skin
> atrophy** [A1].

**Anorectal products may contain a corticosteroid and/or an anaesthetic and/or an astringent**, are
**indicated for haemorrhoids, anal fissure and pruritus ani**, are **safe in pregnancy and breastfeeding**,
and should be **used according to product information** [A1].

⚠️ **"Use according to product information" is the whole dose AMH gives for this class** — see *Unresolved*.
