# Oral hypoglycaemic poisoning in children

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** sulfonylurea overdose (IV glucose, octreotide, observation and daylight-only discharge) and, briefly, metformin exposure in children and adolescents. > ⚠️ **One sulfonylurea tablet can cause life-threatening hypoglycaemia in a toddler.** Observe every possible exposure for 24 hours; treat hypoglycaemia with IV glucose **and** octreotide; discharge in daylight only.

---

## Who is at risk

- ⚠️ **A single sulfonylurea tablet can cause life-threatening hypoglycaemia in a toddler** — a pill as small as 2 mg is enough. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Observe any possible sulfonylurea exposure for 24 hours**: onset of hypoglycaemia can be delayed up to 18 hours, and large overdoses may need several days of treatment. [S1]
- **Assess:** every deliberate self-poisoning or significant accidental ingestion · any symptomatic child · any ingestion of unknown quantity · **any child whose developmental age doesn't fit accidental poisoning — consider non-accidental poisoning.** [S1]
- **Metformin does not cause hypoglycaemia in normal patients**, but large overdoses can cause life-threatening lactic acidosis when there is renal or cardiac failure or a co-ingestant impairing renal perfusion. [S1]
- **Asymptomatic accidental metformin exposure needs no hospital assessment, decontamination or investigation** — including unintentional ingestions of up to 1700 mg. [S1]

## Recognise and investigate

- **Hypoglycaemia features:** autonomic (tachycardia, sweating, anxiety) and CNS (drowsiness, altered mental status, coma, seizures). [S1]
- **Investigate with serial BSLs and UEC**; consider a paracetamol level and ECG for possible co-ingestants. Insulin levels may help in sulfonylurea overdose, on toxicology advice. [S1]
- **Metformin overdose: blood gas with lactate** to confirm lactic acidosis and monitor any unwell or deteriorating patient. **Haemodialysis** resolves the acidosis and removes metformin. [S1]

## Treat sulfonylurea hypoglycaemia

- ⚠️ **Treat sulfonylurea overdose with symptomatic hypoglycaemia with both IV dextrose and octreotide.** [S1]
- **If hypoglycaemic: 10% glucose 5 mL/kg IV bolus, then a 10% glucose infusion at 1–2 mL/kg/hour**; check BSL hourly, send bloods including serum insulin, and start octreotide. [S1]
- **Octreotide, children:** 1 microgram/kg IV bolus (max 50 microgram), then 1 microgram/kg/hour continuous infusion (up to 25 microgram) **for at least 24 hours**. [S1]
- **Octreotide, adolescents or adults:** 50 microgram IV bolus, then 25 microgram/hour infusion **for at least 24 hours**; 50 microgram SC 8-hourly is an alternative. [S1]
- **Once octreotide is running, glucose is usually no longer needed.** If hypoglycaemia recurs, **correct it with glucose and double the octreotide infusion rate.** [S1]
- **Charcoal only for a massive ingestion within 1 hour, or after airway protection** — discuss with a toxicologist. [S1]

## Stopping octreotide and discharge

- **Stop octreotide only when all are met:** 10% glucose stopped at least 4 hours earlier · no hypoglycaemic symptoms · bedside BSL >2.5 mmol/L for more than 4 hours · **daylight hours — never on evening or night shifts.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **After stopping, check BSL hourly for at least 12 hours before discharge.** [S1]
- **Admit any sulfonylurea exposure for at least 12–24 hours** of glucose monitoring, and consider admitting any intentional overdose. **Discharge in daylight hours only.** [S1]
- **Discharge when:** BSLs stay normal for 12 hours after octreotide stops on a normal diet · plasma insulin (if available) is normal 6 hours after stopping · daylight hours only for sulfonylurea exposure. [S1]
- **Before discharging an adolescent after a deliberate ingestion**, assess self-harm risk and other drug and alcohol use. [S1]
