## ⚠️ The PSA trap

> ⚠️ **After 6 months of dutasteride or 1 year of finasteride (5 mg daily), PSA concentration is reduced by
> an average of 50%** — with further reductions possible on longer therapy, particularly with dutasteride.
> ⚠️ **Take this into consideration when measuring PSA** [A1].
>
> ⚠️ **ANY increase in PSA concentration after 6 months — EVEN WITHIN THE NORMAL RANGE — should be assessed
> to exclude prostate cancer** [A1].

**Six months after stopping treatment, PSA concentrations have generally returned to baseline** [A1].

⚠️ **A man on a 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor has a PSA that reads about half its true value. A "normal"
result is not reassuring, and a rise within the normal range is the signal.**

**From the other direction:** ⚠️ **PSA is elevated (>4 µg/L) in 70% of acute bacterial prostatitis
cases** [S1] — **it is not a diagnostic criterion for ABP, rather it is useful to indicate prostatic
involvement** [S1]. **An inflamed prostate raises PSA; a treated prostate lowers it. Neither reading means
what it appears to.**

# Benign prostatic hyperplasia

**Incidence increases with age.** **Voiding symptoms — hesitancy, straining, weak or intermittent urine
flow, sensation of incomplete emptying — and/or bladder storage symptoms — urgency, frequency,
nocturia** [A1]. **Complications such as acute urinary retention or renal impairment may also occur** [A1].

> ⚠️ **The severity of symptoms is not related to prostate size** [A1].
>
> ⚠️ **Prostate size is the most important factor influencing drug choice** [A1].

⚠️ **Both are true, and together they are the whole of BPH prescribing: size does not tell you how bad it
is, but it does tell you what to use.**

## Before drugs

**Assess symptom severity using a scoring system such as the International Prostate Symptom Score** —
**it can also be used to monitor progress and response to treatment** [A1]. **Assess prostate size** [A1].

⚠️ **Look for aggravating factors: constipation, diuretics, or drugs with anticholinergic or sedating
effects** [A1].

**Advise reducing caffeine and alcohol intake, bladder training, and reducing fluid intake at times when
urinary frequency is inconvenient (eg at night)** [A1].

⚠️ **Patients with uncomplicated BPH not bothered by symptoms may be managed with watchful waiting —
education, reassurance, lifestyle measures and periodic monitoring** [A1]. **Consider drug treatment when
symptoms are troublesome, it is preferred by the patient, and surgery is not indicated** [A1].

**Symptomatic relief requires continuous treatment** [A1].

## Drug choice, by prostate size

| | Selective alpha-blockers | 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors |
|---|---|---|
| **Who** | ⚠️ **effective regardless of prostate size** [A1] | ⚠️ **an option when prostate >30–40 mL or PSA >1.4 nanograms/mL** [A1] |
| **How fast** | ⚠️ **improve symptoms within 48 hours, full effect in 4–6 weeks** [A1] | ⚠️ **may take 6 MONTHS before symptoms improve; full effect after 12–18 months** [A1] |
| **What else** | **improve urinary flow rates** | **reduce prostate size and rates of acute urinary retention and surgery** |

**Combination:** ⚠️ **consider when prostate >30–40 mL and rapid relief of troublesome symptoms is
required** [A1]. **Optimal duration is uncertain** — **there is some evidence the selective alpha-blocker
can be stopped after 6–12 months of combination treatment in most patients without worsening urinary
symptoms**, though **some patients, especially those with severe baseline symptoms, may benefit from
continuing** [A1].

**Also:** ⚠️ **men with a prostate >30–40 mL who are NOT bothered by their symptoms may be offered a
5-alpha-reductase inhibitor to reduce the risk of disease progression** [A1].

**Tadalafil** is **indicated for LUTS in males with BPH and may be beneficial in men who also have erectile
dysfunction** — ⚠️ **long-term safety and efficacy data are lacking** [A1].

⚠️ **Saw palmetto is no more effective than placebo in improving LUTS of BPH** (Cochrane systematic
review) [A1].

**Specialists sometimes add an anticholinergic to a selective alpha-blocker in men who have continuing
storage symptoms (bladder overactivity)** [A1].

**Surgery** is appropriate **when symptoms are more severe, when drug treatment is ineffective or not
tolerated, and when there are complications such as urinary retention** [A1].

## Selective alpha-blockers

**Alfuzosin, prazosin, silodosin, tamsulosin** — **block alpha1 receptors, relaxing smooth muscle in the
bladder neck and prostate** [A1].

**All appear similarly effective for BPH.** ⚠️ **Silodosin, tamsulosin and alfuzosin have less effect on BP
than prazosin and may be a better choice**; **dosage titration is required with prazosin** [A1].
⚠️ **Silodosin, followed by tamsulosin, has the highest incidence of abnormal ejaculation** [A1].

**Prazosin has a short duration of action and needs to be taken twice daily; alfuzosin, silodosin and
tamsulosin can be taken once daily** [A1].

⚠️ **Stop if there is no improvement in symptoms of BPH after 4–6 weeks of maximal treatment** [A1].

### ⚠️ Floppy iris syndrome — tell the ophthalmologist

> **Selective alpha-blockers are associated with a risk of intra-operative floppy iris syndrome during
> cataract surgery which may increase rates of postoperative ocular complications.** ⚠️ **INTERRUPTING
> TREATMENT DOES NOT APPEAR TO REDUCE THIS RISK; it may be necessary to modify surgical technique.**
> ⚠️ **These complications have occurred even after these drugs were ceased** [A1].
>
> **Tell your ophthalmologist you are taking, or have taken, this medicine if you are going to have
> cataract surgery** [A1].

⚠️ **Stopping the drug before surgery does not help, so the only useful action is telling the surgeon — and
the history matters even years later.**

### Starting safely

⚠️ **First-dose hypotension, orthostatic hypotension and dizziness** are common — **all less frequent with
alfuzosin, silodosin and tamsulosin** [A1]. **Take the first dose at bedtime, get up gradually from sitting
or lying, and be careful getting up during the night** [A1].

⚠️ **Add antihypertensives cautiously, particularly with prazosin, as BP may fall sharply**, and **combination
with diuretics, beta-blockers or calcium channel blockers increases the risk of first-dose
hypotension** [A1].

### Two uses beyond BPH symptoms

- ⚠️ **Giving a selective alpha-blocker (alfuzosin, tamsulosin or silodosin) daily for 2–3 days before
  catheter removal increases the rate of successful voiding in men >65 years with acute urinary retention
  due to BPH** [A1]
- **Although not a marketed indication, selective alpha-blockers are used for up to 4 weeks in selected
  patients with ureteral stones as they may help stone passage** [A1]

## 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors

**Dutasteride, dutasteride with tamsulosin, finasteride** [A1].

**Common: impotence, decreased libido, ejaculation disorder including decreased ejaculate volume** [A1].

**Dutasteride has a half-life of 3–5 weeks against finasteride's 6 hours and can still be detected 4–6
months after stopping.** ⚠️ **Although dutasteride reduces serum dihydrotestosterone by about 90% against
70% with finasteride, limited evidence suggests no major differences between them in prostate size
reduction, symptom improvement, urinary flow rate or adverse effects** [A1].

> ⚠️ **Women who are or may become pregnant should not handle capsules or broken or crushed tablets without
> gloves — risk of feminisation of a male fetus** [A1].

⚠️ **Breast cancer has been reported in men taking finasteride or dutasteride; the association is unclear;
monitor for any changes in breast tissue** [A1].

# Prostatitis

**Four types, by the NIH classification** [S1]:

| Type | Name | Clinical features [S1] | Share [S1] |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Acute bacterial** | **acute irritative and obstructive urinary symptoms, systemic symptoms and pain** | **up to 10%** |
| **2** | **Chronic bacterial** | **symptoms of ABP or asymptomatic; infection persists beyond a 3-month period** | **<5%** |
| ⚠️ **3** | **Chronic prostatitis / chronic pelvic pain syndrome** | **obstructive urinary symptoms without any bacterial infection** | ⚠️ **>90%** |
| **4** | **Asymptomatic inflammatory** | **no urinary or systemic symptoms despite prostate inflammation** | **rare** |

⚠️ **More than nine in ten men with prostatitis do not have an infection.** A1 agrees in words:
**chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome is not associated with UTI but has similar symptoms…
and is much more common** [A1].

## Acute bacterial prostatitis

**Associated with an acute UTI (may follow trauma or bladder outflow obstruction) with similar pathogens,
eg *E. coli*, *Klebsiella*, *Enterococci* spp. A serious, severe illness; cure is likely, but it may
progress to chronic prostatitis** [A1].

**A clinical diagnosis** [S1]. ⚠️ **On digital rectal examination the prostate gland is tender, hot and
boggy**, with **poorly localised pain — perineal, external genitalia, tip of penis, rectum, suprapubic
region to lower back** [S1].

> ⚠️ **The pre- and post-prostate massage test is CONTRAINDICATED in suspected acute bacterial prostatitis
> because of the risk of bacteraemia** — **reserved only for suspected chronic bacterial prostatitis** [S1].
> **Avoid prostatic massage in acute prostatitis** [A1].

**Investigations:** **urine culture with susceptibility testing**; **first void urine PCR for *Chlamydia
trachomatis* or *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* if STI-related**; ⚠️ **transrectal or bladder ultrasound only if
acute urinary retention is suspected** [S1]. **Obtain urine and, where appropriate, blood for
culture** [A1].

### Treatment

| Source | First line |
|---|---|
| **S1 (2025)** | ⚠️ **trimethoprim 300 mg orally daily for 14 days**, if **haemodynamically stable without signs of severe infection** — **choice guided by susceptibility testing and patient factors including allergies** |
| **A1 (2026)** | **an oral antibacterial effective against most urinary pathogens, eg trimethoprim or cefalexin. Treat for 14 days** |

⚠️ **The two agree on 14 days; S1 supplies the milligrams and it is quoted.**

**Also:** **ensure adequate hydration, rest and analgesia (paracetamol or an NSAID); consider bowel function
as constipation may increase pain** [A1]. **Supportive: antipyretics, anti-inflammatories, alpha-blockers,
stool softeners** [S1].

⚠️ **Refer urgently for IV therapy if** [S1]: **severely unwell or signs of sepsis; recent transrectal or
transurethral prostatic manipulation; urinary retention (also needs catheterisation); unable to tolerate
oral antibiotics; antibiotic resistance.**

**A1's severe regimen:** **IV antibacterials (eg amoxicillin/ampicillin plus gentamicin), changing to oral
when possible; total treatment duration usually 4 weeks** [A1].

**After the course:** **repeat urine culture to check the infection has cleared, then investigate for any
contributing structural abnormalities** [A1]. ⚠️ **S1 adds: give an additional 2–4 weeks if symptoms
persist, repeat cultures grow, or PSA remains elevated** [S1].

### Complications [S1]

| | Features | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **Chronic bacterial prostatitis** | **symptoms persist, or asymptomatic** | ⚠️ **antibiotics for 6 weeks** — **lower tissue penetration because of less inflammation** |
| **Chronic pelvic pain syndrome** | **penile, inguinal pain, pudendal discomfort, prostate hyperplasia, bladder outlet obstruction** | **alpha-1 blockers, phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors**. ⚠️ **Can impact mental health and functioning, causing insomnia and depression** |
| **Epididymitis** | **urethral damage, genital swelling, tenderness** | ⚠️ **oral quinolone for 14–21 days**; **ultrasound to rule out testicular torsion**; ⚠️ **if untreated can contribute to infertility** |
| **Prostate abscess** | **elevated inflammation markers despite antibiotic treatment** | **transrectal ultrasound**; ⚠️ **if >1 cm, surgical drainage** |

## ⚠️ Chronic bacterial prostatitis — the sources differ on duration

| Source | Duration |
|---|---|
| ⚠️ **A1 (AMH, July 2026)** | ⚠️ **treat for 4 WEEKS** with an antibacterial that penetrates the prostate well — **ciprofloxacin, trimethoprim, or trimethoprim with sulfamethoxazole** |
| ⚠️ **S1 (AJGP, October 2025)** | ⚠️ **antibiotics for 6 WEEKS** |

⚠️ **Both are current. This guideline states both and does not average them.** They agree on the mechanism:
**as inflammation is not intense, penetration of antibacterials into prostate tissue may be poor** [A1] /
**lower tissue penetration because of less inflammation** [S1].

**Doxycycline may be used if intracellular bacteria, eg Chlamydia, are suspected or cultured** [A1].

⚠️ **Avoid nitrofurantoin — it penetrates prostatic tissue poorly** [A1].

## Chronic prostatitis / chronic pelvic pain syndrome

> ⚠️ **Although symptoms usually improve over time, chronic prostatitis frequently relapses and is difficult
> to manage. NO TREATMENT HAS PROVEN BENEFIT** [A1].

**A trial of** [A1]:
- **an antibacterial (as for chronic bacterial prostatitis) may be worthwhile, especially in recently
  diagnosed patients, as there may be undiagnosed bacterial infection**
- **adequate analgesia (eg NSAID) and relief of constipation**
- **one of the selective alpha-blockers may ease pain and improve urinary symptoms**
- ⚠️ **quercetin 500 mg twice daily may relieve symptoms, particularly pain (based on small
  placebo-controlled studies)**

⚠️ **Quercetin should NOT be used during quinolone treatment as it may decrease antibacterial
efficacy** [A1] — **and a quinolone is one of the antibacterials offered for the same condition.**

**S1 adds phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors** for chronic pelvic pain syndrome [S1].

**In chronic prostatitis, exclude other causes such as UTI, prostatic abscess, epididymitis, urethritis,
and cancer** [A1]. ⚠️ **Refer men with unexplained haematuria or long-standing chronic prostatitis to a
specialist** [A1].
