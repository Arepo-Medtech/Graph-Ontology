# Infantile spasms

## Recognise it

- **Infantile spasms are sudden, brief, bilateral, symmetric contractions of the neck, trunk and limbs, occurring in clusters.** They often belong to one of the commonest severe infant epilepsy syndromes. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Prompt diagnosis and treatment are critical to limit developmental harm** — over 80% have developmental delay, with high rates of ongoing epilepsy. [S1]
- **Peak onset 3–7 months, but any age in childhood.** Most have Infantile Epileptic Spasms Syndrome: spasms, hypsarrhythmia on EEG and developmental arrest or regression (formerly West syndrome). [S1]
- **Suggestive features:** clusters, usually shortly after waking; sudden flexor or extensor spasms of the whole body; **may be subtle — head nodding or facial/ocular movements**, especially early; usually but not always symmetric. **Ask parents to record episodes.** [S1]
- **Differentials:** non-epileptic episodes (shuddering, Sandifer syndrome, sleep myoclonus), benign myoclonic epilepsy of infancy, other infantile epilepsies. [S1]

## Look for the cause

- **Common causes:** tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC), trisomy 21, focal structural abnormalities, perinatal hypoxic ischaemic encephalopathy. [S1]
- **Clues:** a known associated condition; family history of seizures or delay; **early handedness** (focal lesion); dysmorphism; **hypopigmented macules** (TSC); pre-existing delay; neonatal or early infantile seizures; rapidly increasing head circumference. [S1]

## Work-up

- ⚠️ **Early neurological consultation for every suspected case.** Strongly consider admission where it will speed investigations and treatment. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Urgent awake and sleep EEG; prompt MRI brain**; if the cause is unknown, genetic testing and a urine metabolic screen. [S1]
- **Transfer if urgent EEG, prompt MRI and neurology consultation aren't available locally.** [S1]

## Treat

- **Treatment is always neurologist-led. High-dose prednisolone is first line** — except in TSC and treatable metabolic conditions. Dose and weaning are set by the neurologist. [S1]
- **In TSC, vigabatrin is first line.** [S1]
- **Prednisolone + vigabatrin stops spasms more often than prednisolone alone, but showed no better development at 18 months.** [S1]

## Steroid safety

- ⚠️ **High-dose prednisolone raises the risk of serious infection and adrenal crisis.** Every child needs a **steroid alert card and a sick day management plan.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Febrile or unwell → manage as high-risk under the sepsis guidelines** (immunocompromised). **Vomiting or significant diarrhoea → give IV/IM hydrocortisone** (adrenal insufficiency risk). [S1]
- **While on prednisolone:** monitor blood pressure, glucose and weight twice weekly; consider a PPI and prophylactic trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Delay vaccines until one month after ceasing prednisolone**; consider nirsevimab per local guidelines given the degree of immunosuppression. [S1]

## Discharge

- **Admission is usually brief, and discharge does not depend on spasms stopping.** Arrange paediatric neurology follow-up and **a follow-up EEG, ideally two weeks after starting treatment.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Give parents the steroid alert card and an individualised sick day plan before discharge.** [S1]
