# Cervical spine assessment in children

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** ED assessment of possible cervical spine injury (CSI) after blunt trauma in children: spinal precautions, clinical clearance, PECARN-based imaging, collars and moments of risk. It does not cover confirmed CSI. No adult c-spine guideline exists in this compendium. ## Key points

- **CSI is uncommon in children. Keep spinal precautions until the cervical spine is cleared.** [S1]
- **Most cervical spines can be cleared clinically.** A senior clinician must help assess children with persistent symptoms or who cannot communicate. [S1]
- **This covers ED assessment after blunt trauma, not management of confirmed CSI.** [S1]
- **Under 8 years, high cord injuries (C1–3) are more common**; over 8, injuries are predominantly lower. [S1]

## History

- **A high-risk mechanism means senior review and ongoing reassessment.** Persistent pain, paraesthesia or weakness means further assessment and considering imaging. [S1]
- **High-risk mechanisms:** axial load (diving, trampoline, fall from height) · forced hyperflexion (e.g. collapsed rugby scrum) · improper restraint in an MVC · MVC passenger over 60 km/hr, head-on, rollover, ejection or a passenger death · pedestrian or cyclist struck by a motor vehicle · fall over 3 metres (or twice the child's height) · kicked by or fall from a horse · substantial head or torso injury · clothesline injury and hanging. [S1]
- **Predisposing conditions:** previous CSI or injury, trisomy 21, osteogenesis imperfecta, achondroplasia, and other rheumatological, congenital, genetic or metabolic conditions. [S1]

## Examine and clear

- **Reliable examination needs a child who is** alert and cooperative, developmentally able to take part, not intoxicated, not significantly sedated, and with enough analgesia for distracting injuries. [S1]
- **Palpate the posterior midline from the nuchal ridge to T1 with minimal neck movement**, then either side of the midline. [S1]
- **No significant pain, tenderness or abnormal neurology → test active movement:** rotate to each side, chin to chest, look up. **Expect more than 45 degrees each side. Stop immediately for pain or paraesthesia.** [S1]
- **Children who can't communicate:** assess with a senior clinician, awake and alert, watching free neck movement for restriction or torticollis. Pain-free normal movement with no other significant injury or neurological concern clears the spine. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Red flags:** traumatic torticollis · **child supporting their head with a hand (may be the only sign of atlanto-occipital dislocation)** · abnormal or asymmetric neurology or severe headache · GCS under 15 · neck pain or tenderness · restricted movement · significant head, chest, abdominal, pelvic or other spinal injury · unexplained refractory hypotension (neurogenic shock). [S1]

## Imaging (PECARN)

- ⚠️ **High risk → CT:** GCS 3–8 or AVPU = U · abnormal airway, breathing or circulation · focal neurological deficit. [S1]
- **Intermediate risk → consider X-ray first:** altered mental status (GCS 9–14 or AVPU V/P) · self-reported neck pain or midline tenderness · substantial head or torso injury. **Low risk (none of these) → may be cleared clinically.** [S1]
- **CT is also considered** when X-ray is abnormal or inconclusive, with persistent objective weakness or sensory loss, or — after senior review — when other body regions are being scanned. [S1]
- **X-ray views: 5 years and under, AP and lateral only; 6 years and over, AP, lateral and odontoid.** Laterals must show occiput to T1 (may need shoulder traction). **Flexion/extension views are not recommended.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Inconclusive imaging, or persistent symptoms or signs whatever the imaging shows → keep precautions and consider MRI or specialist consultation.** [S1]

## Restrict movement

- **A soft collar, labelled "C-spine not clear"**, is often useful. ⚠️ **Do not reposition the neck** of a child who supports their head with their hands, has traumatic torticollis or concerning abnormal neurology — leave them in their position of preference. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Hard collars are not recommended** — they can raise intracranial pressure and cause respiratory disturbance, agitation and pressure injury. [S1]
- **Take every child off the spinal board at transfer from the ambulance trolley.** Use a thoracic elevation device under 8 years (head elevation in teenagers) for a neutral position. **Sandbags or foam blocks: never taped.** [S1]
- **Replace any pre-hospital collar with a new foam collar labelled "Cervical spine not cleared".** Babies, infants and uncooperative children who don't fit a foam collar: minimise movement without one, and make clear to staff and family that the spine is not clear. [S1]
- **Moments of risk:** early pain relief for pain or agitation; early antiemetics (or a gastric tube) for vomiting; in conscious infants, consider removing the collar and letting the child find their own position, e.g. in a parent's arms. [S1]
- **Intubation:** assign an attendant to neck positioning, consider fibreoptic intubation, and avoid unpredictable muscle contractions such as from depolarising neuromuscular blockers. [S1]

## Consult, transfer, discharge

- **Consult local specialist teams when** the spine can't be cleared clinically, the choice of imaging is uncertain, or imaging is normal but symptoms or signs persist. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Children meeting major trauma criteria, including suspected or confirmed CSI, should go to a major trauma service.** [S1]
- **Discharge when** the spine has been cleared, or CSI has been discussed with the appropriate team and a treatment and follow-up plan is in place. [S1]
