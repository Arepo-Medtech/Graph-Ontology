# Contraception in adolescents

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** consent, confidentiality, assessment and method choice for contraception in adolescents. The general counterpart is `contraception` (adolescence to menopause, including emergency contraception); STIs are `sti-adolescents`.

> ✅ **OPEN AND QUOTED.** 22 of 22 claims quoted verbatim from the **RCH Melbourne Clinical Practice Guideline *Contraception*** (Last updated October 2023); **37 fragments re-checked by machine. 8 quoted doses.**

> **Long-acting reversible contraception (implant or IUD) is the contraception of choice for young people** — and **condoms should be used as well**, to reduce STI risk.

---

## Principles

- **LARCs are the contraception of choice for young people.** **Use barrier contraception such as condoms in addition** to reduce STI risk. [S1]
- **Discussing contraception does not increase the likelihood of sexual activity.** Adolescents are the group least likely to use the most reliable methods. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Assess and document whether the adolescent is a mature minor with capacity to consent.** Confidentiality holds unless they are not a mature minor and/or there is significant risk (harm to self or others, physical or sexual abuse). [S1]

## Assess

- **Screen for contraindications:** thromboembolism risk, malabsorption, liver disease, and interacting medicines such as enzyme-inducing anti-epileptics. [S1]
- **Examine blood pressure and BMI.** Baseline investigations are not usually needed; **consider an STI screen if sexually active and not screened in the last 12 months.** [S1]
- **Explain correct use, side effects and time to efficacy, with written information.** **Review at least 3 months after starting** for adherence, side effects and satisfaction. [S1]

## Long-acting reversible contraception

- **LARC is most reliable: fewer than 1 pregnancy per 100 women per year with typical use.** [S1]
- **Etonogestrel implant (Implanon):** inner upper arm, inserted and removed under local anaesthetic, **effective for up to 3 years**. 20% become amenorrhoeic but 20% have frequent or prolonged bleeding, usually settling within 3 months. ⚠️ **Some enzyme-inducing drugs reduce its efficacy.** [S1]
- **IUDs (levonorgestrel or copper):** commonly inserted under general anaesthetic in this age group; no drug interactions. ⚠️ **Insertion risks: 1 in 500 uterine perforation, 1 in 300 infection, small risk of expulsion.** ⚠️ **Contraindicated with PID and some STIs.** [S1]
- **Mirena LNG-IUD: effective for up to 5 years**, periods lighter and shorter, 20% amenorrhoeic at 1 year. **Kyleena: up to 5 years**, 12% amenorrhoeic at 1 year, more irregular (usually light) bleeding. [S1]
- **Copper IUD: effective for 5–10 years depending on type**, no hormonal side effects, but periods may be heavier and more painful. [S1]

## Shorter-acting methods

- **Medium reliability: 3–7 pregnancies per 100 women per year with typical use.** [S1]
- **First-choice pill in Australia:** low-dose, **35 micrograms or less ethinylestradiol with levonorgestrel or norethisterone** — the 'gold standard' safety profile with low VTE risk; most are on the PBS. [S1]
- ⚠️ **COC is not reliable if a dose is missed or taken more than 24 hours late**; vomiting, severe diarrhoea, some medicines and malabsorption reduce efficacy. **Contraindicated in malabsorption and severe liver disease.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Thrombosis risk factors favour another method:** smoking, raised BMI, immobilisation, personal or family history of thromboembolism or thrombogenic mutations, **migraine with aura**, diabetes with vascular complications, uncontrolled hypertension. [S1]
- **Vaginal ring (NuvaRing):** in for 3 weeks every month; may be less effective if removed and not reinserted within 24 hours. [S1]
- **Progestogen-only pill — an option if oestrogen is contraindicated or not tolerated.** ⚠️ **Not reliable if taken 3 or more hours late (levonorgestrel/norethisterone POPs) or 24 or more hours late (drospirenone POP).** [S1]
- **Depot injection every 12 weeks:** 96% effective with typical use; ⚠️ **small decrease in bone density, usually recovering after stopping**; weight gain; periods may take 6–12 months to return. [S1]

## Barrier and other methods

- **Condoms are not reliable contraception but are effective at reducing STI risk** (88% effective with typical use). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Withdrawal and fertility-awareness methods are not recommended in adolescents.** [S1]

## Escalation

- ⚠️ **Consult the paediatric team if a non-consensual relationship or abuse is suspected**; for suspected sexual assault follow local guidelines — **early specialist consultation is vital.** [S1]
- **Consult adolescent gynaecology** when medical factors affect eligibility or adherence, or menstrual symptoms do not improve on first-line contraception. [S1]
