# Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-21 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** children, adolescents and adults — across the lifespan.

> **Australian primary source.** Australian ADHD Professionals Association (AADPA). *Australian Evidence-Based
> Clinical Practice Guideline for Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder* — **Summary of Recommendations**,
> 3 October 2022 [S1].
>
> **NHMRC-approved**, and endorsed by **APS, RACP, RACGP, Speech Pathology Australia, Occupational Therapy
> Australia, ACPA, AAPI, ADHD WA, ADHD Foundation, RANZCP, ADHD Australia and the World Federation of
> ADHD** [S2].

**The first cross-discipline Australian ADHD guideline approved by the NHMRC**, and — at 2022 — the most
recently published source in this compendium. Unlike dementia, osteoarthritis and atrial fibrillation, it
carries **no currency warning**.

## How to read the grades

Three recommendation types [S1]:

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **EBR** | **Evidence-based recommendation** — a structured/systematic evidence review was performed to answer a prioritised question |
| **CCR** | **Clinical consensus recommendation** — evidence was sought but insufficient for an EBR, or the question was lower priority; an expert subgroup prepared a narrative review |
| **CPP** | **Clinical practice point** — expert opinion and clinical experience, on implementation issues such as safety, side effects and risks |

**For CCRs and CPPs, certainty and strength ratings are not applicable** [S1]. Strength for EBRs runs from
**strong recommendation for the option**, through **conditional for**, **conditional for either the option or
the comparator**, to **conditional against the option** [S1]. Certainty runs **HIGH / MODERATE / LOW / VERY
LOW** [S1].

> ⚠️ **Read the certainty column.** Every evidence-based recommendation reproduced below carries **LOW** or
> **VERY LOW** certainty, with **one exception** — intensive parent/family training where oppositional defiant
> disorder or conduct disorder co-occurs, at **MODERATE**.

**The first-line stimulant recommendation rests on LOW certainty.** That is not a criticism of the
recommendation; it is how GRADE works, and it is the same pattern this compendium found in the dementia and
osteoarthritis guidelines. It is stated here because a reader who sees only "first-line" learns something
different from a reader who sees "first-line, LOW certainty".

## ⚠️ Do not screen the population — but look where prevalence is high

> **Universal screening for ADHD should not occur at the population level (e.g., in preschools, primary and
> secondary schools)** *(1.2.1, CCR)* [S1].

Instead, screening is targeted. **Organisations providing services to high-risk groups could consider
systematic screening** *(1.2.2, CPP)*; **clinicians conducting mental health or psychiatric diagnostic
assessments with people from high-risk groups could screen for ADHD** *(1.2.3, CCR)*; and **individuals who
screen positive should undergo further diagnostic assessment** *(1.2.5, CCR)* [S1].

**Screen when the person** *(1.2.4, CPP)* [S1]:

- **does not respond to treatment for the high-risk condition as expected, or is unable to adhere to their
  treatment protocol**
- **often has difficulty attending appointments on time or forgets appointments**, or shows restlessness,
  difficulty maintaining routines, lack of time awareness, poor working memory, disorganisation,
  forgetfulness and distraction **that are not explained by other psychiatric diagnoses** and **have resulted
  in, or are associated with, clinically significant psychological, social and/or educational or occupational
  impairment**.

### Higher-prevalence groups *(1.1.1)* [S1]

**Children:** in out-of-home care; diagnosed with oppositional defiant disorder or conduct disorder.
**Children and adolescents:** anxiety disorders; epilepsy; history of substance abuse.
**Adults:** any mental health disorder — including substance use disorders, borderline personality disorder,
intermittent explosive disorder, internet addiction, psychotic disorders, binge eating disorder, gambling
disorder — **and those who experience suicidal behaviour or ideation**.
**All ages:** neurodevelopmental disorders including autism spectrum disorder, intellectual disability, tic
disorders, language disorders and specific learning disorders; **born preterm**; **low birth weight**; **a
close family member diagnosed with ADHD**; **prenatal exposure to substances including alcohol and other
drugs**; **acquired brain injury**; **people who are imprisoned**; anxiety, depressive or bipolar and related
disorders; **sleep disorders**.

## ⚠️ ADHD is under-recognised in girls and women

> **Clinicians should be aware that ADHD could be under-recognised in girls and women and that they: are
> less likely to be referred for assessment for ADHD; may be more likely to have undiagnosed ADHD; may be
> more likely to receive an incorrect diagnosis of another mental health or neurodevelopmental disorder,
> such as an anxiety or depressive disorder** *(1.1.2, CPP)* [S1].

A named diagnostic inequity with a named consequence — not under-diagnosis alone, but **mis**-diagnosis in a
specific direction.

Two related points [S1]:

- **Many children and adults may not present with the most visible symptoms of hyperactivity/impulsivity.
  Inattentive symptoms may not be identified until secondary school or later, following increased demands for
  organisation and independent study or work. People may have developed compensation strategies that may mask
  symptoms** *(2.1.7, CPP)*.
- **ADHD should be considered as a possible diagnosis in all age groups, including adults over age 65 years**
  *(2.1.6, CCR)*.

## Diagnosis

**Clinicians conducting diagnostic assessments should be** *(2.1.1, CPP)* [S1]: appropriately registered
(such as with AHPRA); **adequately trained in diagnostic assessment using DSM and/or ICD**; experienced with
clinical interviews, administering and interpreting standardised rating scales, and assessment of functional
impairment; and **experienced in ADHD diagnostic assessment, or undergoing ADHD-specific supervision with an
experienced clinician**.

**Assessment should include all of** *(2.1.2, CCR)* [S1]: a full clinical and psychosocial assessment
**including the person's symptoms and strengths** and how these present across settings; a full
developmental, mental health and medical history; observer reports and assessment of symptoms and mental
state; and **a medical assessment to exclude other causes and identify associated disorders** —
**"medical investigations should only be performed if clinically indicated."**

**The diagnostic requirements** *(2.1.3, CCR)* [S1] — symptoms meeting **DSM-5, ICD-10 (hyperkinetic disorder)
or ICD-11** criteria; **clinically significant psychological, social and/or educational or occupational
impairment**; **pervasive, occurring in two or more important settings**; **assessed in the context of the
person's age, developmental level and intellectual ability**; plus assessment of needs, functional
impairments, participation and quality of life; differential and co-occurring conditions; **the person's
strengths and factors they have identified that minimise symptoms**; and, for children and adolescents,
**enquiry about family functioning and parents' or carers' mental health, to enable support for
parents/carers at the time of diagnosis**.

> **A diagnosis of ADHD should not be made solely based on rating scales or observational data. However,
> rating scales... are valuable adjuncts** *(2.1.4, CCR)* [S1].

**Observations from more than one setting and reporter** — a teacher, in the case of children — **should be
used to confirm that symptoms and difficulties occur in more than one setting** *(2.1.5, CCR)* [S1].

**The views of people with ADHD, including children and adolescents, should be considered when determining
the importance of their symptoms and limitations** *(2.1.8, CPP)* [S1].

### ⚠️ The differential runs both ways

> **Clinicians should be aware that some conditions, such as substance use, anxiety and depressive disorders,
> may be a consequence of undiagnosed and/or untreated ADHD** *(2.2.1, CCR)* [S1].

This inverts the usual reading. The comorbidity that looks like the explanation may be the result.

**Look for mimics and exacerbators** *(2.2.2, CCR)* [S1]: **hearing or vision impairment, thyroid disease,
anaemia**; and **medications with psychomotor side effects** — **cognitive dulling (e.g. mood stabilisers)**
and **psychomotor activation (e.g. decongestants, asthma medication, non-prescribed stimulants like
caffeine)**.

**Treatment for any co-occurring conditions should be offered**, following best-practice guidelines for each,
**but with treatment delivery methods adjusted to account for ADHD symptoms** *(2.2.3, CPP)* [S1].

## Treatment — multimodal, with access named as a factor

> **Pharmacological treatment is most effective in reducing core ADHD symptoms**, and **non-pharmacological
> treatments provide additional support to minimise the daily impact of ADHD symptoms and associated
> difficulties** *(3.1.1, CPP)* [S1].

Use both concurrently **unless** *(3.1.2, CPP)* [S1]:

- symptoms are likely to be adequately supported by only one mode;
- **severity necessitates pharmacological treatment first, to reduce symptoms as quickly as possible and
  enable later engagement in non-pharmacological treatment**;
- **one mode is more accessible than the other, based on cost, location, and service availability including
  waiting times.**

That third clause makes **access an explicit clinical variable** rather than an excuse — and cost recurs in
the medication-choice recommendations below.

**Where multiple clinicians and/or educators are involved, a care coordinator should be appointed.** The
person with ADHD or a family member may take the role; otherwise they should be supported to arrange one
*(3.1.3, CPP)* [S1].

**Transition planning:** identify people needing transition **early — at least 12 months before their 18th
birthday** for those moving to adult services — with a **trained transition lead** coordinating *(3.2.1,
3.2.2, CCR)* [S1].

## Non-pharmacological interventions

| Intervention | Recommendation [S1] | Certainty |
|---|---|---|
| **Parent/family training — young children under 5** | **should be offered** *(4.2.1, EBR)* | **LOW to MODERATE** |
| **Parent/family training — children and adolescents 5–17** | **should be offered** *(4.2.2, EBR)* | LOW |
| **More intensive parent/family training — where ODD or conduct disorder co-occurs** | **should be offered** *(4.2.3, EBR)* | **MODERATE** |
| **Cognitive-behavioural interventions — children** | **could be offered** *(4.2.8, EBR)* | LOW |
| **Cognitive-behavioural interventions — adolescents** | **should be offered** *(4.2.9, EBR)* | LOW |
| **Cognitive-behavioural interventions — adults** | **should be offered** *(4.2.11, EBR)* | LOW |
| **ADHD coaching — adolescents 13–17 and adults** | **could be considered** as part of a treatment plan *(4.3.1, 4.3.2, CCR)* | NA |

**The highest certainty in the guideline attaches to intensive parent/family training where ODD or conduct
disorder co-occurs.** Not to a drug.

**Parent/family training should be delivered to all parents/carers involved in the care of the child, where
feasible** *(4.2.4, CCR)*, and **provided with sensitivity and awareness of the stigma and misunderstandings
that parents/carers of children with ADHD may have experienced** *(4.2.5, CPP)* [S1].

**Clinicians should be aware of the capabilities of the parent/carer themselves** — additional needs may
include **grief and adjustment to their child's diagnosis**, adjustment of interpersonal dynamics, managing
multiple family members' needs, **emotion-regulation, resilience and self-care**, the parent's own **ADHD,
mental health conditions, language and learning disorders**, and **skills and confidence for advocating for
their child** *(4.2.7, CPP)* [S1].

**If children and adolescents receive cognitive-behavioural interventions, these should be provided alongside
parent/family training**, with parents involved enough to support implementation *(4.2.12, CCR)* [S1].

**Lifestyle:** ask about **sleep**, and about **diet and physical activity levels**, offering strategies
and/or referral *(4.1.1, CPP)* [S1].

## Medication

### Before starting

**A comprehensive assessment** *(5.1.2, CPP)* [S1]: confirmation that diagnostic criteria are met; **evaluation
of current educational or employment circumstances**; **risk assessment for substance misuse and drug
diversion**; and physical health — medical history considering contraindications, current medication,
**height and weight measured and recorded against the normal range for age and sex**, and **a cardiovascular
assessment including baseline heart rate and blood pressure, measured with an appropriately sized cuff and
compared with centile for age and height**.

> **An electrocardiogram (ECG) is not needed before starting stimulants, atomoxetine or guanfacine**, unless
> the person has any of the features below or a co-occurring condition treated with medications that may pose
> an increased cardiac risk *(5.1.2, CPP)* [S1].

A deliberate **de-escalation** — the guideline removing a routine test, as the candidiasis guideline does for
fluconazole liver monitoring.

**Refer for a cardiology opinion before commencing stimulants if any of** *(5.1.3, CCR)* [S1]: **a history of
congenital heart disease or previous cardiac surgery**; **sudden death in a first-degree relative under 40
suggesting cardiac disease**; **shortness of breath on exertion compared with peers**; **fainting on
exertion**; **palpitations that are rapid, regular, and start and stop suddenly**; **chest pain suggesting
cardiac origin**; **a heart murmur** (not including innocent murmurs in children); **hypertension**.

**Refer to an appropriate physician if blood pressure is consistently above age-based normal values — or for
children and adolescents above the 95th centile for age and height** *(5.1.4, CCR)* [S1].

### Two things to say before the first dose

> **Explain to the person with ADHD or their parents/family/carers that sometimes when a person starts taking
> ADHD medication that reduces symptoms, they become aware of how severe their untreated symptoms were, and
> prepare them for this awareness** *(5.1.1, CPP)* [S1].

> **Explain that medication reduces symptoms but rarely reduces them completely; therefore it is important to
> have realistic expectations and ensure medication is only one part of a person's treatment and support
> plan** *(5.1.1, CPP)* [S1].

The first is unlike anything else in thirty-four guidelines: a warning about an **insight** the treatment
produces, and an instruction to prepare the person for it.

### Choice of agent

| Group | Recommendation [S1] | Certainty |
|---|---|---|
| **Children and adolescents 5–17 — first line** | **Methylphenidate or dexamfetamine or lisdexamfetamine should be offered as the first-line pharmacological treatment, where ADHD symptoms are causing significant impairment** *(5.3.1, EBR)* | **LOW** |
| **Adults 18+ — first line** | **Methylphenidate or dexamfetamine or lisdexamfetamine should be offered as first-line** *(5.4.1, EBR)* | **LOW** |
| **Children and adolescents — second line** | **Atomoxetine or guanfacine or clonidine should be offered** *(5.3.4, EBR)* | **LOW** |
| **Adults — second line** | **Atomoxetine or guanfacine should be offered** *(5.4.4, EBR)* | **VERY LOW** |
| **Adults — further options, in no particular order** | **bupropion, clonidine, modafinil, reboxetine, venlafaxine.** Careful monitoring of adverse side effects is required *(5.5.1, EBR)* | **VERY LOW** |
| **Adults — could also be offered, in no particular order** | **lamotrigine, aripiprazole, agomelatine, armodafinil, desvenlafaxine** *(5.5.2, CPP)* | NA |
| **Under 5 years** | **A specialist with expertise in child development and treatment of ADHD in young children — a paediatrician or child psychiatrist — should assess the child.** **Medication should be used cautiously, and monitored closely, in this age group** *(5.2.1, CPP)* | NA |
| **Over 65 years** | **Apply the same recommendations and principles of prescribing, with careful monitoring of side effects** *(5.4.5, CPP)* | NA |

**Second-line agents are indicated when** *(5.3.4, 5.4.4)* [S1]: stimulants are contraindicated; the person
cannot tolerate them; **symptoms have not responded to separate trials of dexamfetamine or lisdexamfetamine,
and of methylphenidate, at adequate doses**; or the clinician considers the medication may be beneficial **as
an adjunct** to the current regimen. **Due consideration of risks and safety is required, especially if
medications are used in combination.**

**If one medication type or duration of action is not effective or poorly tolerated, another should be
trialled** *(5.3.3, 5.4.3, CPP)* [S1].

### Short-acting or long-acting

The choice is clinical **together with the wishes of the person or their family**, weighing *(5.3.2, 5.4.2,
CPP)* [S1]:

- **a short-acting formulation may be preferred when close monitoring is required**
- **a long-acting formulation may be preferred for convenience, or when there is a medical contraindication**
- **consideration of any potential cost implications**

The guideline names what evidence it assessed **as available in Australia**: **short-acting — immediate-release
methylphenidate or dexamfetamine; long-acting — modified-release methylphenidate or lisdexamfetamine** [S1].
And a practical formulation note: **some short-acting stimulants contain gluten and/or lactose; a preparation
free of these should be used in someone with gluten or lactose intolerance** [S1].

**Modified-release once-daily preparations could be offered for** convenience; **improving adherence**;
**reducing stigma by removing the need to take medication at school or in the workplace**; **reducing problems
of storing and administering controlled drugs at school or work**; **if there is a risk of misuse and
diversion with immediate-release preparations**; or if the pharmacokinetic profile offers an advantage
*(5.6.6, CPP)* [S1].

**Short-acting and long-acting stimulants could be offered together to optimise effect** — e.g. modified-release
methylphenidate in the morning and immediate-release at another time to extend duration *(5.6.7, CCR)* [S1].

### ⚠️ Diversion and misuse

> **Clinicians should not offer immediate-release stimulants or modified-release stimulants that can be easily
> injected or inhaled if there is a risk of stimulant misuse or diversion** *(5.6.5, CPP)* [S1].

> **Clinicians should exercise caution when prescribing stimulants if there is a risk of diversion for
> cognitive enhancement** *(5.6.4, CPP)* [S1].

### Other prescribing cautions

- **If an acute psychotic or manic episode occurs during stimulant treatment** *(5.6.2, CPP)* [S1]: stop
  stimulants and review other ADHD medication; treat the episode; **consider introduction of a mood
  stabiliser**; consider alternative ADHD treatment after resolution; and consider costs and benefits of
  reintroduction — **if reintroduced, take extra precautions such as admitting the person to a hospital or
  clinic for observation.**
- **Consider the impact of appetite suppression where there is a co-occurring eating disorder or other medical
  conditions contributing to weight loss** *(5.6.3, CPP)* [S1].
- **Titrate more slowly and monitor more frequently** where there are other neurodevelopmental disorders
  (autism, tic disorders, intellectual disability), other mental health conditions (anxiety, schizophrenia or
  bipolar disorder, depression, personality disorders, eating disorders, PTSD, substance misuse), or physical
  health disorders (cardiac disease, epilepsy, acquired brain injury) *(5.1.7, CCR)* [S1].
- **Record baseline symptoms and functioning before titration; monitor and record adverse effects at each dose
  change; review progress regularly during titration** *(5.1.5, CPP)* [S1].
- **Titrate against symptoms, level of functioning and adverse effects until the optimal dose is identified** —
  **the dose at which symptoms are reduced and functional outcomes are improved, with minimal adverse
  effects** *(5.1.6, CPP)* [S1].

### Monitoring

**Once titrated and stabilised, arrange individualised monitoring based on a chronic disease management
model** *(5.7.1, CPP)* [S1]. **People receiving treatment should have regular review and follow-up according
to severity, regardless of whether or not they are taking medication** *(5.7.4, CPP)* [S1].

**Monitor** *(5.7.5, CPP)* [S1]: **height and weight; cardiovascular function; tics; sexual function;
seizures; sleep quality; worsening symptoms; worsening of mood; increased anxiety; the risk of stimulant
diversion; other side-effects.**

**Growth monitoring** *(5.7.6, CCR)* [S1]: **measure height every 6 months in children and adolescents**;
**measure weight at 3 and 6 months after starting treatment and 6 months thereafter**, or more often if
concerns arise; **plot height and weight on a growth chart**; **for adults, monitor weight if indicated**.

**People taking ADHD medication should be encouraged to monitor and record their own adverse effects**
*(5.7.2, CPP)*, and **standard symptom and adverse effect rating scales should be used throughout the course
of treatment** *(5.7.3, CPP)* [S1].

## Co-occurring substance use disorder

> **In cases of concurrent ADHD and substance use disorders, clinicians should start treatment aimed at
> abstaining from or reducing/stabilising the use of substances first, since current substance use disorders
> may complicate diagnosis and treatment of ADHD. However, start of pharmacological or non-pharmacological
> treatment of ADHD should not unnecessarily be delayed** *(6.3.5, CCR)* [S1].

**Treatment of substance use disorders in patients with ADHD should follow a multimodal approach comprising
both pharmacological and cognitive behavioural based interventions** *(6.3.6, CCR)* [S1].

Sequencing without a queue — stabilise first, but the second clause exists to stop "first" becoming "never".
