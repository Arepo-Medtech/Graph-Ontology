**This closes the gap named in the heavy menstrual bleeding guideline**, whose first indicator requires testing
for **iron deficiency *and* anaemia** and whose treatment statement names iron deficiency as the harm
accumulating while a woman waits — but which does not address treatment.

## ⚠️ The common half of the problem is the one without anaemia

> **Iron deficiency without anaemia is even more common** than iron deficiency anaemia [S1].

> **Although it is three times as common as iron deficiency anaemia, iron deficiency without anaemia is an
> under-recognised and undertreated condition** [S1].

**Approximately 70% of the iron in adults is found within haemoglobin in red blood cells, so anaemia is the
most readily recognised effect of iron deficiency. However, it is now apparent that iron deficiency in the
absence of anaemia also has unfavourable consequences** [S1].

**The Australian burden** [S1]: **iron deficiency anaemia affects approximately 20% of the world's
population**; WHO estimates **8% of preschool children, 12% of pregnant women and 15% of non-pregnant women of
reproductive age in Australia have anaemia, with iron deficiency being the major cause**. And:

> **Anaemia is highly prevalent in indigenous communities. A study of an Aboriginal community in Western
> Australia identified anaemia among 55% of women and 18% of men** [S1].

## What it looks like without anaemia

**Iron deficiency without anaemia has been associated with** [S1]:

- **weakness, fatigue, reduced exercise performance, difficulty in concentrating, and poor work productivity**
- **neurocognitive dysfunction including irritability**
- **fibromyalgia syndrome**
- **restless legs syndrome**
- **symptom persistence in patients treated for hypothyroidism**
- **poor neurodevelopmental outcomes in infants born to mothers with iron deficiency**

> **A deficiency can therefore result in a wide range of symptoms that are non-specific and may not be
> initially recognised as being due to iron deficiency** [S1].

## ⚠️ The threshold moves with the condition

**Ferritin is the initial test — but it is an acute-phase protein.**

> **A reduced ferritin is the most reliable initial marker to diagnose iron deficiency without anaemia.
> Although WHO defines low serum ferritin as less than 12 microgram/L in adults, a concentration of less than
> 30 microgram/L has a high sensitivity (92%) and specificity (98%) for iron deficiency, correlating with the
> absence of iron stores in the bone marrow** [S1].

> **Ferritin is an acute-phase protein so it rises in inflammatory conditions and in the elderly. This can make
> the diagnosis of iron deficiency challenging** [S1].

### Ferritin and transferrin thresholds [S1, Table 2]

| Patients and conditions | Ferritin | Transferrin saturation |
|---|---|---|
| **General population** | **< 30 microgram/L** | — |
| **Inflammatory states** | **< 100 microgram/L** | **< 20%** |
| **Heart failure** | **< 100 microgram/L** | — |
| | **< 300 microgram/L** | **< 20%** |
| **Kidney disease** | **< 500 microgram/L** | **< 30%** |

**The heart failure thresholds are the European Society of Cardiology's**; **the kidney disease thresholds are
KDIGO's — advising iron supplements if ferritin is below 500 microgram/L and transferrin saturation is less
than 30%** [S1].

**A ferritin of 250 is a normal result in a healthy adult and a treatable deficiency in a patient with heart
failure.** Two guidelines already in this compendium — **heart failure** and **chronic kidney disease** — carry
populations whose iron deficiency is invisible at the general-population threshold.

**From the 2024 MJA update** [S2]: **as an acute-phase reactant, ferritin may be falsely normal or elevated in
iron deficient individuals when there is concurrent inflammation, obesity, steatotic liver disease, malignancy
or other chronic disease** — **thus a serum ferritin level less than 70 μg/L in an individual with a concurrent
inflammatory condition may suggest iron deficiency**.

### Iron studies in the differential [S1, Table 1]

| Test | ID without anaemia | Iron deficiency anaemia | Anaemia of chronic disease | Both |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Haemoglobin** | N | ↓ | ↓ | ↓ |
| **Mean cell volume** | N or ↓ | ↓ | N (or mildly ↓) | ↓ |
| **Serum ferritin** | ↓ | ↓ | N or ↑ | ↓ or N |
| **Total iron-binding capacity** | N or ↑ | ↑ | ↓ or N | N or ↑ |
| **Transferrin saturation** | ↓ or N | ↓ | ↓ or N | ↓ |
| **Soluble transferrin receptor** | N or ↑ | ↑ | N | ↑ |

**An elevated soluble transferrin receptor is another useful marker which can distinguish iron deficiency from
anaemia of chronic disease. However, there is currently no Medicare rebate for this test** [S1]. **The gold
standard is a bone marrow biopsy, but this is not commonly used** [S1].

**Changes in iron status before the development of anaemia may be suggested on a full blood count by falling
values for mean cell haemoglobin and mean corpuscular volume and an increased red cell distribution
width** [S1].

## ⚠️ Always find the cause

> **The underlying cause of iron deficiency should always be sought and managed** [S1].

**Causes** [S1]: **inadequate dietary intake; increased requirements (e.g. pregnancy and breastfeeding);
impaired absorption (e.g. coeliac disease, bariatric surgery); or blood loss (e.g. menstrual, blood donation,
gastrointestinal).**

> **A drug history should be taken particularly regarding anticoagulants, over-the-counter non-steroidal
> anti-inflammatory drugs and antiplatelet drugs** [S1].

**This compendium prescribes all three.** The atrial fibrillation guideline recommends anticoagulation; acute
coronary syndromes audits aspirin or dual antiplatelet therapy at discharge; osteoarthritis and heavy menstrual
bleeding both offer NSAIDs. **The drug history in this guideline is a list of the other guidelines' outputs.**

## ⚠️ Alternate-day dosing beats daily

> **To improve tolerability, increase adherence to therapy and improve absorption, alternate-day dosing
> (60–200 mg, depending on tolerability) is superior to daily dosing** [S1].

**From the 2024 MJA update** [S2]:

> **Elemental oral iron doses of larger than or equal to 60 mg reduce the absorption of subsequent oral
> doses.** **A single 200 mg dose taken on alternate days results in about twice the absorption of a single
> 100 mg daily dose.**

**Twice the absorption from half the dosing frequency** — the most counterintuitive practical fact in this
guideline, and the one most likely to be got wrong.

**Practical points** [S1]:

- **Ferrous salts (fumarate, sulphate, gluconate) are preferred as they are the best absorbed.**
- **Guidelines recommend that patients should be counselled to take their iron supplements one hour before or
  two hours after food. Sometimes it may be possible to compromise on the timing of the dose if this helps the
  patient adhere to therapy.**
- **Gastrointestinal adverse effects such as nausea, epigastric pain and diarrhoea reduce adherence.**
- **Controlled-release preparations and iron polymaltose complex are reported to have a lower incidence of
  gastrointestinal adverse effects, however, the iron polymaltose complex is expensive which limits its use.**

**From S2**: **ferrous iron taken on an empty stomach ensures optimal absorption** [S2].

### The vitamin C habit

> **Vitamin C co-administration has long been recommended to improve oral iron absorption. However, a recent
> study reported no significant between-group difference for the mean change in serum ferritin at eight
> weeks** [S1].

## Diet

**Optimising nutritional iron intake can be achieved by integrating dietary haem iron and free iron. Haem iron
(liver, red meat, seafood, poultry) has a superior gastrointestinal uptake compared to free iron
(plant-based)** [S1].

> **Vegetarians can maintain adequate iron intake if a wide variety of non-haem iron is consumed in foods such
> as wholegrains, legumes, nuts, seeds, dried fruits and green leafy vegetables, but these strategies are
> unlikely to be sufficient to correct iron deficiency** [S1].

**Inhibitors of iron absorption (tea, coffee, cocoa, and red wines) should be avoided.** **This strategy is
appropriate for asymptomatic patients who are not at risk of poor absorption** [S1].

**Diet maintains; it does not correct.** The source is explicit about the distinction.

## ⚠️ Intravenous iron — and no intramuscular

> **If parenteral iron supplementation is required, intravenous iron is indicated. There is no role for
> intramuscular therapy** [S1].

**Preparations available**: **ferric carboxymaltose, ferric derisomaltose, iron polymaltose and iron
sucrose** [S1].

> **The use of intravenous iron should be limited because of its adverse effects, including permanent skin
> staining, hypophosphataemia and rarely anaphylaxis** [S1].

> **Intravenous iron should be avoided if there is active systemic infection to avoid any possibility that iron
> may promote microbial growth and disrupt immune responses** [S1].

**Main indications for intravenous iron** [S1]:

- **unsuccessful oral therapy — failure, poor adherence, intolerance**
- **malabsorption (e.g. coeliac disease, bariatric surgery)**
- **inflammatory bowel disease**
- **chronic kidney disease receiving erythropoiesis-stimulating drugs**
- **rapid increase in iron required (e.g. pre-operatively for urgent surgery or following acute blood loss)**
- **heart failure**

**From S2**: **supplementation with intravenous iron results in a faster haemoglobin concentration increase
compared with oral iron supplementation, although haemoglobin levels at 12 weeks are similar** [S2].

**Faster, not better, at twelve weeks** — which is why the indications are about *inability to use oral iron*
or *urgency*, not superiority.

## Follow-up

> **After oral supplements have been taken for 60–90 days, fasting iron studies are repeated one week after
> stopping therapy to check if the iron deficiency has been corrected** [S1].

> **Serum iron has a diurnal variation and the ideal specimen is a fasting morning sample after oral iron
> supplementation has been withheld for at least 24 hours before testing** [S1].

**Iron studies should be repeated approximately 60–90 days, or as clinically appropriate, after intravenous
iron therapy** [S1].

**From S2**: **oral iron should be continued for at least three months following an effective response;
adherence and impaired iron uptake should be considered with inadequate responses** [S2].

> **If there is no response to oral iron therapy or if iron deficiency recurs, further investigations should be
> considered to exclude blood loss or malabsorption. A fall in haemoglobin concentration may be significant
> even if the patient does not become anaemic. Depending on the clinical findings, referral to a gynaecologist
> or a gastroenterologist may be appropriate** [S1].

**A test taken too soon, or while the patient is still taking iron, is uninterpretable** — hence the one-week
washout and the fasting morning sample.

## Where correcting it helps, and where the evidence is thin

**The evidence regarding the benefit of iron replacement in non-anaemic iron deficiency is based on several
small trials and observational studies, mainly in women of reproductive age and patients with heart
failure** [S1].

| Condition | Evidence [S1, Table 3] |
|---|---|
| **Fatigue and neurocognitive dysfunction** | **Improves fatigue in some studies but impact on neurocognitive dysfunction is uncertain** |
| **Fibromyalgia** | **Improves symptoms**, possibly related to the role of iron as a cofactor in neurotransmitter synthesis |
| **Restless legs** | **Small, randomised trials have shown improved symptoms with iron supplementation if the serum ferritin is ≤ 75 microgram/L** |
| **Thyroid disease** | **Case reports** describe correction improving persistent symptoms in patients treated for hypothyroidism with adequate levothyroxine |
| **Heart failure** | **Several randomised clinical trials in patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction and iron deficiency have reported improvements in symptoms and quality of life after intravenous iron therapy** |
| **Chronic kidney disease (haemodialysis)** | **Intravenous iron in patients with ferritin < 700 microgram/L and transferrin saturation < 40% results in less need for erythropoiesis-stimulating drugs, possible cardiovascular benefits and reduced blood transfusion requirements** |
| **Inflammatory bowel disease** | **May improve quality of life** |
| **Pregnancy** | **Iron deficiency should be corrected before and during pregnancy to prevent impaired neurocognitive function (poor memory and slower neural processing) in the child** |

> **While there is extensive literature surrounding iron deficiency anaemia, there is a paucity of evidence in
> non-anaemic iron deficiency. The current literature is predominantly based on small studies with often
> heterogeneous populations. As a result, there are no firm pathways to guide investigations, treatment and
> monitoring** [S1].

**The source says plainly that no firm pathway exists** — and then gives the best available practice anyway.
That is worth preserving exactly as stated.
