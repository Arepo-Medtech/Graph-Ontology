# Emergency laparotomy

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-22 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults 18 and over, from first presentation or deterioration on the ward through to discharge.

> **Australian primary source.** Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care (ACSQHC).
> ***Emergency Laparotomy Clinical Care Standard*** [S1]. **Nine quality statements. Seventeen indicators.**

**The newest standard in this compendium.** It **was launched on 1 May 2026 at the Royal Australasian
College of Surgeons (RACS) Annual Scientific Congress** [S1], and the page was last updated 30 April 2026.

## ⚠️ This is the standard that measures death

Sixty-seven guidelines into this compendium, every ACSQHC indicator set has measured a **process**: was the
test done, was the drug reviewed, was the discussion documented, did the patient get to theatre in time.
This one measures the outcome.

> **Indicator 10b — Proportion of patients who had an emergency laparotomy who died within 30 days of their
> surgery** [S1]
> **Indicator 10c — Proportion of patients who had an emergency laparotomy who died within 90 days of their
> surgery** [S1]

And it is the only standard here in which **an indicator's denominator is a number the clinician must
compute before the operation**:

| | Threshold | What the standard then requires |
|---|---|---|
| **Statement 6 · Indicators 6a, 6b** | **mortality risk ≥5%** | **a consultant surgeon and a consultant anaesthetist present in theatre** |
| **Statement 7 · Indicator 7a** | **mortality risk ≥10%** | **discussion with a consultant intensivist** about direct post-operative critical care |

The footnote defining that number is the same in both statements: **\*Estimated risk of death within 30 days
of emergency laparotomy surgery** [S1]. So the standard requires a **predicted** 30-day mortality to be
calculated before surgery, uses it to gate staffing and a critical care bed — and then measures the
**observed** 30-day mortality in indicator 10b. Prediction and outcome are both in the instrument.

## Why the Commission wrote it

> **Each year, more than 15,000 Australians undergo an emergency laparotomy (emergency abdominal surgery on
> the gastrointestinal tract) for time-critical conditions such as intra-abdominal infection, bowel
> obstruction, perforation, internal bleeding and ischaemia** [S1].

> ⚠️ **Emergency laparotomy is a high-risk procedure with mortality estimates in Australia ranging from 6.2%
> to more than 20% in older adults and people with comorbidities** [S1].

> **The financial burden associated with the procedure is also significant at an estimated cost to Australian
> hospitals of around $400 million per year** [S1].

> **More than half of patients who have an emergency laparotomy are aged over 65, and many of these older
> patients have a high level of frailty and multiple age-related comorbidities. More than 20% of emergency
> laparotomy patients have sepsis on presentation** [S1].

### The gap is named with numbers

Unusually for a clinical care standard, the Commission publishes the registry data showing where current
care falls short — three figures, each of which a quality statement then addresses:

> **Although 74% of patients undergoing emergency laparotomy were assigned a surgical urgency of 18 hours or
> less, just 59% of these patients arrived in theatre within the appropriate timeframe** [S1] → *statement 5*

> **Only 22% of older patients were assessed post-operatively by a geriatric medicine specialist or team,
> despite evidence that such reviews are associated with lower mortality** [S1] → *statement 8*

> **Around 64% of highest-risk patients were admitted to an intensive care or high dependency unit
> post-operatively, even though direct admission to critical care following surgery is known to support
> earlier recognition and management of post-operative complications** [S1] → *statement 7*

And the benchmark it is reaching for is British:

> **It was modelled on the United Kingdom's National Emergency Laparotomy Audit (NELA) which has been
> credited with substantial improvements in emergency laparotomy outcomes, including a reduction in mortality
> across the UK from 11.8% to 8.1% in its first ten years** [S1].

## Scope — including the patients who never reach theatre

> **The Standard relates to the care that patients aged 18 and over should receive when they may need an
> emergency laparotomy** … **This includes patients for whom surgery is indicated but who do not go on to
> have surgery** [S1].

That inclusion is deliberate and it is what statement 4 is built on: deciding **not** to operate is inside
the standard, not outside it.

**Not covered** [S1]: **trauma laparotomy**; **emergency appendicectomy or cholecystectomy**; **vascular
surgery including abdominal aortic aneurysm repair**; **primary gynaecological or urological emergency
procedures**. The exclusions are justified on risk, not on anatomy — **more common procedures with
significantly lower mortality, such as appendicectomy and cholecystectomy, are excluded** [S1] — and
**emergency laparotomy for bowel complications of earlier surgery in these specialties is included** [S1].

On under-18s: **while patients under 18 years of age are not in scope for this Standard, it is recognised
that patients between 16 and 18 years are able to make decisions and give consent to medical treatment in
certain circumstances** [S1].

## Statement 1 — rapid assessment and escalation

> **A patient with symptoms suggestive of a time-critical intra-abdominal condition – including infection,
> perforation, bleeding, obstruction or ischaemia – is rapidly assessed and escalated in line with local
> protocols.** … **In critically ill patients, investigations include blood lactate measurement** [S1].

> **When sepsis is suspected, care is initiated urgently in accordance with the local sepsis pathway and the
> Sepsis Clinical Care Standard** [S1].

The rationale for the lactate is given plainly: **blood lactate is an independent predictor of mortality for
emergency laparotomy patients and is valuable in decision making about surgery** [S1].

⚠️ **Do not delay surgical referral or review due to pending investigations or while awaiting investigation
results** [S1].

**Maintain a high index of suspicion** [S1] in **patients commonly at risk of delayed diagnosis, including
older patients, patients with a high degree of frailty or significant comorbidities, people with cognitive
impairment, delirium or communication difficulties, and people who are immunocompromised** [S1].

## Statement 2 — diagnostic imaging, and a one-hour clock on the radiologist

> **A patient with symptoms suggestive of a time-critical intra-abdominal condition has a computed tomography
> (CT) scan as soon as possible, with intravenous contrast unless contraindicated. The radiologist verbally
> communicates critical findings to the referring or responsible clinician, within one hour of the scan being
> performed. Acquiring a CT scan should not delay very urgent surgery** [S1].

Two instructions here cut against habit, and both are about not waiting:

⚠️ **Be aware of current guidance for the use of contrast media in renal impairment: do not delay emergency
imaging requiring IV contrast in order to obtain renal function testing results** [S1].

⚠️ **Ensure that accessing CT or awaiting results does not delay very urgent surgery** [S1].

The verbal communication requirement is specified down to who counts as a recipient: **communicate findings
verbally to the referring and/or responsible clinician; this must be a clinician who can take appropriate
clinical action. Document the discussion in the patient's medical imaging report, including who the results
were discussed with** [S1].

On who may report it: **where emergency laparotomy may be indicated, review and reporting by a consultant
radiologist before surgery is preferred. In some services, particularly overnight, this may be provided by a
senior radiology trainee under appropriate supervision** [S1]. And for outsourced reporting, the standard
reaches into the contract — **ensure that service agreements support local policies and procedures** [S1].

## Statement 3 — assessment of risk

> **A patient being considered for an emergency laparotomy has their risk assessed and documented before
> surgery, using a validated, locally endorsed mortality risk prediction tool in addition to clinical
> judgement. In older patients, frailty, cognitive impairment and delirium are identified and documented
> pre-operatively using brief, validated tools** [S1].

**Commonly used tools** [S1] — and note that only one carries an Australian validation claim:

- **National Emergency Laparotomy Audit (NELA) risk calculator – which has been validated for use in
  Australia**
- **American College of Surgeons' National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (ACS NSQIP) Surgical Risk
  Calculator**
- **Surgical Outcome Risk Tool (SORT)**

⚠️ **Frailty overrides the calculator.** **Frailty is independently associated with higher morbidity and
mortality following emergency laparotomy and is not considered in some mortality risk calculators. Patients
with frailty (for example, a Clinical Frailty Scale score of 5 or greater) should be considered high-risk
regardless of their calculated mortality risk** [S1].

**Cognitive impairment and delirium risk (or presence): Use a brief, validated tool such as the 4AT.
Perioperative delirium is associated with increased mortality, readmissions, perioperative complications and
long-term cognitive decline** [S1].

And the standard says what its own numbers are worth:

> ⚠️ **While risk scores can help guide discussions, they should be presented as an estimate and
> communication should be individualised, noting that mortality risk models may over-estimate or
> under-estimate risk for the individual** [S1].

### The 45-year threshold

Assessment is required **in patients aged 65 years and older – and on an individual basis for younger
patients, including Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients aged 45 years and above, and patients
with complex healthcare needs** [S1].

This is the **second** guideline in this compendium to set an Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander age
threshold of 45 — the delirium standard is the other. Here the basis is stated: **Aboriginal and Torres
Strait Islander people may experience chronic health conditions that can impact their recovery earlier in
life. Medical input to their care should also be considered on an individual basis from 45 years** [S1].

## Statement 4 — shared decision making, including the decision not to operate

> **When an emergency laparotomy is being considered, there is shared decision making about the patient's
> treatment plan** … **The patient's goals of care are discussed and documented before surgery, and updated
> throughout the perioperative period** [S1].

> **When surgery may be non-beneficial, senior doctors are involved in discussing the likely outcomes,
> benefits and risks of surgical and non-surgical approaches to support shared decision making** [S1].

Two named frameworks: **the BRAN methodology ('Benefits, Risks, Alternatives, do Nothing')** and the
**Best Case / Worst Case scenarios framework** [S1].

⚠️ **Risk assessment scores can help inform these discussions but must be interpreted with care and cannot be
used in isolation to make treatment decisions** [S1].

When surgery may be non-beneficial, the standard requires **structured shared decision making that considers
whether to proceed with an emergency laparotomy, and explores alternative procedures or medical management
options, which may include palliative and supportive care, ensuring the patient's and family's final
treatment choices are informed by a realistic understanding of likely outcomes** [S1].

**If the patient is proceeding with medical management, likely outcomes are explained, including whether
surgical management will be re-evaluated** [S1] — the non-operative pathway is not a discharge from the
standard.

## Statement 5 — timely access to surgery

> **A patient having an emergency laparotomy commences surgery within the timeframe specified by their
> assigned surgical urgency category** [S1].

⚠️ The clock is **local**. The statement and indicator 5a both measure against **the timeframe specified by
their assigned surgical urgency category**, and the categories come from **the local framework** [S1] — so
the same patient may be compliant in one hospital and late in another.

The one nationally stated timing in this standard is for sepsis:

> ⚠️ **Guidelines recommend that patients with septic shock receive surgical source control as soon as
> possible and within three hours. Patients with sepsis without septic shock should receive surgical source
> control within six hours** [S1].

> **For patients with an intra-abdominal source of sepsis, mortality rises with every hour of delay** [S1].

A documentation requirement makes the measurement possible at all: **document the date and time of the
decision to operate in the patient's healthcare record to enable accurate calculation of timely theatre
access** [S1].

And a prioritisation rule that will be read in operating-theatre scheduling meetings: **systems should support
the prioritisation of critically ill patients for emergency surgery before patients receiving non-critical or
elective surgery** [S1].

## Statement 6 — who is in the room

> **A high-risk emergency laparotomy patient (mortality risk ≥5%) has a consultant surgeon and a consultant
> anaesthetist present in theatre during their surgery** [S1].

⚠️ **This includes when urgent surgery must occur overnight** [S1].

⚠️ **If it is considered appropriate in a particular case for a non-consultant to perform the surgery or
provide anaesthesia under supervision, the supervising consultant should be physically present in theatre and
free of other commitments** [S1].

The justification is stated as fact, not preference: **the absence of consultants from operating theatres in
emergency general surgery is associated with increased patient morbidity and mortality, and increased length
of stay** [S1].

**Box 3 defines the terms** [S1], because **there is some variability across Australia in the use of the terms
'consultant surgeon' and 'consultant anaesthetist'**. Both definitions require **specialist registration by
Ahpra and the Medical Board of Australia** plus appointment **to a consultant position or equivalent senior
position by a healthcare service**; the surgeon's definition adds **is operating within their locally defined
scope of practice**, and the anaesthetist's admits an equivalence — **in some rural settings, this may be a
rural generalist anaesthetist who has been appointed to a consultant, or equivalent senior position, by their
healthcare service**.

## Statement 7 — post-operative critical care

> **A patient's post-operative critical care needs are considered based on mortality risk, frailty,
> comorbidities and clinical judgement. A patient with a mortality risk ≥10% is discussed with a consultant
> intensivist for consideration of direct post-operative admission to critical care** [S1].

**Post-operative admission to critical care should be considered for all patients with a mortality risk ≥10% –
and as needed on a case-by-case basis for patients with a risk between 5 and 10%** [S1].

⚠️ **Once a decision has been made to operate, a patient should not be denied critical care on the basis of
age or frailty alone** [S1].

⚠️ **If the patient has a mortality risk ≥10% and the decision is made not to admit to ICU or HDU
post-operatively, document the reason in their record** [S1]. The standard does not forbid the decision; it
forbids making it silently.

The evidence framing is a contrast between two routes into the same unit: **proactive admission of high-risk
emergency general surgery patients to intensive care is associated with improved outcomes, including reduced
mortality. Unplanned admission of patients to ICU from the ward following emergency gastrointestinal surgery
is associated with very high mortality** [S1]. Indicator 7b measures the second route.

For high-risk patients not admitted to critical care, monitoring is referred to **the National Safety and
Quality Health Service (NSQHS) Standard for Recognising and Responding to Acute Deterioration** [S1].

## Statement 8 — the older patient, within 72 hours

> **An older patient who has an emergency laparotomy is proactively assessed and collaboratively managed by
> an appropriate physician, such as a geriatrician, skilled in the perioperative care of older adults. This
> assessment occurs as early as practicable and no later than 72 hours following presentation to hospital**
> [S1].

Note what the clock runs from: **presentation to hospital**, not the operation. A patient who waits a day for
a decision and a day for theatre has already used two-thirds of it.

**Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) is a multi-dimensional, interdisciplinary process used to assess an
older person's medical, psychosocial and functional capabilities and develop a comprehensive management plan**
… **CGA is associated with improved outcomes for older emergency general surgery patients, including reduced
mortality and length of stay** [S1].

**In most settings, the 'appropriate physician' will be a geriatrician or general physician and will have
expertise in the perioperative care of older adults. In regional, rural and remote settings, it may be a
suitably skilled general practitioner or rural generalist** [S1].

**Timely assessment by an appropriate physician is also required for a patient undergoing emergency laparotomy
following deterioration on the ward, or on step-down from critical care** [S1].

## Statement 9 — transition from hospital care

> **Before a person leaves hospital following an emergency laparotomy, an individualised care plan is
> developed describing their ongoing care needs. The plan addresses medicines, pain management, nutrition,
> wound care, and other services and supports needed to optimise recovery and reduce the risk of
> complications** [S1].

> **The written plan is provided to the patient and their support people before they leave hospital. At the
> time of discharge, the plan is communicated to the patient's general practice, and to clinicians and other
> care providers involved in their ongoing care** [S1].

**Enable uploading of the plan by default to My Health Record, which allows other clinicians to access details
about the patient's care** [S1].

**Box 4** lists what belongs in the plan [S1], including **information about medicines (including new or
changed medicines) and ongoing pain management strategies**; **guidance on managing comorbidities during
recovery, and on managing persistent delirium if present**; **advice on responding to mental health concerns
such as anxiety and loss of confidence**; and **for patients who have had sepsis, information on post-sepsis
syndrome and available supports**.

## Transfer is treated as a hazard

**Box 2** opens by naming transfer as the risk rather than the remedy:

> ⚠️ **For patients with acute abdominal symptoms who may require emergency laparotomy, transfers are a major
> source of risk, often delaying time-critical care and surgery** [S1].

**In some cases, transfer may not be possible or appropriate or may need to be deferred. The urgency of
treatment or source control may necessitate that the emergency laparotomy be performed on-site, with transfer
afterwards for further intervention and critical care** [S1].

The escalation is specified as a peer conversation: **this should be a consultant-to-consultant peer
discussion, and involve multidisciplinary input as needed. If there is no consultant at the originating
service, involve the most senior clinician responsible for the patient** [S1].

And the conversation with the patient is required to be explicit about what transfer can cost:
**discussions should include, as appropriate, the risk of experiencing serious complications, the possibility
of losing independence or dying away from home, and the likelihood of admission to intensive care** [S1].

**Box 1** sets the posture for smaller services: **its intent is not to prevent or discourage the provision of
emergency laparotomy in rural and regional settings where a service is equipped and supported to do so, and
where this is clinically appropriate** [S1].

## Indicators

**No benchmarks are set for these indicators by the Commission** [S1] — the standing ACSQHC position, and the
one that makes a 30-day mortality indicator publishable. **The definitions required to collect and calculate
indicator data are specified online at the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare's Metadata Online
Registry (METEOR)** [S1].

| Indicator | Measures | Statement |
|---|---|---|
| **1a** | blood lactate available at first surgical review | 1 |
| **2a** | CT results verbally communicated by a radiologist **within one hour** | 2 |
| **3a** | risk assessed with a validated tool and documented before surgery | 3 |
| **3b** | frailty assessed, patients **65 or older** | 3 |
| **4a** | goals of care documented on a locally endorsed form, **65 or older** | 4 |
| **5a** | surgery within the timeframe of the assigned urgency category | 5 |
| **6a** | **consultant surgeon** in theatre, **risk ≥5%** | 6 |
| **6b** | **consultant anaesthetist** in theatre, **risk ≥5%** | 6 |
| **7a** | critical care discussed with a consultant intensivist, **risk ≥10%** | 7 |
| **7b** | **unplanned** admission to critical care from the ward | 7 |
| **8a** | evidence of local arrangements for physician involvement | 8 |
| **8b** | assessed by an appropriate physician **within 72 hours** | 8 |
| **9a** | evidence of local arrangements for the discharge care plan | 9 |
| **9b** | discharge summary sent to the nominated provider | 9 |
| ⚠️ **10a** | **unplanned readmission to any hospital within 30 days** | *none* |
| ⚠️ **10b** | **died within 30 days of surgery** | *none* |
| ⚠️ **10c** | **died within 90 days of surgery** | *none* |

⚠️ **The three outcome indicators sit under no quality statement.** There is no statement 10. Readmission and
mortality are measured, but no statement describes the care that would move them — which is consistent, since
they are the aggregate of all nine.

**ANZELA-QI is a clinical quality registry providing feedback to participating hospitals on key
evidence-based indicators of emergency laparotomy care** [S1]. **The Clinical Care Standard and ANZELA-QI
indicators align where possible. Healthcare services that perform emergency laparotomies are encouraged to
participate in the registry** [S1] — encouraged, not required.

## Where this connects in the compendium

| Guideline here | Connection |
|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Sepsis** | statement 1 defers to it by name; **more than 20% of emergency laparotomy patients have sepsis on presentation**; this standard adds the **source control clocks — three hours in septic shock, six hours without** — and Box 4 sends survivors back to the sepsis standard's **post-sepsis syndrome** statement |
| ⚠️ **Delirium** | the **4AT** pre-operatively, **persistent delirium** in the discharge plan, and the **only other guideline here using a 45-year Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander threshold** |
| **Hip fracture care** | the other standard built on **an older patient, an urgent operation and a physician co-managing from admission**; hip fracture names an orthogeriatric model, this one names **72 hours from presentation** |
| **Dementia** | **cognitive impairment** is assessed pre-operatively and shapes the management plan; **the KICA** is named for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients |
| **Antimicrobial stewardship** | the sepsis pathway's **timely administration of appropriate antimicrobials** — the only medicines in this standard, and never named |
| **Opioid analgesic stewardship — acute pain** | the discharge plan must address **ongoing pain management strategies** after a laparotomy; this standard specifies no agent |
| **Venous thromboembolism prevention** | Box 4 requires **strategies to prevent complications such as those related to infection or venous thromboembolism** |
| **Colonoscopy** · **Cataract** · **Peripheral intravenous catheters** | the ACSQHC standards whose indicators reach past process — but only this one reaches **mortality** |
| **Stillbirth** · **Stroke** · **Cataract** | cultural safety with **a stated basis**: **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Australians are twice as likely to be admitted for emergency surgery as non-Indigenous Australians** |
