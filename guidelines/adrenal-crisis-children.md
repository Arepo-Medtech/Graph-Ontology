# Adrenal crisis and acute adrenal insufficiency in children

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** recognition, stress-dose hydrocortisone, fluids, glucose and potassium management of adrenal crisis in children, sick-day prevention without a plan, and peri-operative stress dosing. No adult adrenal-crisis guideline is in this corpus; `sepsis-children-qld` touches hydrocortisone for suspected adrenal insufficiency in shock, and `thyroid-disorders` flags adrenal crisis before thyroid replacement.

> ✅ **OPEN AND QUOTED.** 24 of 24 claims quoted verbatim from the **RCH Melbourne Clinical Practice Guideline *Adrenal crisis and acute adrenal insufficiency*** (Last updated October 2024); **38 fragments re-checked by machine. 14 quoted doses.**

> ⚠️ **Follow the child's own sick day / adrenal crisis plan first.** Without one: stress-dose IV/IM hydrocortisone by age, 0.9% saline boluses for shock, and treat hypoglycaemia.

## Recognise it

- **Most crises occur in children with known adrenal insufficiency who develop an intercurrent illness or injury.** Consider a **new** presentation in any child with unexplained severe dehydration or shock. [S1]
- **Classic biochemical triad (primary): low sodium, high potassium, low glucose.** [S1]
- **At-risk children:** primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison's), prolonged steroid therapy (2–4 weeks) in the past 12 months, or hypopituitarism. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Consider first presentation of congenital adrenal hyperplasia in a collapsed neonate.** [S1]
- **In secondary insufficiency, mineralocorticoid deficiency may be absent** — sodium may be only mildly low and potassium normal. Acidosis on blood gas indicates mineralocorticoid deficiency. [S1]
- **First presentation: take cortisol, ACTH, 17-hydroxyprogesterone, aldosterone and plasma renin activity before steroids if possible**, plus a urinary steroid profile and urinary sodium. [S1]

## Stress-dose hydrocortisone

- ⚠️ **Severely unwell: involve a senior clinician and discuss with endocrinology.** Without a crisis plan, give **IV/IM hydrocortisone sodium succinate** by age; consider repeating the dose if response is poor in the first hour; then give hydrocortisone IV every 6 hours. [S1]
- **Initial IM/IV hydrocortisone, then every 6 hours:** birth–6 weeks 25 mg, then 5–10 mg · 6 weeks–2 years 25 mg, then 10 mg · 3–12 years 50 mg, then 12.5 mg (3–6 years) or 25 mg (7–12 years) · over 12 years 100 mg, then 25 mg. [S1]
- **These equate to about 50–75 mg/m² stat, then 50–75 mg/m²/day in 4 doses**, which also covers mineralocorticoid needs over that period. [S1]
- **Once stable:** reduce the IV dose, or if tolerating oral, switch to **triple-dose oral hydrocortisone (~30–50 mg/m²/day)** and wean under paediatric/endocrine advice. **Primary insufficiency:** start fludrocortisone once taking oral fluids, after endocrinology discussion (usually 50–100 micrograms/day). [S1]

## Fluids, glucose and potassium

- ⚠️ **Shock or moderate–severe dehydration: 0.9% sodium chloride 10 mL/kg IV bolus, repeated until circulation is restored**; then replace deficit plus maintenance evenly over 24 hours with 0.9% sodium chloride and 5% glucose. [S1]
- **Monitoring:** blood gas and glucose hourly for 2 hours, then 2–4-hourly once normoglycaemic and acidosis correcting; UEC 2-hourly initially. ⚠️ **Avoid a sodium rise over 8 mmol/L in 24 hours.** [S1]
- **Mild or no dehydration:** no bolus; 1–1.5 × maintenance as 0.9% sodium chloride and 5% glucose IV evenly over 24 hours. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Hypoglycaemia: 10% glucose 2–5 mL/kg IV**, recheck at 15 and 30 minutes to confirm glucose above 4.0 mmol/L; maintenance may need up to 10% glucose in 0.9% sodium chloride. [S1]
- **Hyperkalaemia usually normalises with fluids and steroids.** Potassium over 5.5 mmol/L: ECG and cardiac monitoring. ⚠️ **Over 7.0 mmol/L with ECG changes: nebulised salbutamol and insulin–glucose infusion** as per the Hyperkalaemia guideline. [S1]

## Preventing a crisis (no sick day plan available)

- **Moderately unwell** (moderate illness/injury and/or fever over 38°C, tolerating oral): **hydrocortisone 30 mg/m²/day** and increase fluids and carbohydrate. **Mildly unwell** (fever under 38°C, looks well, no antibiotics needed): **20 mg/m²/day**. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Ongoing vomiting = imminent adrenal crisis**, even if otherwise well, because oral medicines are not reliably absorbed. One or two vomits: hydrocortisone 30 mg/m²/day. [S1]
- **With an obvious cause (e.g. family gastroenteritis), give a stress dose IV/IM and observe 4–6 hours; admit for parenteral hydrocortisone if there is any doubt.** [S1]

## Surgery

- ⚠️ **Known or suspected adrenal insufficiency needs IV stress-dose hydrocortisone peri-operatively.** Elective: initial dose at induction, then 6-hourly. Emergency: give the initial dose without delay; IM if no IV access. [S1]
- **Short anaesthetic for a non-invasive procedure (e.g. MRI):** stress dose at induction; resume usual therapy if well and eating afterwards. [S1]
- **Oral step-down after surgery, e.g. 30–50 mg/m²/day for 3 days then 20 mg/m²/day for 2 days**, before maintenance. Usual maintenance is ~10–15 mg/m²/day (primary) or ~5–8 mg/m²/day (secondary). [S1]

## Escalation and discharge

- **Consider transfer for** severe electrolyte or glucose abnormalities, no response to stress-dose steroids, or haemodynamic instability. [S1]
- **Discharge when** recovering, tolerating oral intake **and** back on the usual glucocorticoid dose or with a clear weaning plan. [S1]
- **Children on corticosteroids should wear ID reading 'Adrenal insufficiency: In emergency give hydrocortisone at 2 mg/kg IM/IV'** and have an adrenal action plan for sick days. [S1]
