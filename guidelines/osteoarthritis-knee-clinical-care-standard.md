# Osteoarthritis of the knee — Clinical Care Standard

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-22 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** patients aged 45 and over presenting with knee pain and suspected knee osteoarthritis.

> **Australian primary source.** Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care (ACSQHC).
> ***Osteoarthritis of the Knee Clinical Care Standard*** [S1]. **First published 2017, revised 2024.**
> **Eight quality statements. Twelve indicators.**

**Paired with [`osteoarthritis-knee-and-hip.md`](osteoarthritis-knee-and-hip.md)**, which holds the RACGP
guideline. ⚠️ **The pairing is not symmetrical: this Standard covers the knee only.** The hip has a RACGP
guideline in this compendium and no Clinical Care Standard anywhere.

## ⚠️ The Standard exists because of one number about one operation

> **The Osteoarthritis of the Knee Clinical Care Standard was first released in 2017 in response to findings
> from the 2015 Australian Atlas of Healthcare Variation (the Atlas). The Atlas identified considerable
> variation in rates of knee arthroscopy in Australia, despite the limited value of this intervention for
> degenerative disease due to knee osteoarthritis** [S1].

Eight statements later, the instrument closes on the same thing it opened with:

> **Indicator 8a — Number of patients who have undergone arthroscopic procedures to treat uncomplicated knee
> osteoarthritis** [S1]

⚠️ **This is the only indicator in the compendium expressed as a *number* rather than a proportion.** Every
other indicator across all ACSQHC standards here has a denominator. This one has none, because the target is
not a rate — it is zero.

## ⚠️ Four indicators that measure a test *not* being done

> **Indicator 2a — Proportion of patients diagnosed with knee osteoarthritis without imaging**
> **Indicator 2b — ... without magnetic resonance imaging**
> **Indicator 2c — ... without computerised tomography scan**
> **Indicator 2d — ... without ultrasound** [S1]

Colonoscopy and cataract measure whether a procedure was *appropriate*. These four measure whether it was
**avoided**, one modality at a time. A third of this Standard's indicators are restraint measures.

## Statement 1 — the diagnosis is clinical

> **A patient with suspected knee osteoarthritis receives a comprehensive, person-centred assessment which
> includes a detailed history of the presenting symptoms, comorbidities, a physical examination, and a
> psychosocial evaluation of factors affecting quality of life and participation in activities. A diagnosis
> of knee osteoarthritis can be confidently made based on this assessment** [S1].

**If the clinical signs, symptoms, and findings of a comprehensive assessment are typical of knee
osteoarthritis, a diagnosis can be made without imaging or further investigations** [S1].

**The physical examination** covers **the patient's gait, range of motion, joint line tenderness,
malalignment or deformities, bony enlargement, effusion, restricted movement, and crepitus** [S1].

**Atypical features that may indicate alternative or additional diagnoses** [S1]: **a history of past trauma
to the knee**; **malignancy**; **prolonged morning joint-related stiffness**; **rapidly worsening symptoms or
the presence of a hot swollen joint**; and **whether pain may be referred from hip or spine pathology**.

**Identify and address with the patient any misconceptions and unhelpful beliefs about knee osteoarthritis,
its management, trajectory, and treatments** [S1] — belief correction written in as a clinical task.

## Statement 2 — appropriate use of imaging

> **Imaging is not routinely used to diagnose knee osteoarthritis and is not offered to a patient with
> suspected knee osteoarthritis. When clinically warranted, X-ray is the first-line imaging. Magnetic
> resonance imaging (MRI), computerised tomography (CT) and ultrasound are not appropriate investigations to
> diagnose knee osteoarthritis** [S1].

The mechanism of harm is stated precisely, and it is not radiation:

> ⚠️ **Degenerative meniscal tears are common in osteoarthritic knees, whether people have symptoms or not.
> Therefore, detection of meniscal tears does not provide useful additional information and may lead people
> to pursue inappropriate management such as arthroscopy** [S1].

**The scan causes the operation.** That is the causal chain the Atlas found and this Standard interrupts.

> **Patients may experience substantial pain with only minor structural changes to joints identified on
> imaging, while minimal symptoms may accompany more notable (though modest) structural changes** [S1].

**If a patient with suspected knee osteoarthritis requests diagnostic imaging, ask about their concerns and
their expectations of imaging. Reassure them that having X-rays or other diagnostic imaging will not change
initial treatment, which will be guided by their mobility and function** [S1].

### A reporting instruction to radiologists

> ⚠️ **When knee osteoarthritis is observed on MRI, including thinning of the cartilage or degenerative
> meniscal changes including tears, a finding of 'knee osteoarthritis' should be included in the report. This
> is of greater clinical relevance than the presence of a meniscal tear** [S1].

The Standard cannot stop the MRI that was ordered for something else, so it changes what the report says
first. This is the only instance in this compendium of a standard specifying the **ordering of findings
within a report** to change downstream behaviour.

## Statement 3 — education and self-management

> **Information about knee osteoarthritis and treatment options is discussed with the patient. The patient
> participates in developing an individualised self management plan that addresses their physical,
> functional, and psychosocial health needs** [S1].

The plan includes **strategies to support increased physical activity participation such as pacing
activities, management of painful episodes and flares, and pain management techniques**; **strategies for
protecting the knee joints such as the use of walking aids**; and **weight management and nutrition
guidance** [S1].

## Statement 4 — physical activity and exercise

> **A patient with knee osteoarthritis is advised that being active can help manage knee pain and improve
> function** … **The patient is encouraged to set exercise and physical activity goals and is recommended
> services or programs to help them achieve their goals** [S1].

⚠️ **Reassure the patient that exercise will not cause damage and is not a risky activity** [S1]. And to the
patient directly: **feeling some pain or discomfort when exercising is normal and does not mean it is
damaging your knee joint** [S1]; **exercise is safe for your knee, even if you have severe knee
osteoarthritis** [S1].

> **Changes in activity may reduce the need for medicines and avoid surgery, as well as help patients manage
> chronic comorbidities and improve their overall health** [S1].

> ⚠️ **Nine out of 10 people with knee osteoarthritis can manage without needing joint replacement
> surgery** [S1].

⚠️ **Passive manual therapies, such as therapeutic ultrasound and electrotherapy, do not play a significant
role in the treatment of knee osteoarthritis** [S1].

**Review factors such as the physical home environment, level of support, cultural activities, access to safe
spaces to exercise, falls risk, and attitudes towards physical activity** [S1] — access to a safe place to
exercise treated as a clinical variable.

## Statement 5 — weight management and nutrition

> **A patient with knee osteoarthritis is advised of the impact of body weight on symptoms. The patient is
> offered support to manage their weight and optimise nutrition that is tailored to their priorities and
> preferences** [S1].

The one quantified target in the Standard:

> **If the patient is living with excess weight, advise them that a 5–10% or greater weight loss over a
> 20-week period is associated with reduced pain and improved quality of life** [S1].

**Loss of excess weight reduces knee pain and improves function for patients with knee osteoarthritis, and
can improve other comorbidities. These changes may reduce the need for medicines or knee surgery** [S1].

**Acknowledge to the patient the challenges of losing weight and any previous weight loss attempts.
Communicate in a sensitive, empathetic, and non-judgemental way** [S1].

⚠️ **Body mass index (BMI) may not always be an appropriate measure for indicating whether a patient is above
a healthy weight; other measures may be used, including body composition measurements or waist
circumference** [S1].

**If a patient living with excess weight is considering or seeking surgery, explain that weight loss can
improve their eligibility for surgery, reduce risks, and improve outcomes** [S1] — weight as an **eligibility**
criterion, stated plainly.

## Statement 6 — medicines, and a list of what not to offer

> **A patient with knee osteoarthritis is offered medicines to manage their pain and mobility in accordance
> with the current version of the Therapeutic Guidelines or locally endorsed, evidence-based guidelines. A
> patient is not offered opioid analgesics for knee osteoarthritis because the risk of harm outweighs the
> benefits** [S1].

⚠️ **The prohibition is in the quality statement itself**, not in the supporting detail — one of very few
statements in this compendium whose headline text forbids a drug class outright.

**Do not offer opioid analgesics to patients with knee osteoarthritis. Opioids have significant risk of harm
which outweigh potential benefits in pain management for knee osteoarthritis** [S1].

The narrow exception: **opioid analgesics may have a role in very limited circumstances. For example, this
may include short-term use in patients with severe persisting pain not relieved by first-line medicines and
optimal non-surgical interventions, and who are awaiting non-general practitioner specialist review** [S1].

**If a patient is already using an opioid analgesic to treat knee osteoarthritis, discuss changes in therapy
with them and explain the risks and need to start tapering the dose with a view to stopping the
medicine** [S1].

### Six more treatments named and refused

> ⚠️ **Do not offer platelet-rich plasma (PRP), hyaluronan, stem cell treatments, medicinal cannabis,
> gabapentin or pregabalin, as they are not recommended in knee osteoarthritis** [S1].

**Two of those six are PBS-listed medicines, two are injectables widely sold privately, and one is a
regulated access scheme.** The Standard names them in a single sentence and refuses all of them.

**Ensure they understand that medicines should not replace self-management strategies, including physical
activity** [S1].

### A disparity in the other direction

> ⚠️ **Consider the variation in pharmacological pain management for Aboriginal patients, with studies
> showing Aboriginal patients are more than twice as likely to be prescribed opioids in primary care than
> non-Aboriginal patients** [S1].

Most equity statements in this compendium concern under-treatment or under-access. This one names
**over-prescribing of the exact drug class the Standard forbids** — and it is a cultural safety note with a
stated evidentiary basis.

## Statement 7 — patient review

> **A patient with knee osteoarthritis receives planned clinical review at agreed intervals, and management
> is adjusted for any changing needs. A patient who has worsening symptoms and severe functional impairment
> that persists despite optimal non-surgical management is referred for assessment to a non-general
> practitioner (GP) specialist or multidisciplinary service** [S1].

**Dedicate an appointment to each review** [S1] — the review is not an add-on to another consultation.

It includes **monitoring symptoms and response to treatment, using the same tools as used at the initial
assessment (such as PROMs)**; **reviewing all prescription, over-the-counter, and complementary medicines the
patient may be using**; and **evaluating any side effects from treatment** [S1].

At referral: **weight-bearing X-ray imaging of the knee** [S1] — the first point in the pathway where imaging
is affirmatively indicated, and it is **weight-bearing**, which the earlier prohibition never mentions.

**Non-general practitioner specialist assessment, such as a rheumatologist, orthopaedic surgeon, or sports
and exercise physician** [S1], following **the RACGP Guideline for the management of knee and hip
osteoarthritis** — the Standard deferring by name to the guideline held in the paired file.

## Statement 8 — surgery

> **A patient with knee osteoarthritis who has severe functional impairment despite optimal non-surgical
> management is considered for timely joint replacement surgery or joint-conserving surgery. The patient
> receives comprehensive information about the procedure and potential outcomes to inform their decision.
> Arthroscopic procedures are not offered to treat uncomplicated knee osteoarthritis** [S1].

**Optimal non-surgical management is given a duration**: **assess whether the patient has undertaken optimal
non-surgical management, such as 12 weeks of optimal physical activity and exercise** [S1].

⚠️ **Do not offer arthroscopic procedures as treatment for uncomplicated knee osteoarthritis** [S1].

> **Arthroscopic procedures, including debridement and partial meniscectomy, provide little or no clinically
> significant benefit in pain or function and are not indicated as a primary treatment in the management of
> uncomplicated knee osteoarthritis** [S1].

**Uncomplicated** is defined so the exclusion cannot be argued around: **uncomplicated knee osteoarthritis is
not accompanied by true mechanical locking, septic arthritis, or inflammatory arthropathy. Meniscal changes,
such as tears identified by imaging, do not warrant arthroscopy** [S1]. **Arthroscopy may be indicated if the
patient has an alternate diagnosis such as true mechanical locking, septic arthritis, or inflammatory
arthropathy requiring synovectomy** [S1].

**Use PROMs before and after all surgical interventions** [S1].

## Scope

**Patients aged 45 years and over** [S1]. **Not covered**: **management of knee pain in people aged under 45
years**; **management of knee pain due to recent trauma or with symptoms that are not suggestive of knee
osteoarthritis and where another diagnosis is suspected**; and **rehabilitation after knee surgery** [S1].

**Knee osteoarthritis is more common in women than men and, although it may affect people of all ages, the
prevalence increases sharply from the age of 45 years** [S1] — the age cutoff is derived, not arbitrary.

## Indicators

**No benchmarks are set for these indicators by the Commission** [S1].

| Indicator | Measures | Statement |
|---|---|---|
| **1a** | comprehensive person-centred assessment at new diagnosis | 1 |
| ⚠️ **2a–2d** | diagnosed **without** imaging / **without** MRI / **without** CT / **without** ultrasound | 2 |
| **3a** | has an individualised self-management plan | 3 |
| **3b** | plan includes documented **physical activity** advice | 3 |
| **3c** | plan includes documented **weight management** advice, patients who are overweight | 3 |
| **6a** | evidence of local arrangements for guideline-concordant prescribing | 6 |
| **7a** | a **documented timeframe for review** | 7 |
| ⚠️ **8a** | **Number** who underwent arthroscopy for uncomplicated knee osteoarthritis | 8 |

⚠️ **Statements 4 and 5 carry no indicator of their own.** Physical activity and weight management — the two
interventions the Standard says can **reduce the need for medicines and avoid surgery** — are measured only
indirectly, by **3b** and **3c**, which check whether advice was *written into a plan*. What was documented is
audited; what was done is not.

**Indicator 6a is an arrangements indicator**, not a patient-level one: the medicines statement, which
contains the Standard's only outright prohibitions, is measured by whether the service has a policy.
