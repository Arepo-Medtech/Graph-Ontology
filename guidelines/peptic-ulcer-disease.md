## ⚠️ The finding: the PBS supplies seven days, the evidence needs fourteen

> **A Cochrane review found extending the treatment duration to 14 days significantly increased the
> eradication rate compared to a seven-day regimen** [S1].

> ⚠️ **This presents logistical and financial challenges for patients, as the Pharmaceutical Benefits
> Scheme permits a single prescription without repeats. A follow-up review at seven days is recommended to
> consider the need for an additional prescription, providing a total treatment duration of 14 days** [S1].

⚠️ **The 14-day course requires two consultations and two prescriptions. If the second does not happen, the
patient gets seven days.**

**S1's own case is exactly that failure:** a man aged 40 **completed a combined course of esomeprazole,
clarithromycin and amoxicillin for seven days**, represented weeks later, and his repeat urea breath test
was positive — **refractory infection**, then gastroscopy and salvage quadruple therapy.

> ⚠️ **Book the seven-day review when you write the first script, not after it runs out.** S1 makes that
> review do double duty: **assess side effects and monitor adherence at the seven-day review to support
> antibiotic completion and promote antimicrobial stewardship** [S1].

## Who has it

**Australian general population prevalence in adults 24.8%** — and ⚠️ **Aboriginal and Torres Strait
Islander peoples and Asian communities disproportionately represented with prevalence as high as
50–80%** [S1].

**Most patients remain asymptomatic, but nearly all will develop chronic gastritis** [S1].

| Complication | Lifetime risk [S1] |
|---|---|
| **Peptic ulcer disease** | **10%** |
| ⚠️ **Gastric adenocarcinoma** | **1–3%** |
| **MALT lymphoma** | **<0.1%** |

⚠️ **Asymptomatic H. pylori infection does not require treatment other than in populations with high
gastric cancer prevalence or if starting long-term NSAIDs** (including low-dose aspirin and selective
COX-2 inhibitors) [A1].

⚠️ **Read those two together.** A1 carves out **populations with high gastric cancer prevalence**; S1
identifies **which Australian populations carry 50–80% prevalence**. **Neither source states the gastric
cancer incidence in those communities**, so the guideline does not join the two into a screening
recommendation — see *Unresolved*.

**Family members with upper gastrointestinal symptoms or a positive family history of gastric cancer
should be offered screening, treatment and follow-up to reduce the risk of progression** [S1].

## Who to test

**Test for H. pylori infection (usually a urea breath test) in patients with acute, recurrent or chronic
gastric or duodenal ulcer, or with risk factors for GI complications who are starting long-term NSAID
treatment** [A1].

**Testing for ulcer prevention in asymptomatic patients needs to be assessed on a case-by-case basis and is
not routinely recommended. However, testing and eradicating H. pylori is recommended in patients with a
past history of peptic ulcer disease before starting low-dose aspirin or NSAIDs** [S2] — **A1 agrees, and
adds: consider testing all patients** [A1].

### ⚠️ Washout before the breath test — the sources agree

| Medication | Washout [S1] | [A1] |
|---|---|---|
| **Antibiotics and bismuth compounds** | **4 weeks** | **antibacterials 4 weeks** |
| **Cytoprotective agents (sucralfate)** | **2 weeks** | — |
| **Proton pump inhibitors** | **2 weeks** | **at least 2 weeks** |
| **H2 antagonists and antacids** | ⚠️ **fasting period only** | — |

⚠️ **All antibiotics, cytoprotective agents and proton pump inhibitors must be ceased prior to testing as
they can cause false negative results** [S1]. **False negatives may also occur in acute bleeding** [A1].

⚠️ **S1 is the only source that says H2 antagonists and antacids need only the fasting period.** That
matters: a patient on a PPI faces a two-week symptomatic gap before testing, and the alternative acid
suppressant does not carry the same wait.

### Alternatives to the breath test

| | |
|---|---|
| **Monoclonal stool antigen** | **an acceptable alternative in adults and preferred in paediatric patients because of increased sensitivity and specificity (96.6–98.0% and 94.1–100%)** [S1] |
| ⚠️ **Urea breath test in children** | ⚠️ **sensitivity and specificity significantly decrease in paediatric populations** [S1] |
| **Serology** | **might be considered as an alternative in adults unable to cease antibiotics, cytoprotective agents or PPIs (sensitivity 55.6–85%, specificity 79%)** [S1] |

> ⚠️ **Serology is for diagnosis, never for cure.** **Serology is not a valid method for confirming
> eradication as antibody levels can take up to a year to fall** [A1]. **The two uses are easy to conflate
> and the sources address different ones** — S1 permits it to diagnose, A1 forbids it to confirm.

## Eradication

### ⚠️ Both sources give the same regimen

| | **S1 (AJGP 2025)** — quoted, re-checkable | **A1 (AMH 2026)** — paraphrased, queued |
|---|---|---|
| **PPI** | ⚠️ **esomeprazole 20 mg twice daily** | **standard dose twice daily** |
| **Clarithromycin** | **500 mg twice daily** | **500 mg twice daily** |
| **Third drug** | **amoxicillin 1 g twice daily** | **amoxicillin 1 g twice daily** |
| **Duration** | **14 days** | **14 days** |
| **Penicillin allergy / amoxicillin unsuitable** | **metronidazole 400 mg twice daily** | **metronidazole 400 mg twice daily** |

⚠️ **The agreement is stated rather than assumed** — and note that **S1's version is quoted verbatim**, so
this regimen is **machine re-checkable in this repository** even though AMH's is not. **A combination pack
is available via the PBS** [A1].

### ⚠️ Where they part: the 7-day escape clause

| Source | Says |
|---|---|
| ⚠️ **A1 (AMH 2026)** | ⚠️ **a 7-day course may be considered if the local clarithromycin resistance rate is low** |
| ⚠️ **S1 (AJGP 2025)** | **clarithromycin resistance has significantly increased over the last 20 years in Australia**, and **a Cochrane review found 14 days significantly increased the eradication rate compared to a seven-day regimen** |

⚠️ **A1's condition is "if local resistance is low". Neither source held here states what any local
Australian clarithromycin resistance rate actually is**, so ⚠️ **the condition cannot be evaluated from
this guideline.** S1's key point is unconditional: **duration of treatment should be 14 days**.

**Not options:** ⚠️ **dual therapy and other ad hoc regimens are not recommended because of unsatisfactory
eradication rates and the risk of developing drug resistance** [A1].

**Timing:** ⚠️ **in patients with acute severe complications of PUD (eg bleeding, perforation), delay
eradication until the patient is stable and able to take medication orally** [A1].

### Adverse effects to warn about

**The most common side effects of H. pylori therapy are diarrhoea, altered taste and gastrointestinal
upset** [S1]. Specifically: ⚠️ **clarithromycin — QT prolongation**; ⚠️ **metronidazole — alcohol
interactions can cause headache, flushing, nausea and vomiting**; **amoxicillin — maculopapular rash,
anaphylaxis** [S1].

**Inform patients about possible adverse effects, the need for compliance, and the possibility of a cure
without the need for long-term medication** [A1].

### Test of cure

**A repeat UBT is recommended as a test of cure at least four weeks after completion of therapy** [S1].

**Eradication is defined as testing negative 4 weeks or longer after eradication therapy, and at least 2
weeks after stopping PPI treatment to avoid a false-negative result** [A1]. **Confirmation is generally
advisable but not always necessary** [A1] — **use urea breath test, faecal antigen test or endoscopy,
endoscopy particularly for gastric ulcers and complicated duodenal ulcers** [A1].

**Continue maintenance PPI in those with gastric ulcers, large ulcers (>1 cm diameter) or complicated
duodenal ulcers until healing has been confirmed by endoscopy** [A1].

### When it fails

**In Australia treatment failure is primarily caused by poor adherence and increasing clarithromycin
resistance** [S1]; **non-compliance with the treatment regimen or bacterial resistance** [A1].

**Also associated with failure:** ⚠️ **previous use of proton pump inhibitors and macrolides** and
⚠️ **smoking**, which also carries **an increased risk of developing gastric cancer with a concurrent
H. pylori infection** [S1].

> ⚠️ **Patients with refractory H. pylori should avoid repeat eradication with first-line
> clarithromycin-based triple therapy because of low repeat success rates and the potential for further
> resistance** [S1].

**Salvage** — **either restricted or available through special access schemes**: **levofloxacin, colloidal
bismuth subcitrate and tetracycline**, with **rifabutin and moxifloxacin as alternative options** [S1];
**bismuth-containing quadruple therapy or levofloxacin-based triple therapy** [A1]. ⚠️ **Refer to a
specialist, including for gastroscopy** [S1]; **the optimal second-line regimen is unclear** [A1].

⚠️ **No salvage dose is given by any source held — see *Unresolved*.**

**True reinfection is uncommon after successful eradication** [A1]. **Long-term maintenance acid
suppressant treatment may be necessary if eradication fails, is contraindicated, or if peptic ulcer recurs
even in the absence of reinfection** [A1].

## NSAID-induced ulcers

> ⚠️ **Peptic ulcers induced by NSAIDs (including low-dose aspirin and selective COX-2 inhibitors) are
> often silent and present with a complication — bleeding, perforation, obstruction** [A1].

**NSAIDs exert their therapeutic effects by inhibiting COX-2; the gastric and renal toxicities are related
to inhibition of COX-1** [S2].

⚠️ **NSAID treatment in the presence of H. pylori infection significantly increases the risk of PUD and its
complications** [A1] — **the two major risk factors compound** [S2].

### Risk stratification

**Risk factors: increased age (>65 years), past history of peptic ulcer disease, heart disease, and
co-prescription of antiplatelets, corticosteroids and anticoagulants. Higher doses of NSAIDs lead to
increased risk** [S2].

> ⚠️ **In patients who are chronic users of NSAIDs and who have no risk factors, only 0.4% have serious
> adverse events. However, the risk is as high as 9% in patients with multiple risk factors** [S2].

### ⚠️ Which NSAID — a 25-fold spread

**All NSAIDs cause some degree of gastrointestinal toxicity** [S2], but the relative risk of upper GI
complications is not uniform:

| Relative risk [S2] | Drugs |
|---|---|
| **Low (<2)** | **aceclofenac, celecoxib, ibuprofen** |
| **Intermediate (2–4)** | **diclofenac, meloxicam, ketoprofen** |
| **Higher (4–5)** | **naproxen, indomethacin, diflunisal** |
| ⚠️ **7.4** | **piroxicam** |
| ⚠️ **11.5** | **ketorolac** |

⚠️ **COX-2 inhibitors are associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular events**, so **to avoid
possible cardiovascular complications the use of NSAIDs should be at the lowest possible dose and for the
shortest time** [S2].

**Low-dose aspirin:** **a meta-analysis of 57,000 patients revealed a relative risk of 2 for significant
gastrointestinal adverse events such as bleeding.** ⚠️ **In patients with a history of ulcer bleeding
consideration needs to be given to secondary ulcer prevention with PPI therapy. Changing aspirin to other
antiplatelet therapy is not recommended** [S2].

### Treating an established NSAID ulcer

**Stop the NSAID (excluding low-dose aspirin) if possible and substitute paracetamol and non-drug
treatment. Treat with a PPI for at least 8 weeks. Test for H. pylori and treat if present** — possibly
**delaying treatment until the ulcer has healed, eg if it is bleeding** [A1].

⚠️ **If the NSAID cannot be stopped, healing is slower and recurrence more likely** [A1]. **Follow-up
endoscopy to confirm healing is advisable** [A1].

### Prevention

| Strategy | Evidence |
|---|---|
| ⚠️ **COX-2 inhibitor + PPI** | ⚠️ **lowest absolute event probability for ulcer complications** [S2] |
| **COX-2 inhibitor alone** | second [S2] |
| **Non-selective NSAID + PPI** | third — **all three strategies were well tolerated** [S2] |
| **Full-dose PPI** | **eg omeprazole 20 mg once daily**, **continued for the duration of NSAID treatment** [A1]. **PPIs are slightly better at preventing duodenal than gastric ulcers** and have **assumed therapeutic dominance** [S2] |
| ⚠️ **H2 antagonist** | ⚠️ **a single daily dose does NOT protect against NSAID-related gastric ulcers**; **double doses (at least ranitidine 300 mg twice daily) were effective** [S2] |
| **Misoprostol** | **800 micrograms daily**; **prevented gastric ulcers 74% and duodenal 53%** but **limited by cramping, abdominal pain and diarrhoea — a third-line option** [S2]. ⚠️ **400 micrograms daily has fewer adverse effects but is less effective in preventing gastric ulcers** [A1] |

**Use a PPI with an NSAID in those at highest risk — the elderly, history of PUD especially if complicated;
consider use in other patients** [A1]. **Prophylactic PPI needs to be offered to patients with a past
history of peptic ulcer disease and those on dual antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy** [S2].

**Use regular paracetamol to help minimise NSAID use** [A1].
