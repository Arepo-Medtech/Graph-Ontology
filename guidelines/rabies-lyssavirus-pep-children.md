# Rabies and Australian bat lyssavirus post-exposure prophylaxis in children

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** exposure history, wound first aid, rabies immunoglobulin (HRIG) dose and administration, vaccine precautions, PEP started overseas and state arrangements after a bat exposure in Australia or a mammal exposure overseas, in children. ## Key points

- **Post-exposure prophylaxis = thorough washing of wounds with soap plus the appropriate vaccine ± immunoglobulin regimen**, which prevents the virus entering the central nervous system. [S1]
- **In Australia, exposure is from bats**, and in travellers returning from rabies-endemic areas. [S1]
- Australian bat lyssavirus (ABLV) is closely related to rabies and is found in bats, including flying foxes and microbats. The disease caused by rabies and the other lyssaviruses is clinically indistinguishable. [S1]
- **Transmission is by bites, scratches, or direct viral contact with a mucosal surface**; human-to-human transmission is not proven. **Incubation ranges from 5 days to many years.** [S1]
- **Advise families on prevention:** avoid contact with wildlife and vaccinate before travel to rabies-endemic areas. [S1]

## Assessment

- **History:** nature of exposure (bite, scratch, mucous membrane) · site (head and neck, highly innervated areas such as fingers) · animal source · country · first aid given · immunisation history (pre-exposure rabies prophylaxis, tetanus) · any rabies treatment given overseas — date, volume, type, route. [S1]
- **Within Australia, if a bat is the source and it can be done without putting others at risk, keep the bat for ABLV testing.** [S1]
- **No investigations are needed for asymptomatic children.** [S1]

## Wound care

- ⚠️ **First aid: wash the wound with soap or detergent, flush it for 15 minutes, then apply an iodine-containing solution.** Appropriate first aid substantially reduces the infection risk. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Open wound: clean ± debride — do not suture.** [S1]

## Post-exposure prophylaxis

- ⚠️ **Category 2 bat exposures are managed differently from category 2 mammal exposures**, because superficial wounds from bats carry a higher infection risk. [S1]
- **If unsure whether treatment is needed, discuss with the local public health unit or health department, or the infectious diseases team.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Give the first vaccine dose as soon as possible after exposure.** Check allergies: **Rabipur® contains egg protein.** [S1]
- **Give the vaccine away from the HRIG site**, e.g. the contralateral deltoid or anterolateral thigh. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Rabies immunoglobulin (HRIG) 20 IU/kg.** Infiltrate as much of the dose as feasible in and around all wounds; **give the remainder IM in a different limb from the vaccine.** [S1]
- **HRIG infiltration is painful — ensure adequate analgesia ± sedation.** Topical or local anaesthetic infiltration into the wound may dilute HRIG and lessen its effect; the evidence is uncertain. [S1]
- **Immunocompromised children:** consult the Immunisation Handbook and the infectious diseases team for treatment and serological follow-up. [S1]
- **PEP started overseas:** with clear documentation of an appropriate vaccine course, complete the standard regimen; if documentation is unclear, consult the Immunisation Handbook on whether to restart at Day 0. [S1]

## State arrangements, notification and discharge

- **Provision varies by state.** Victoria: give the family a patient-held record and order later doses through their nominated GP. NSW: Public Health Unit on 1300 066 055. QLD: contact the local Public Health Unit (case report form). [S1]
- ⚠️ **Any confirmed case of rabies or ABLV requires urgent notification to the Department of Health.** [S1]
- **Discharge when** a plan is in place to complete the vaccination schedule and review the wounds. [S1]
