# Sodium valproate — benefits, risks, and people of childbearing potential

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-22 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** valproate prescribing in epilepsy and bipolar disorder, with emphasis on reproductive risk.

> **Australian primary source.** McLaughlin D. *Sodium valproate: balancing benefits and risks especially in
> people of childbearing potential.* **Aust Prescr 2026;49(3)**. doi:10.18773/austprescr.2026.023.
> **Published 2 June 2026; finalised 21 April 2026. Peer reviewed.** [S1]

⚠️ **This is a medicine guideline, not the epilepsy guideline.** It covers one drug across two conditions.
**Seizure classification, first-line selection beyond valproate, status epilepticus management and
drug-resistant epilepsy remain absent from this compendium** — see *Unresolved*.

## The four-line summary, as the source states it

> - **Sodium valproate (valproate) is an approved treatment for epilepsy and bipolar disorder in Australia.**
> - **It is associated with a range of adverse effects that require monitoring and may limit its use.**
> - **Due to its teratogenicity, valproate should not be prescribed to women of childbearing potential.
>   Lamotrigine or levetiracetam are preferred alternatives in epilepsy. If no alternative is suitable and
>   valproate is prescribed, contraception should be co-prescribed.**
> - **Concerns have been raised about the potential risk of neurodevelopmental disorders in children born to
>   men taking valproate; although these findings have not been confirmed in robust studies, male patients
>   should be informed of the potential risk.** [S1]

## ⚠️ The dose threshold, from an Australian registry

> **Maternal valproate therapy is associated with an increased risk of major congenital malformations. This
> risk is dose dependent, with a reported incidence of **3.3% at valproate daily doses below 1100 mg,
> increasing to 30.2% at daily doses of 1100 mg or more**** [S1].

**Nearly a tenfold difference across a single dose threshold.** The source of that number is the **Australian
register of anti-epileptic drugs in pregnancy** — an Australian registry producing the figure that governs
this drug's use internationally.

> **To date, no dose of valproate has been established as completely free of risk** [S1].

**Malformations associated with valproate** [S1]: **neural tube defects such as spina bifida and anencephaly,
craniofacial, cardiac and limb defects, and hypospadias.**

**Neurodevelopmental effects, as odds ratios** [S1]:

| Outcome | OR |
|---|---|
| **Intellectual disability** | **2.4 to 4.5** |
| **Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder** | **1.4 to 1.8** |
| **Autism spectrum disorder** | **1.7 to 4.4** |

## ⚠️ Folic acid does not fix it

> **Folic acid has not been shown to reduce the risk of major congenital malformations associated with
> valproate; however, if used, a dose of at least 0.4 mg daily prior to conception and during pregnancy is
> recommended** [S1].

**This corrects a widely held belief.** Folate protects against neural tube defects in general; **it has not
been shown to protect against valproate's**. The recommendation to take it anyway stands — but not as a reason
to consider valproate acceptable in a planned pregnancy.

## What to prescribe instead, and the PBS position

> **For women of childbearing potential who require treatment for epilepsy, levetiracetam or lamotrigine are
> preferred. Both are listed on the Australian Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme for the initial treatment of
> epilepsy for women of childbearing potential, are safe during pregnancy, have no known long-term safety
> issues and are well tolerated** [S1].

**The PBS listing is written to match the clinical position** — another instance of the access gradient this
compendium keeps meeting, but here running *with* the guidance rather than against it.

If valproate is nonetheless prescribed to a woman of childbearing potential, the TGA and overseas regulators
recommend against it **unless other drugs are ineffective or not tolerated**, and then [S1]:

- **it should be prescribed by a specialist experienced in managing epilepsy or bipolar disorder**
- **and co-prescribed with effective contraception**

⚠️ **The contraceptive interacts with the drug it is protecting against:**

> **Estrogen-containing contraceptives may increase the clearance of valproate and reduce plasma valproate
> concentrations; prescribers should monitor clinical response when an estrogen-containing contraceptive is
> started. A non–estrogen-containing contraceptive such as an intrauterine device or a progesterone-only pill
> is an option, depending on patient preference** [S1].

If a woman taking valproate for epilepsy becomes pregnant: **changing to levetiracetam or lamotrigine may be
considered. If there is no effective alternative, the dose of valproate should be kept to the lowest dose that
prevents seizures** [S1].

## ⚠️ In bipolar disorder, the safer drug has a dangerous window

> For women who require treatment for bipolar disorder and are planning pregnancy, **the use of valproate
> should be avoided, if possible. Lithium is the most effective option, but it too can cause congenital
> malformations. Lamotrigine is less effective but is safe in pregnancy and **requires slow titration over
> around 6 weeks to reach an effective dose. During this period, the patient is at risk of harm**, and advice
> should be sought from the treating psychiatrist** [S1].

**A named six-week window of exposure created by switching to the safer drug.** Few guidelines state the
hazard of their own recommended alternative this plainly.

And the source refuses a blanket rule:

> **In some circumstances, such as a pregnant individual with severe or unstable epilepsy or bipolar disorder,
> the benefits of continuing valproate treatment may outweigh the risks of harm to the fetus; for example,
> stopping valproate may lead to a relapse of bipolar disorder, which can increase risk of maternal self-harm
> or suicidality** [S1].

Assessment should be **contextualised to the patient, considering the severity of their condition, the risk
and impact of adverse effects on the mother and fetus, gestational timing and patient preferences** [S1].

## ⚠️ The evidence says no; the label says be careful anyway

A **retrospective cohort study published in 2023** reported **an increased risk of neurodevelopmental
disorders in the offspring of men who took valproate in the 3 months before conception**, compared with men
taking lamotrigine or levetiracetam [S1].

> **However, these findings were regarded as unsubstantiated by the TGA and the Epilepsy Society of Australia
> because of methodological issues, and subsequent studies were not able to reproduce these findings. The
> European Medicines Agency is undertaking a signal procedure to analyse the conflicting results of these
> studies** [S1].

> **Despite study limitations, as a precaution, sponsors of sodium valproate products have added new warnings
> to their product information and consumer medicine information documents** [S1].

**Two authorities reached opposite operational conclusions from the same evidence.** The regulator called the
finding unsubstantiated **and** the product information now carries the warning. The **TGA issued a 2025
safety update** advising prescribers to [S1]:

- **inform male patients of the potential risks**
- **and the need for effective contraception (for both partners) during valproate treatment and for 3 months
  after stopping it**
- advise male patients to **consult their doctor about alternative treatments if they plan to father a child,
  before stopping contraception, or if pregnancy occurs**
- advise them **not to donate sperm during valproate treatment and for 3 months after stopping treatment**

**This is the precautionary principle made visible.** In this compendium it is the mirror image of two earlier
findings: **tecovirimat for mpox**, where two RCTs showed no benefit and national stockpiles were retained;
and **partner treatment in bacterial vaginosis**, where a single trial created a recommendation. Here the
studies are negative or unreproduced and **the warning was added anyway** — and the article, in the same
breath, tells clinicians to pass the uncertainty on to the patient.

## Where valproate is *not* recommended

> Valproate **has also been used off label to manage behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia.
> However, studies have shown that these symptoms are not improved by valproate and its use is associated with
> increased serious adverse effects; therefore, it is not recommended for this indication** [S1].

**Not merely ineffective — harmful.** This lands directly on this compendium's **dementia** guideline and its
**psychotropic medicines for people with cognitive disability** guideline, both of which address prescribing
for behaviours in the absence of benefit.

Other off-label uses — **neuropathic pain, status epilepticus and status migrainosus** — have **efficacy that
has not been established** [S1]. For **migraine prevention**, off-label use has **efficacy comparable with
topiramate or candesartan** [S1].

**Where valproate is first line**: **genetic generalised epilepsies, which include absence, myoclonic and
tonic-clonic seizures** [S1]. In **bipolar disorder** it is **recommended as adjunct therapy to an
antipsychotic for acute mania and is an option for maintenance therapy if lithium cannot be used** [S1].

## Adverse effects and monitoring

| Effect [S1] | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Common (≥1%)** | **increased appetite (which can lead to weight gain), tremor, drowsiness and hair loss** — may require dose reduction or withdrawal to resolve |
| **Paraesthesia** | **usually mild and transient** |
| **Polycystic ovary syndrome** | higher rate than with other antiepileptic drugs — **almost a twofold increased risk**; magnitude varies between studies because of differing PCOS definitions. **If suspected, consider an alternative antiepileptic drug and refer to an endocrinologist or gynaecologist** |
| **Thrombocytopenia** | **mild, usually asymptomatic — 12 to 18% of patients**; generally resolves on dose reduction |
| **Leucopenia** | **0.4 to 5%**; **pancytopenia is rare** |
| **Hepatotoxicity** | **rare (1 in 20,000) but can be fatal**; **95% of cases in the first 6 months**. **Avoid in hepatic impairment** |
| **Raised liver enzymes** | **asymptomatic elevation of ALP, AST and ALT in approximately 10%**. **Consider reducing the dose and repeat liver function tests after 1 month** |
| **Bone** | **therapy of more than 10 years is associated with decreased bone mineral density and increased risk of osteoporosis**; vitamin D status may be assessed depending on individual risk factors |
| **Overdose** | dose related — **drowsiness, tremor, confusion and ataxia** |

⚠️ **A useful negative**: > **Unlike some other antiepileptic drugs (e.g. carbamazepine, phenytoin),
valproate does not cause elevations of gamma-glutamyl transferase** [S1] — so a raised GGT in a patient on
valproate points elsewhere.

**Stevens-Johnson syndrome**: rare but potentially fatal, **typically presents as a rash that spreads quickly
over a few days, often with fever and sore throat**; **more likely in the first 8 weeks of antiepileptic
therapy**; **patients should be advised to stop taking valproate if a rash appears and to inform the
prescriber**. Its occurrence with valproate **may have been confounded by concomitant administration of
lamotrigine** [S1].

**Hepatotoxicity risk factors**, transcribed as the source gives them: **age 3 years and below, congenital
metabolic disorders (carnitine deficiency, urea cycle disorders, *POLG* mutations), degenerative disorders,
organic brain disease, brain damage or mental retardation, and concurrent use of multiple antiepileptic
drugs** [S1]. *(The last term is the source's, carried from older product information; it is transcribed here
unchanged rather than silently modernised — see Unresolved.)*

### Monitoring — deliberately minimal

- **Clinical monitoring should address tolerability, alertness, weight gain and tremor** [S1].
- > **There is not universal agreement on routine laboratory monitoring to reduce risk of valproate-associated
  > hepatotoxicity.** Baseline testing (e.g. full blood count, biochemical profile) has been suggested.
  > **Further blood tests are undertaken only if significant clinical symptoms arise** — **anorexia, vomiting,
  > malaise, loss of seizure control, oedema, jaundice**. Patients or families **should report these symptoms
  > promptly to a healthcare professional and valproate should be stopped** [S1].
- > **Therapeutic drug monitoring has a limited role, as there is no direct correlation between valproate
  > plasma concentration and clinical efficacy** [S1].

**Two tests that feel prudent are both discouraged**: routine bloods and valproate levels. The safety net is
**symptom reporting by the patient or family**, which places the monitoring burden on education rather than
pathology.
