# Atrial fibrillation

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-21 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults, primary care.

> **Australian primary source.** Brieger D, Amerena J, Attia J, et al. *National Heart Foundation of
> Australia and the Cardiac Society of Australia and New Zealand: Australian clinical guidelines for the
> diagnosis and management of atrial fibrillation 2018.* **Med J Aust 2018; 209(8)** — executive summary
> [S1]. Full guidelines: *Heart Lung Circ* 2018; 27(10):1209–1266, doi:10.1016/j.hlc.2018.06.1043 [S2].

> ⚠️ **Currency.** This is the **first** Australian guideline on atrial fibrillation [S1] and remains the one
> the Heart Foundation publishes for professionals. It is dated **2018**. International guidance has been
> revised since. **Confirm currency before clinical use** — recorded under Unresolved.

## ⚠️ Australia dropped the sex category from the stroke score

> **The CHA₂DS₂-VA score — the sexless CHA₂DS₂-VASc score — is recommended for predicting stroke risk in
> AF.** *GRADE: Strong; Evidence: Moderate* [S1].

> **To avoid the cumbersome practice of selecting different CHA₂DS₂-VASc thresholds for males and females
> when recommending anticoagulation, these guidelines recommend a sexless CHA₂DS₂-VASc score, abbreviated as
> CHA₂DS₂-VA score** [S1].

This is a deliberate national divergence from the international scores, and it changes the arithmetic at the
bedside: **one threshold, applied identically to men and women.** A clinician carrying the VASc habit will
score women one point higher than this guideline intends.

| Letter | Points | Definition [S1] |
|---|---|---|
| **C** | 1 | **Congestive heart failure** — recent signs, symptoms or admission for decompensated heart failure; **includes both HFrEF and HFpEF**, or moderately to severely reduced systolic LV function, **whether or not there is a history of heart failure** |
| **H** | 1 | **History of hypertension, whether or not blood pressure is currently elevated** |
| **A₂** | **2** | **Age ≥ 75 years** |
| **D** | 1 | **Diabetes** |
| **S₂** | **2** | **History of prior stroke, TIA or systemic thromboembolism** |
| **V** | 1 | **Vascular disease** — prior MI, peripheral arterial disease, complex aortic atheroma or plaque on imaging (if performed) |
| **A** | 1 | **Age 65–74 years** |

**Stroke risk factors change over time with ageing and new comorbidities, so annual review of low-risk
patients is recommended** [S1].

## Anticoagulation

| CHA₂DS₂-VA | Recommendation [S1] | GRADE |
|---|---|---|
| **≥ 2** | **Oral anticoagulation is recommended**, unless contraindicated | **Strong; Evidence High** |
| **1** | **Oral anticoagulation should be considered** | **Strong; Evidence Moderate** |
| **0** | **Oral anticoagulation is not recommended** | **Weak; Evidence Moderate** |

> **When oral anticoagulation is initiated in a patient with non-valvular AF, a non-vitamin K OAC (NOAC;
> apixaban, dabigatran or rivaroxaban) is recommended in preference to warfarin.** *GRADE: Strong; Evidence:
> Moderate* [S1].

> **Antiplatelet therapy is not recommended for stroke prevention in non-valvular AF patients, regardless of
> stroke risk.** *GRADE: Strong; Evidence: Moderate* [S1].

Aspirin is not a weaker alternative here — it is **not an option**. *"The evidence for stroke prevention with
aspirin is weak, and aspirin may have bleeding rates similar to OACs"* [S1].

**Non-valvular AF means AF in the absence of moderate to severe mitral stenosis or a mechanical heart
valve** [S1].

**Atrial flutter: the same anticoagulation recommendations apply** — slightly lower stroke risk, but the risk
exists and many of these patients have episodes of AF [S1]. **Asymptomatic patients found on opportunistic
screening have a comparable stroke risk to symptomatic patients** [S1].

## ⚠️ Do not use a bleeding score to withhold anticoagulation

> **Patients at high risk of stroke are also at high risk of major bleeding. The net clinical benefit almost
> always favours stroke prevention over major bleeding, so bleeding risk scores should not be used to avoid
> anticoagulation in patients with AF. Treating reversible bleeding risk should be prioritised** [S1].

The bleeding table is therefore a **list of things to fix**, not a reason to withhold [S1]:

| Modifiable | Comment |
|---|---|
| **Hypertension (SBP > 160 mmHg)** | Blood pressure control reduces the potential risk of bleeding |
| **Labile INR (TTR < 60%)** | **Consider changing to a NOAC** |
| **Concomitant antiplatelets and NSAIDs** | Minimise duration of double or triple therapy |
| **Excess alcohol (> 8 drinks/week)** | |
| **Potentially modifiable** | **Correct where possible** |
| Anaemia; impaired renal function; impaired liver function | Monitor renal function especially when it may be affected |
| **Frailty and falls** | **Walking aids, footwear, aged care home review** |

Among the **non-modifiable** factors, two are explicitly arguments *for* treating: **"Advanced age — stroke
risk outweighs bleeding risk"** and **"Previous stroke — risk of recurrent stroke outweighs risk of
bleeding"** [S1]. **Cirrhotic liver disease is a contraindication to NOACs** (patients excluded from trials);
**in dialysis-dependent kidney disease the role of anticoagulation is controversial and only warfarin is
indicated** [S1].

### Two Australia-specific safety points

> **ICH risk is high in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients receiving anticoagulants. Pay careful
> attention to blood pressure control in these populations** [S1].

> **INR monitoring may be difficult in remote Australian communities, and therefore NOACs have the capacity
> to greatly improve anticoagulant therapy in patients with non-valvular AF** [S1].

Neither point exists in an international guideline. Together they are the clearest instance in this
compendium of a national guideline reasoning about **its own country's geography**.

**Point-of-care INR measurement is recommended in the primary care management of patients receiving
warfarin.** *GRADE: Strong; Evidence: Moderate* [S1] — most useful for stable patients, and in acute
situations where a timely result is needed [S1].

## Screening

> **Opportunistic point-of-care screening in the clinic or community should be conducted in people aged 65
> years or more.** *GRADE: Strong; Evidence: Moderate* [S1].

> **Devices that provide a medical quality electrocardiogram trace are preferred to pulse-taking or
> pulse-based devices for screening, because an electrocardiogram is required to confirm the
> diagnosis** [S1].

**Pacemakers and defibrillators should be interrogated regularly for atrial high rate episodes (AHREs), and
AF should be confirmed by atrial electrocardiogram.** *GRADE: Strong; Evidence: Moderate* [S1]. **An AHRE
indicates high risk of subsequent clinical AF**; assess stroke risk factors and maintain surveillance [S1].
For device-detected AF with **CHA₂DS₂-VA ≥ 2**, follow up closely and **consider an OAC when an episode lasts
more than 24 hours** [S1].

**A transthoracic echocardiogram should be performed in all patients with newly diagnosed AF.** *GRADE:
Strong; Evidence: Low* [S1] — it identifies valvular disease and quantifies LV function and atrial size.
**Transoesophageal echo is considered primarily where cardioversion is indicated and intracardiac thrombus
may affect timing** [S1].

## Risk factors are treatment, not background

> **Intercurrent risk factors and comorbidities — including hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, valvular
> heart disease and alcohol excess — should be identified, and their management considered an important
> component of treatment in patients with AF.** *GRADE: Strong; Evidence: Low* [S1].

**Newer risk factors** named: **obesity, sleep apnoea, physical inactivity and prehypertension** [S1].
**Physician-led intervention of weight and risk factor management in overweight and obese patients has been
shown to lead to a marked reduction in AF burden, and to improvement in quality of life in patients with
paroxysmal AF** [S1].

## Rate or rhythm — decide, document, communicate, review

> **A rhythm control or a rate control strategy should be selected, documented and communicated for all AF
> patients, and this strategy should be reviewed regularly.** *GRADE: Strong; Evidence: Low* [S1].

**Favouring rhythm control:** younger, more physically active, highly symptomatic patients; paroxysmal or
early persistent AF; LV dysfunction; no severe left atrial enlargement; and those in whom adequate
ventricular rate control is difficult to achieve [S1]. **Rate control may be preferred** in patients with
minimal symptoms, or where maintaining sinus rhythm is likely to be or is futile [S1].

### Rate control

**Acute:** **β-adrenoceptor antagonists or non-dihydropyridine calcium channel antagonists** in
haemodynamically stable patients, **with caution if given intravenously.** *GRADE: Strong; Evidence:
Low* [S1].

- **Oral administration is sufficient in many situations**; more rapid onset with careful IV aliquots of
  **metoprolol or esmolol** [S1].
- **Intravenous verapamil must be used with extreme caution because of its strong negative inotropic
  effect** [S1].
- **Digoxin** may be added, but has **delayed onset and a weak rate-control effect, particularly as
  monotherapy** [S1].
- **In marginal haemodynamic reserve, established heart failure or significant structural heart disease,
  amiodarone may be the most effective rate control option** [S1].

**Long term:** **β-blockers or non-dihydropyridine calcium channel antagonists are first line.** *GRADE:
Strong; Evidence: Moderate* [S1].

- **Digoxin** second line or in combination; **monitor serum concentration, target < 1.2 ng/mL** [S1].
- **Verapamil and diltiazem should not be used in the presence of left ventricular systolic
  dysfunction** [S1].
- **Amiodarone is a last line option, given its toxicity profile** [S1].
- **Membrane-active rhythm control agents (e.g. flecainide, sotalol) should not be continued** in patients
  started on or transitioned to long-term rate control [S1].

### Rhythm control

**Acute:**

- **Electrical cardioversion should be performed urgently in haemodynamically unstable patients.** *GRADE:
  Strong; Evidence: Low* [S1].
- **Electrical cardioversion can be considered** — first line, or when pharmacological rhythm control fails
  — in stable patients, **after consideration of thromboembolic risk.** *GRADE: Strong; Evidence: Low* [S1].
- **Flecainide can be considered for rapid conversion**, IV or oral, **in patients without LV systolic
  dysfunction, moderate LV hypertrophy or coronary artery disease**, after consideration of thromboembolic
  risk. *GRADE: Strong; Evidence: Moderate* [S1].

> **Flecainide results in earlier and more effective conversion to sinus rhythm when compared with
> amiodarone** [S1].

Three practice points that change what happens in the room [S1]:

- **There is a high spontaneous reversion rate for new-onset AF within 48 hours**, so **watch-and-wait with
  rate control may be reasonable in a mildly symptomatic patient.**
- **Give an AV nodal blocking medication before flecainide, to avoid 1:1 conduction of atrial flutter.**
- **If AF duration is more than 48 hours or unknown, acute rhythm control should generally not be attempted
  unless left atrial thrombus is excluded by transoesophageal echocardiography.**

**Long term:**

- **Flecainide can be considered for maintenance of sinus rhythm** in patients **without LV systolic
  dysfunction, moderate LV hypertrophy or coronary artery disease.** *GRADE: Strong; Evidence: High* [S1].
  **Use with an AV nodal blocking agent** [S1].
- **Amiodarone** — second line, **or first line in patients with LV systolic dysfunction, moderate LV
  hypertrophy or coronary artery disease.** *GRADE: Strong; Evidence: High* [S1]. It has **superior efficacy**
  but **long-term toxicities, so should not be a first line choice** [S1].
- **Sotalol has modest efficacy, and torsades de pointes occurs in about 2% of patients, necessitating close
  monitoring of the QT interval for all patients** [S1].
- **β-blockers are generally regarded as less effective than antiarrhythmic drugs in maintaining sinus
  rhythm** [S1].

### Ablation

- **Catheter ablation should be considered for symptomatic paroxysmal or persistent AF refractory or
  intolerant to at least one class I or III antiarrhythmic medication.** *GRADE: Strong; Evidence: High* [S1].
- **Catheter ablation can be considered for symptomatic paroxysmal or persistent AF in selected patients
  with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction.** *GRADE: Strong; Evidence: Moderate* [S1].

**For the consent conversation** [S1]: **20–30% of ablation patients will require a second procedure within
the first 12 months.** **Major complication rates from experienced Australian institutions have been about
1%.** And critically:

> **In patients at increased risk of stroke, anticoagulation should be continued indefinitely, even
> following a successful procedure** [S1].

**Surgical ablation concomitant with cardiac surgery may be considered** for symptomatic paroxysmal,
persistent or long-standing persistent AF. *GRADE: Strong; Evidence: Moderate* [S1].

## Anticoagulant plus antiplatelet

> **Careful assessment of the bleeding and ischaemic risks should be undertaken for patients with AF who
> have a long term requirement for anticoagulation and require dual antiplatelet therapy after acute
> coronary syndrome or stent implantation (or both).** *GRADE: Strong; Evidence: Low* [S1].

- **Duration of triple therapy (aspirin, P2Y12 inhibitor and OAC) should be as short as possible** to
  minimise bleeding, while covering the initial period of high thrombotic risk [S1].
- **Concomitant proton pump inhibitors likely reduce the risk of gastrointestinal bleeding on triple
  therapy** [S1].
- **Where dual antiplatelet therapy is required with an OAC, aspirin and clopidogrel are recommended** [S1].
- **Discontinuation of antiplatelet therapy should be considered 12 months after stent implantation, acute
  coronary syndrome, or both, with continuation of OAC alone** [S1].

## Integrated care

> **An integrated care approach is recommended; such an approach aims to provide patient-centred
> comprehensive treatment delivered by a multidisciplinary team.** *GRADE: Strong; **Evidence: High*** [S1].

The **only High-evidence recommendation in the guideline that is about how care is organised rather than
what is prescribed.** It rests on three elements: **multidisciplinary teams, patient-centred care with a
focus on shared decision making, and application of eHealth** [S1].

> **All patients prescribed pharmacotherapy for AF, including core rhythm control and anticoagulation
> therapies, should have their treatment adherence and persistence regularly monitored and supported using
> accessible and patient-centred strategies.** *GRADE: Strong; Evidence: Low* [S1].

> **Long term persistence to OACs tends to decrease over time; about one-third to half of patients
> discontinue therapy within 2.5 years of initiation** [S1].

That figure is the reason adherence gets its own graded recommendation. The anticoagulant with High-quality
evidence behind it stops working when half the patients have stopped taking it — and **interventions to fix
this have shown mixed results** [S1].

## Where this connects in the compendium

The stroke score's own components point at five conditions already written here: **heart failure**
(C — including HFpEF), **hypertension** (H), **diabetes** (D), **stroke and TIA** (S₂) and **vascular
disease** (V). **Chronic kidney disease** governs anticoagulant choice, and **dialysis-dependent disease is
where the evidence runs out.**
