# Stroke and TIA — pre-hospital care, assessment and diagnosis

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-21 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** chapters 1–3 of 8 — pre-hospital care, early assessment and diagnosis, and the opening of acute
management. **Reperfusion therapy, secondary prevention and rehabilitation are not covered here** — see
*Unresolved*.

> **Australian living guideline.** Stroke Foundation, *Clinical Guidelines for Stroke Management* — Summary
> of recommendations, **June 2025** [S1]. "Living" means continuously updated rather than revised in
> editions, which makes this the best-current source in this set. Contrast heart failure, whose Australian
> guideline is eight years old.
>
> Recommendation strengths are the source's own: **Strong**, **Weak**, **Weak AGAINST**, and
> **Practice statement** (consensus-based). Practice points are guidance, not graded recommendations.

## Pre-hospital care

**Strong** — All stroke patients potentially eligible for reperfusion therapies should have an ambulance
dispatched as an **immediate response** and be managed as a **time critical emergency** [S1].

**Strong** — Ambulance services should **preferentially transfer** suspected stroke patients to a hospital
capable of delivering reperfusion therapies *as well as* stroke unit care, and should **pre-notify** the
hospital where the patient may be eligible [S1].

**Strong** — For patients in **major cities** with suspected stroke potentially eligible for reperfusion,
pre-hospital treatment in a **mobile stroke unit** is recommended [S1].

*Practice point:* general practitioners are encouraged to **educate reception staff in the FAST message and
to redirect any calls about suspected acute stroke to 000** [S1]. That is a practice-level instruction, not
a clinical one, and it is the one most likely to be overlooked.

## Transient ischaemic attack

**Strong** — All patients with suspected TIA — focal neurological symptoms due to focal ischaemia that have
**fully resolved** — should have **urgent clinical assessment** [S1].

**Strong** — Patients whose symptoms are **present or fluctuating** at initial assessment should be
**treated as having a stroke** and immediately referred for emergency department and stroke specialist
assessment, investigation and reperfusion therapy where appropriate [S1]. The distinction is resolution, not
severity.

**⚠ Weak recommendation AGAINST** — In TIA patients, **use of the ABCD² risk score in isolation** to
determine urgency of investigation **may delay recognition of atrial fibrillation and symptomatic carotid
stenosis in some patients and should be avoided** [S1].

This is the only explicit *recommendation against* in the retrieved text, and it targets a score in
widespread use. High-risk indicators usable in pre-hospital settings instead: crescendo TIA, current or
suspected AF, current anticoagulant use, carotid stenosis, or high ABCD² score [S1] — ABCD² as one input
among several, not as the gate.

**Strong** — All TIA patients with **anterior circulation symptoms** should undergo early carotid imaging
(CTA aortic arch to cerebral vertex, carotid Doppler, or MR angiography), preferably during initial
assessment and **not delayed more than 2 days** [S1].

**Strong** — Patients with suspected TIA should **commence secondary prevention therapy urgently** [S1].

**Strong** — All TIA patients should be **investigated for atrial fibrillation with ECG** at initial
assessment and referred for prolonged cardiac monitoring as required. Those with AF should **commence
anticoagulation early after brain imaging has excluded haemorrhage**, unless contraindicated [S1].

**Weak** — Patients with TIA should routinely undergo **brain imaging to exclude stroke mimics and
intracranial haemorrhage**; MRI where available improves diagnostic accuracy [S1].

## Assessment of suspected stroke

**Strong** — Pre-notified suspected stroke patients who may be reperfusion candidates should be **met at
arrival** and assessed by the stroke team or other experienced personnel [S1].

**Weak** — Clinical screening tools by ED staff are recommended **where an expert stroke team cannot
immediately assess** the patient [S1].

*Practice points* [S1]: initial diagnosis reviewed by a clinician experienced in stroke evaluation; severity
assessed and recorded on admission using a validated tool such as **NIHSS**; and a **blood glucose reading**
taken — **hypoglycaemia can present as a stroke mimic**.

## Imaging

**Strong** — Reperfusion candidates should undergo brain imaging **immediately**. All other suspected stroke
patients should have urgent brain CT or MRI — *"immediately where facilities are available and preferably
within 60 minutes"* [S1].

**Weak** — MRI is **more sensitive and specific than non-contrast CT** and is preferred where diagnostic
confirmation is required [S1]. *Practice statement:* either is acceptable acutely, but **must be immediately
accessible so as not to delay reperfusion** [S1].

**Strong** — If using CT to identify hyperdense thrombus, use **thin slice (<2 mm) non-contrast CT rather
than standard 5 mm slices** to improve sensitivity for vessel occlusion [S1].

**Strong** — Potential **endovascular thrombectomy** candidates should have vascular imaging **from aortic
arch to cerebral vertex** (CTA or MRA) to establish an occlusion target and assess proximal access [S1].

*Practice points* [S1]:
- Routine brain imaging **~24 hours after reperfusion therapy** to identify haemorrhagic transformation and
  delineate infarct extent — both bear on antithrombotic and DVT prophylaxis decisions.
- **Intravenous iodinated contrast should not be delayed by concerns about renal function** when clinically
  indicated; post-hydration with 0.9% saline is advisable.
- **Vascular imaging should not be performed for syncope or other non-focal presentations.**

## Cardiac investigations

**Weak** — Initial ECG monitoring for all stroke patients, generally **at least the first 24 hours**, mode
and duration guided by individual factors [S1].

**Strong** — For **embolic stroke of uncertain source**, longer-term ECG monitoring (external or implantable)
should be used [S1].

**Weak** — Echocardiography considered based on individual factors; **transoesophageal is more sensitive**
for suspected valvular, left atrial and aortic arch pathology [S1].

## Acute care — stroke unit

**Strong** — **All stroke patients should be admitted to hospital and treated in a stroke unit with an
interdisciplinary team** [S1]. *Practice point:* preferably admitted directly to a stroke unit **within
three hours of onset**; where transfer is not feasible, smaller hospitals should adhere as closely as
possible to stroke unit criteria, in geographically discrete units where possible [S1].

**Strong** — All acute stroke services should implement **standardised protocols to manage fever, glucose
and swallowing difficulties** [S1].

**Rehabilitation needs assessed within 24–48 hours** of stroke unit admission by the interdisciplinary team
using an appropriate tool (practice point) [S1].
