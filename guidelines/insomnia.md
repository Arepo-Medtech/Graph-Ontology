# Chronic insomnia disorder

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-21 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults of any age, including those with comorbidities.

> Every clinical sentence carries a source tag. Claims no retrieved source supports are listed under
> *Unresolved*. **No free Australian guideline exists for insomnia** — sources are European and
> international; the Australian layer is PBS listing only.

## Treatment aims

Restore sleep quality and daytime function. The central point of the current evidence is that the first
effective intervention is **not a drug**, and that drug treatment is mostly a short-term adjunct.

## Diagnosis

Clinical interview covering sleep and medical history; sleep questionnaires and diaries, with physical
examination and additional measures where indicated (grade A) [S2].

**Actigraphy is not recommended** for routine evaluation (grade C), though it may help differential
diagnosis (grade A) [S2].

**Polysomnography** is for evaluating other suspected sleep disorders — periodic limb movement disorder,
sleep-related breathing disorders — and for treatment-resistant insomnia (grade A) [S2].

## Non-drug treatment — first line

**Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the first-line treatment for chronic insomnia in
adults of any age, including patients with comorbidities, delivered either in person or digitally**
(grade A) [S2].

The digital equivalence matters for access: it removes the therapist-availability constraint that otherwise
pushes practice straight to medication. The same guideline notes that medications are more frequently used
worldwide because of inadequate resources [S1] — a resourcing fact, not a clinical one.

## Drug treatment

Offered **when CBT-I is not sufficiently effective** (grade A) [S2] — second line, not parallel.

For **short-term treatment (≤4 weeks)**: benzodiazepines (A), benzodiazepine receptor agonists (A),
daridorexant (A), and low-dose sedating antidepressants (B) [S2].

Longer-term treatment with these may be initiated in some cases, weighing advantages and disadvantages (B)
[S2]. **Orexin receptor antagonists can be used for up to 3 months or longer in some cases** (A) [S2] — the
only class the guideline extends beyond the four-week frame with a grade A.

Comparative efficacy across drug classes is addressed by a network meta-analysis of **170 randomised
trials**, assessing both acute and long-term treatment, with certainty rated by the CINeMA framework [S1].

## Australian context

PBS Schedule 4333, checked directly [S3]:

| Agent | PBS | Benefit type |
|---|---|---|
| Temazepam | listed | unrestricted (also R, A) |
| Nitrazepam | listed | unrestricted (also A) |
| Amitriptyline | listed | **unrestricted** |
| Zopiclone | listed | restricted |
| Melatonin | listed | authority required |
| **Daridorexant** | **not on Schedule 4333** | — |
| **Suvorexant, lemborexant** | **not on Schedule 4333** | — |
| **Zolpidem** | **not on Schedule 4333** | — |
| **Doxepin** | **not on Schedule 4333** | — |

The pattern mirrors migraine. **No orexin receptor antagonist is PBS-listed** — daridorexant, the agent
carrying a grade A recommendation for short-term use and the only class the European guideline extends past
four weeks, is unsubsidised in Australia. What remains unrestricted is benzodiazepines and amitriptyline.

An Australian prescriber following the evidence toward orexin antagonists and away from benzodiazepines
finds the subsidy pointing the other way.
