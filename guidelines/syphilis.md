## Why the threshold for testing is low

**Around 50% of people have no symptoms** and are diagnosed only by serological screening. **Syphilis can
mimic many other conditions — consider testing in all patients with unexplained symptoms** [S1].

Prevalence is rising: continuing high prevalence in men who have sex with men and remote Aboriginal and
Torres Strait Islander communities, increasing in major cities, and a **recent concerning increase in the
general population, especially women of reproductive age** [S1].

**Syphilis in pregnancy has led to the re-emergence of congenital syphilis.** If diagnosed during pregnancy,
**seek urgent specialist advice and ensure urgent and active recall for treatment** [S1].

## Staging — and why it decides treatment

| Stage | Definition [S1] |
|---|---|
| **Early infectious** | primary, secondary and **early latent** — asymptomatic infection acquired within the previous **2 years** |
| **Late latent** | asymptomatic infection acquired **more than 2 years** before diagnosis, **or duration unknown** |
| **Tertiary / late symptomatic** | neurological, cardiovascular or gummatous complications |

> **If there is any doubt about the length of infection, treat as late latent disease** [S1].

**After 2 years people are no longer infectious to sexual partners — but late latent syphilis can still be
transmitted to the fetus in pregnancy** [S1]. Those two facts travel together and the second is the one that
gets forgotten.

**Primary:** chancre at site of entry, usually painless with well-defined margin and indurated base;
**~30% have multiple ulcers or atypical presentations**; incubation **10–90 days (average 3 weeks)**; the
ulcer **heals spontaneously within a few weeks even if untreated** [S1].

**Secondary:** usually **more than 6 weeks** after infection; **skin involved in over 90%**, generalised
rash on trunk or just palms and soles, easily confused with drug eruption, pityriasis rosea or guttate
psoriasis; incubation **2–24 weeks (average 6 weeks)** [S1].

**Neurological signs — visual changes, tinnitus, deafness, cranial nerve palsies or meningitis — may
indicate early neurosyphilis requiring intravenous therapy** [S1].

## Treatment

| Situation | Recommended [S1] |
|---|---|
| **Early syphilis** (primary, secondary, early latent) | **Benzathine benzylpenicillin 2.4 MU (1.8 g) IMI stat**, given as **2 injections of 1.2 MU (0.9 g)** |
| **Late syphilis or unknown duration** (late latent >2 years) | **Benzathine benzylpenicillin 2.4 MU (1.8 g) IMI, given as 2 injections of 1.2 MU (0.9 g), weekly for 3 weeks** |

**The formulation must be long acting — short-acting formulations such as benzylpenicillin are
ineffective** [S1].

**Take an RPR on the day treatment commences** to provide an accurate baseline for monitoring response [S1].

**Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction** is common in primary and secondary syphilis: **6–12 hours** after treatment,
fever, headache, malaise, rigors and joint pains lasting several hours. Controlled with analgesics and rest.
**Patients should be alerted to the possibility and reassured** [S1].

### Penicillin unavailable or allergy — non-pregnant people

Assess allergy history **using a validated tool such as PENFAST**. For people with only a family history or
a reaction suggesting intolerance, **immediate delabeling and treatment with penicillin is appropriate**;
consider community oral challenge in those assessed as low risk [S1].

| Stage | Doxycycline regimen [S1] |
|---|---|
| Early (<2 years), and **presumptive treatment of exposed people** | **100 mg orally twice a day for 14 days** |
| Late (>2 years) or unknown duration | **100 mg orally twice a day for 28 days** |

**Non-penicillin regimens have less evidence than penicillin**; seek specialist advice if considering
alternatives [S1].

### Pregnancy

**Seek urgent specialist advice.** Treat as for non-pregnant according to stage. **Only penicillin has been
shown to be effective, so those allergic should be desensitised and treated with penicillin** — the
doxycycline alternative above does **not** apply. Fetal monitoring may be advised beyond 20 weeks. Ensure
the partner is tested and presumptively treated. Repeat testing during pregnancy. Arrange birth and
post-natal testing of mother and clinical review of baby at delivery [S1].

**HIV co-infection:** discuss with a specialist if **CD4 <350 cells/μL**, as lumbar puncture for CSF
examination may be advised [S1].

## Contact tracing — by stage

| Stage | Trace back [S1] |
|---|---|
| Primary | **3 months** plus duration of symptoms, or last negative test |
| Secondary | **6 months** plus duration of symptoms, or last negative test |
| Early latent | **12 months**, or most recent negative test |
| Late latent | test current partner(s); **if any doubt between early and late latent, trace as early latent** |

**Presumptively treat all sexual contacts from the last 3 months of patients with primary or secondary
syphilis, regardless of serology**, with benzathine benzylpenicillin 2.4 MU (1.8 g) IMI stat [S1].

**Ongoing sexual contacts of pregnant people are the highest priority and must be presumptively treated as
soon as possible to prevent re-infection during pregnancy** [S1].

Advise no sexual contact for **7 days** after treatment commences, or until the course is complete and
symptoms resolved, whichever is later; and no sex with partners from the last **3 months (primary), 6 months
(secondary) or 12 months (early latent)** until they are tested and treated [S1].

**Notifiable.** Syphilis registers operate in Queensland, South Australia and the Northern Territory with
direct contact numbers [S1].

## Follow-up

**Review clinically and with repeat RPR at 3 months, then 6 months, and if necessary 12 months** after
completing treatment. **A 4-fold drop (e.g. 1:8 to 1:2) indicates adequate response.** Seek specialist advice
if RPR is rising, or if a 4-fold drop is not achieved by 12 months [S1].

**Auditable outcome: 100% of patients with syphilis have had follow-up serology by 6 months** [S1].

## Serology notes

In patients with **prior treated syphilis, CMIA/EIA and TPPA antibodies are usually positive for life**.
**RPR is the marker of disease activity and treatment response** — it declines slowly untreated, and after
treatment usually falls rapidly and often reverts to non-reactive. **Re-infection is assessed on symptoms
and a significant rise in RPR titre** [S1].

In very early infection, **NAAT/PCR of an ulcer may be positive before seroconversion**. If primary syphilis
is clinically suspected but serology is negative, ensure a PCR swab has been done and **repeat serology after
2 weeks following presumptive treatment** [S1].

**Positive syphilis results in a child should be urgently discussed with a specialist and child protection
services** [S1].
