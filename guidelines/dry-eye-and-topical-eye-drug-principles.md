# Dry eye disease, and how to use a topical eye drug

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-22 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults in general practice, plus **eye-drop administration in children**. ⚠️ **Paediatric dry
eye disease is out of scope.**

## ⚠️ The finding: you are probably choosing on the wrong axis

> **There is no evidence that any lubricant is superior to another; patient preference after an empirical
> trial usually determines choice** [A1].
>
> ⚠️ **Preservative-free products are preferred for patients who require frequent lubricant use (eg >4
> times daily) or who use other eye drops; THIS IS MORE IMPORTANT THAN CHOICE OF LUBRICANT for these
> patients** [A1].

⚠️ **The decision that feels important — which brand — has no evidence behind it. The decision that
matters — preserved or not — is the one buried in the fine print of the pack.**

S1 agrees from the other direction: **preservative-free lubricants are preferred for severe dry eye as
preservatives can worsen DED and cause epithelium toxicity** [S1], and its patient advice includes
⚠️ **avoid allergens and irritants — especially any eye drops that have preservatives** [S1].

## What dry eye is

> **Dry eye is a multifactorial disease of the ocular surface characterised by a loss of homeostasis of the
> tear film and accompanied by ocular symptoms in which tear film instability and hyperosmolarity, ocular
> surface inflammation and damage, and neurosensory abnormalities play etiological roles** [S1] (**TFOS
> DEWS II**).

⚠️ **DED, in most cases, is not curable and involves mainly symptomatic management, even when
ophthalmologist referral is indicated** [S1]. **Tell the patient at the start: symptom relief rather than
cure** [S1].

**Prevalence approximately 7.4% in the Australian adult population**, and the **Blue Mountains Eye Study
(2003) showed 57.1% of the older population had one DED symptom** [S1]. **Prevalence increases with
age** [A1].

### Two mechanisms, often both

| | Mechanism | Leading cause |
|---|---|---|
| **Aqueous deficient (ADDE)** | **increased tear film osmolarity due to hyposecretion of the lacrimal gland** [S1] | — |
| **Evaporative (EDE)** | **increased tear film osmolarity from excessive water evaporation with normal lacrimal secretory function** [S1] | ⚠️ **meibomian gland dysfunction** [S1] |

⚠️ **Distinguishing between them can be difficult due to overlap** — **in Sjögren's syndrome, while it is
recognised as ADDE, the effect on the meibomian gland can lead to EDE as well** [S1].

**The tear film has a volume of approximately 7–10 µL** and **three layers, from superficial to deep: lipid,
aqueous and mucin** [S1].

### Look for a cause

**Consider secondary causes, eg meibomian gland dysfunction, Sjögren's syndrome, rheumatoid arthritis,
contact lenses, or adverse drug effects (eg anticholinergics, estrogens, isotretinoin)** [A1].

⚠️ **Facial surgery (eg in skin cancer management) can cause trigeminal nerve or facial nerve damage,
resulting in neurotrophic keratitis and corneal exposure keratopathy respectively — both of which increase
the severity of DED** [S1].

## Symptoms

**Dry and gritty sensation, foreign body sensation, soreness, stinging or burning, photophobia, intermittent
blurry vision, excessive tearing (due to reflex tear from corneal irritation), eye fatigue and fluctuating
diurnal vision** [S1]. ⚠️ **Symptoms tend to be worse on waking** [S1].

> ⚠️ **Excessive tearing is a dry eye symptom.** A watering eye is not evidence against the diagnosis.

⚠️ **Itching — consider allergic conjunctivitis** [S1]; see that guideline, where **itching is the most
pathognomonic symptom**.

**Scoring:** **a positive result for DEQ-5 is a score of ≥6** [S1]. **The OSDI is assessed 0–100: 13–22
mild, 23–32 moderate, ≥33 severe** [S1].

## Examination

**External examination focusing on skin, eyelids, adnexa, proptosis and any visible cranial nerve deficits
(mainly 3rd, 5th and 7th, which may affect lid closure and blink rate). Check for eyelid deformity such as
ectropion/entropion as well as incomplete closure and infrequent blinking** [S1].

⚠️ **The discharge should be a transparent liquid oil; thick or discoloured meibum indicates dysfunction.
Expression of the meibomian gland with gentle pressure to the lid margin from a cotton bud can determine
whether MGD is playing a role — and is also a validated way of unblocking the glands** [S1].

**Checking visual acuity is an integral part of the examination, as worse visual acuity drives
vision-related symptoms in dry eye** [S1].

**Without a slit lamp:** ⚠️ **an ophthalmoscope with a high plus lens will give a better resolution than
the ophthalmoscope alone** [S1].

**Normal tear film height is 0.3 mm; a low tear film height of <0.2 mm suggests ADDE** [S1].

⚠️ **Cells and flare in the anterior chamber indicate intraocular inflammation suggesting uveitis — this
will need discussion with an ophthalmologist ON THE DAY** [S1].

## Investigation

⚠️ **It is not expected that GPs carry out TBUT tests in the office, as specific equipment is required.**
**If you do not have access to these tests, then referring to a local optometrist is an option** [S1].

| Test | Abnormal [S1] |
|---|---|
| **Tear break-up time** | ⚠️ **less than 10 seconds** — **relatively specific in screening for dry eyes** |
| **Ocular staining** | **>5 corneal spots, >9 conjunctival spots, or lid margin epitheliopathy of ≥2 mm length and ≥25% width** |
| **Schirmer** | **no universal cut-off**, but **<10 mm in five minutes is considered abnormal** |

**Blood tests are usually not required in diagnosing DED** [S1].

## Treatment

### 1 · Before any drug

**Advice to give** [S1]: **reduce computer use (or lower computer screen height, which reduces lid
aperture); increase frequency of eye rest; avoid allergens and irritants — especially any eye drops that
have preservatives; humidify home and work; minimise air conditioners/heaters; avoid rubbing the eyes;
cease smoking and avoid second-hand smoke; reduce alcohol consumption; ensure contact lenses are inserted
and used correctly; wear sunglasses or tinted glasses, wrap-around frames in windy conditions; increase
omega-3 fatty acids**.

**A1 agrees on the environment:** **low humidity and/or increased air movement (eg air conditioners) can
increase tear evaporation and worsen symptoms; consider a humidifier or wrap-around glasses; avoid
prolonged screen viewing which can reduce blinking** [A1].

**Omega-3:** **a meta-analysis concluded that due to the anti-inflammatory effect of omega-3
supplementation on the ocular surface, it could help with DED** [S1]. ⚠️ **No dose is given.**

### 2 · Ocular lubricants

**Usually used first line** [A1]. ⚠️ **The dose is one to two drops, or one application, three to four
times a day as required. Some patients may need to apply them more frequently** [S1] — **drops may require
frequent administration, sometimes up to hourly, depending on severity** [A1].

| Form | Behaviour |
|---|---|
| **Drops** | **frequent administration, sometimes up to hourly** [A1] |
| **Gels** | **longer relief than drops, can blur vision but less so than ointments** [A1][S1] |
| **Ointments** | ⚠️ **most often overnight for symptoms that interrupt sleep or occur on awakening**; **may also be used through the day if severe**; **contain no preservatives**, but ⚠️ **lanolin in some products may cause irritation** [A1] |
| **Sprays** | **phospholipid (usually soy lecithin) liposomes thought to stabilise the lipid layer**; ⚠️ **sprayed onto CLOSED eyelids from about 10 cm away, eyes briefly remaining closed afterwards** [A1] |

⚠️ **Consider referral to an optometrist or ophthalmologist if there is an inadequate response after 2–4
weeks treatment** [A1].

### 3 · Treat the lids

**Treat any underlying meibomian gland dysfunction and/or blepharitis** [A1].

**Lid massage several times a day, in the direction of meibomian gland opening; warm compresses applied to
the eyelids (with eyes closed) for two to five minutes to soften the crusts; and gentle scrubbing of the
lashes with eyelid solutions or wipes** [S1].

> **S1 gives doses the conjunctivitis guideline recorded as missing:**
> **chloramphenicol 1% ointment topically to the eyelid margin twice daily for one to two weeks** for
> **anterior blepharitis** if eyelid hygiene does not suffice; **doxycycline 100 mg daily for two to four
> weeks** for **posterior blepharitis** if eyelid hygiene is inadequately controlling symptoms [S1].
>
> ⚠️ **Both are quoted and machine re-checkable.** ⚠️ **S1 frames the first as "some evidence".**

### 4 · Specialist territory

| | |
|---|---|
| **Topical corticosteroids** | **short periods in more severe cases to reduce ocular surface inflammation**, ⚠️ **only under specialist supervision with monitoring** [A1]. ⚠️ **If used long term they increase the risk of glaucoma and early cataracts** [S1] |
| **Ciclosporin** | **anti-inflammatory, shown to reduce symptoms and corneal surface damage** [S1]; used in **moderate-to-severe dry eye when lubricants are insufficient** [A1] |
| **Lifitegrast** | **available via the Special Access Scheme**; **direct comparative trials are limited but it appears to have a faster onset of action** [A1] |
| **Acetylcysteine** | **occasionally used for its mucolytic effect in filamentary keratitis associated with severe dry eyes**; ⚠️ **prepared by some pharmacies as 5% or 10% eye drops** [A1] |
| **Punctal plugs** | **prevent tear drainage** [A1]; **may help severe ADDE** [S1] |
| **Autologous serum eye drops** | listed by both [A1][S1] |

## ⚠️ How to use a topical eye drug

**This section closes two AMH general-principles topics and applies to every eye drop in this
compendium — glaucoma, conjunctivitis, allergic conjunctivitis and dry eye alike.**

### Why an eye drop has systemic effects

> **Excess drug drains over the lids onto the cheek, and through the tear duct to the nasal mucosa where it
> is absorbed into the systemic circulation (WITHOUT FIRST PASS THROUGH THE LIVER) and may lead to systemic
> adverse effects** [A1].

⚠️ **That is the mechanism behind timolol eye drops causing bronchospasm** — see the **glaucoma** guideline.
**Adverse effects due to systemic absorption are more common with eye drops than with ointments** [A1].

### ⚠️ The technique that halves it

> **Try not to blink after putting in a drop. Close your eyes and gently press against the inner corner of
> your eye with your finger (over the tear duct) for 1–2 minutes. This increases the effectiveness of the
> eye drop and helps reduce the amount of medicine that gets into the rest of your body** [A1].

**One drop is enough:** ⚠️ **the eye pouch will be full after a single drop**. **If you need to use another
eye drop at the same time of day, wait at least 5 minutes** [A1]. **If you think the first drop missed your
eye, put in another** [A1].

**Ointment:** **squeeze about 1 cm along the pouch then blink several times to spread it**; ⚠️ **if you need
drops at a similar time of day, use the ointment LAST** [A1].

**Practical:** **wash hands, sit or lie down, tilt head back, look up, gently pull down the lower eyelid to
form a pouch**; ⚠️ **avoid letting the tip of the dropper or tube touch your eyes, skin or any other
surface** [A1]. ⚠️ **Storing drops in a refrigerator lets you feel the cold drop go in** [A1].

### Children

**Hold the child's eyelids open between index finger and thumb of one hand and put drops in with the other.
If this is difficult, put a couple of drops onto the skin at the inner corner of the eye and wait for the
eyes to open** [A1].

### Preservatives

| | |
|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Benzalkonium chloride** | ⚠️ **the most frequently used preservative in ophthalmic products, but the most irritant.** Use **only if used infrequently or for short periods**; **consider the number of drops containing it**; ⚠️ **stopping it may not improve irritation immediately because of its accumulation in ocular tissues** [A1] |
| **Less irritant** | **polyquaternium, sodium chlorite, stabilised oxychloro complex and sodium perborate** — **may be used when drops are required more frequently**, though ⚠️ **they may still be irritating, especially in severe dry eye syndrome** [A1] |
| **None** | **single-use units are preservative free but may be more difficult to use** [A1] |

⚠️ **Using less irritant or preservative-free eye drops reduces the risk of damage to the conjunctiva and
cornea WITHOUT AFFECTING EFFICACY** [A1] — there is no trade-off to weigh.

**Preservative adverse effects: mild irritation (burning or stinging), symptoms of dry eye syndrome (with
disruption of tear film), severe conjunctivitis and, rarely, corneal scarring** [A1].

### Contact lenses

⚠️ **Do not wear contact lenses when using eye ointments or if there is an infection** [A1]. **Wearing soft
contact lenses (other than daily disposables) is generally not recommended while using eye drops containing
preservatives; remove before the drop and reinsert after at least 15 minutes** [A1].

### Housekeeping

⚠️ **Write the date on the bottle or tube when you first open it and discard it 28 days later** [A1].
⚠️ **Do not use topical drugs in open eye injuries** [A1]. **In general, there are 20 drops in each mL of an
eye drop** [A1].
