## Key principles

- **Examine repeatedly** to catch persisting or evolving signs and to judge response to treatment. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Give appropriate analgesia — it will not mask potentially serious causes of pain.** [S1]
- **Most children do not need investigations**; choose tests by the most likely cause. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Appendicitis in young children may be atypical** and can present as sepsis or perforation. [S1]
- **The key task is separating conditions that need specific or urgent — especially surgical — management** from the common self-limited ones (gastroenteritis, constipation, viral illness). Check for red flags before labelling pain non-specific. [S1]

## Presentations to recognise

- **Neonates** may show symptoms that parents read as abdominal pain — **examine thoroughly and keep a broad differential.** Infants may present only with crying, irritability, poor feeding, lethargy, drawing up the legs or refusing to weight bear. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Appendicitis under 5 years** is often diagnosed late and may be atypical: fever, vomiting and pain, perforation or sepsis, or diarrhoea mimicking gastroenteritis. **It needs careful fluid and antibiotic management.** [S1]
- **Pain character clues:** sudden onset — testicular or ovarian torsion/rupture, intussusception, volvulus, perforated viscus, incarcerated hernia. **Bilious vomiting** — volvulus or obstruction. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Hirschsprung enterocolitis** presents with sudden painful distension and bloody diarrhoea; these children can deteriorate rapidly and risk colonic perforation. [S1]

## Examine

- **Palpate for** focal vs generalised tenderness, percussion (then rebound) tenderness, guarding or rigidity, masses and faeces. **Serial examination is required. Examine the genitalia** (e.g. for testicular torsion). [S1]
- **Rectal or vaginal examination is rarely indicated**; discuss with a senior clinician first, and if needed do it only once. [S1]
- **Peritonism:** the child won't move in bed, can't walk or hop comfortably, and has percussion tenderness; internal rotation of the right hip can irritate an inflamed appendix. [S1]

## Investigate only what the differential needs

- **Targeted tests** may include urinalysis (± culture ± pregnancy test), LFTs, lipase, venous gas for lactate/acidosis, glucose for DKA, and FBE/CRP (± ESR). [S1]
- **AXR only if obstruction is suspected** — it does not help diagnose constipation. CXR if pneumonia is suspected. [S1]
- **Ultrasound after senior discussion** — very low yield if used indiscriminately. ⚠️ **Not indicated in the initial assessment of testicular torsion; it may delay time-critical surgery.** Useful for suspected ovarian torsion and when the history suggests intussusception, even with a normal examination. [S1]

## Treat

- **Severe pain: IV morphine or intranasal fentanyl may be needed as initial analgesia.** Fluid resuscitation may be required. [S1]
- **Consider fasting** depending on the differential; **give enteral or IV fluids if assessment or diagnosis is delayed** (e.g. 4 hours if very young). [S1]
- **Refer early** if a surgical or gynaecological diagnosis is possible; **consider a nasogastric tube** if bowel obstruction is suspected. [S1]

## Escalate, transfer, discharge

- **Involve the paediatric team** when a surgical cause is suspected, or with severe pain, peritonism, bilious vomiting, scrotal pain/redness and swelling, or if admission is needed. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Before transferring a child with a possible surgical condition**, ensure adequate analgesia, venous access and IV fluids. [S1]
- **Discharge when** there are no concerning features, the picture fits non-specific abdominal pain, a clear follow-up plan exists (e.g. GP) and parents know when to return. [S1]
