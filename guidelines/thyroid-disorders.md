# Hypothyroidism

**Common causes of primary hypothyroidism are Hashimoto's thyroiditis, radioactive iodine treatment and
thyroid surgery** [A1].

## Before starting

**Confirm the diagnosis biochemically: elevated TSH with low free T4.** ⚠️ **Where abnormalities are mild,
confirm that hypothyroidism persists over 2–3 months before starting treatment** [A1].

> ⚠️ **Consider secondary hypothyroidism** — TSH typically **normal or low** with low free T4, from pituitary
> or hypothalamic disease. **Assess the need for glucocorticoid replacement first: adrenal crisis may be
> precipitated if thyroid hormone is given without glucocorticoid replacement** [A1].

**Consider drug precipitants such as lithium and amiodarone** [A1].

## Drug choice

**Levothyroxine (T4) is the drug of choice** in both primary and secondary hypothyroidism; **its long
half-life gives stable serum concentrations and a long duration of effect** [A1].

**Liothyronine (T3)** has **faster onset and shorter duration**, and may be used in **severe hypothyroidism,
eg myxoedema coma**. ⚠️ **Not recommended in pregnancy, because the developing brain preferentially uses
T4** [A1].

## Dosing — levothyroxine

> ⚠️ **Paraphrased from the AMH monograph, no stored quote. Check before prescribing** — flagged `dose: true`.

| Situation | Dose [A1] |
|---|---|
| **Adult, by weight** | **approximately 1.6 micrograms/kg once daily based on ideal weight**, **rounded to the nearest 25 micrograms** |
| **Adult, usual range** | **oral 50–200 micrograms once daily** |
| **Adjustment** | **every 6–8 weeks if necessary, according to TSH** |
| ⚠️ **Frail, elderly, or ischaemic heart disease** | **initially 25–50 micrograms once daily**, increasing by **25 micrograms every 6–8 weeks** according to TSH |
| ⚠️ **Very frail or severe ischaemic heart disease** | **consider starting at 12.5 micrograms once daily** — **seek specialist advice** |
| **Child** | ⚠️ **seek paediatric endocrinologist advice**; **adjust every 2–4 weeks by 25 micrograms daily** to keep **serum thyroxine upper normal and TSH normal** |

**Liothyronine** [A1]: ⚠️ **seek endocrinologist advice**. **20–25 micrograms of liothyronine is approximately
equivalent in activity to 100 micrograms of levothyroxine.** **Usual range oral 20–60 micrograms daily in 2 or
3 doses.**

## Monitoring

**In primary hypothyroidism, monitor TSH and T4 every 6–8 weeks and adjust levothyroxine to bring TSH into
the low-to-mid normal range; then monitor annually, and more frequently in children** [A1]. **Additional
monitoring is needed with significant weight change, interacting drugs, or pregnancy** [A1].

> ⚠️ **In hypothyroidism due to hypothalamic or pituitary disease, TSH cannot be used as an indication of
> adequate replacement.** **Adjust to achieve T4 in the mid-to-upper normal range** [A1].

⚠️ **Allow at least 6 weeks after a dose change before checking TSH, because levothyroxine has a long
half-life** [A1].

## Special cases

**Pregnancy**: **monitor thyroid function each trimester** — **an increased levothyroxine dose is likely to be
required, starting in the first trimester** — and **reassess the maintenance dose 6–8 weeks postpartum** [A1].

**Congenital hypothyroidism**: **replacement should begin as early as possible, under careful supervision, to
prevent intellectual disability**; **monitor intellectual and physical development and thyroid
function** [A1].

## ⚠️ When levothyroxine appears not to work

AMH ranks the causes, and puts the commonest first [A1]:

1. ⚠️ **Lack of compliance — the most common reason**
2. **Impaired absorption by food, binding to other drugs in the gut (eg calcium, iron), or small intestinal disease**
3. **Increased clearance by interacting drugs**
4. **Loss of tablet potency from inappropriate storage**

## Practice points

**Replacement is lifelong**, except for some people whose hypothyroidism followed **subacute or postpartum
thyroiditis, radioactive iodine treatment, or partial thyroidectomy** [A1].

⚠️ **After a transient treatment interruption, if clinical symptoms are minor, it is usually unnecessary to
reintroduce levothyroxine slowly at low doses — including in ischaemic heart disease or the elderly** [A1].
That is the opposite of the *initiation* advice, and the distinction is easy to lose.

⚠️ **Overtreatment, shown by suppressed TSH, may increase the risk of bone density loss and fracture,
particularly in elderly women** [A1].

⚠️ **Clearance of many drugs — digoxin, hypnotics, anaesthetic agents, analgesics — is reduced in
hypothyroidism. Lower doses may be sufficient, and doses may need increasing as the person becomes
euthyroid** [A1].

## Subclinical hypothyroidism

AMH files this under **Unanswered questions** [A1]. **Mild TSH elevation, often asymptomatic, with normal
T4**; **risk of progression to overt hypothyroidism is higher with thyroid peroxidase antibodies and rising
TSH**. **Monitor thyroid function every 6–12 months.**

> **The decision to begin T4 replacement should be individualised; TSH >10 mIU/L is generally accepted as an
> indication to start** [A1].

**Euthyroid goitre** is likewise unsettled: **conflicting evidence for levothyroxine in reducing size or
stopping growth**, weighed against **harm from TSH suppression, eg decreased bone density**, with **a risk of
inducing hyperthyroidism in patients with autonomous nodules**. ⚠️ **Avoid levothyroxine if TSH is already
suppressed** [A1].

# Hyperthyroidism

**The most common causes are Graves' disease and toxic multinodular goitre**; others include **toxic adenoma
and sub-acute thyroiditis** [A1].

**Confirm with suppressed TSH and high free T4 and/or high free T3** [A1].

⚠️ **Exclude sub-acute thyroiditis, as recovery is spontaneous and definitive treatment is usually not
needed** [A1] — and **antithyroid drugs are ineffective in it** [A1].

**Consider iodine-containing precipitants: amiodarone, IV contrast media, kelp.** ⚠️ **Iodine-induced
hyperthyroidism may initially resist antithyroid drugs but may ultimately be self-limiting** [A1].

## Drug choice

**Carbimazole and propylthiouracil block thyroid hormone synthesis; in high doses propylthiouracil also
inhibits peripheral conversion of T4 to T3.** ⚠️ **Both can cause agranulocytosis** [A1].

> **Carbimazole is generally preferred to propylthiouracil, except in thyroid storm or the first trimester of
> pregnancy** [A1].

**Beta-blockers** are used **short term for symptom relief — tremor, tachycardia, sweating — particularly in
hyperthyroidism secondary to sub-acute thyroiditis**. **Beta1-selective and nonselective agents are both
effective; there has been more experience with propranolol** [A1].

**Iodine** may be used **short term in Graves' disease to prepare for thyroid surgery, after control with
antithyroid drugs**; ⚠️ **long-term use may exacerbate hyperthyroidism** [A1]. **Radioactive iodine** is used
in **toxic multinodular goitre, or in Graves' disease relapsing after antithyroid drugs — seek specialist
advice** [A1].

## Dosing — antithyroid drugs

> ⚠️ **Specialists should guide treatment** [A1]. Doses paraphrased from AMH monographs, no stored quote,
> flagged `dose: true`.

| | Carbimazole [A1] | Propylthiouracil [A1] |
|---|---|---|
| **Initial** | **oral 10–45 mg daily (up to 60 mg daily in severe cases) in 2 or 3 doses for 3–4 weeks** | **oral 200–400 mg daily in 2–4 doses for 3–4 weeks** |
| **Maintenance (adjusted regimen)** | **usually 5–15 mg daily**, ⚠️ **highly variable, range 2.5–40 mg daily** | **25–300 mg daily in divided doses** |
| **Block-replace** | continue initial dose and **add 100–150 micrograms levothyroxine when T4 is in the normal range** | same |
| ⚠️ **Thyroid storm** | **oral 60–80 mg daily in divided doses, gradually reduced** | **oral 600–1200 mg daily in divided doses, gradually reduced** |

**Propranolol** for **tachyarrhythmias including hyperthyroidism**: **oral 10–40 mg 3 or 4 times daily** [A1].

## Regimens

**The aim is clinical and biochemical euthyroidism — T4 and/or T3 about mid-normal range, with eventual
normalisation of TSH over several months** [A1]. **In Graves' disease, antithyroid drugs are usually given for
12–18 months** [A1].

**Adjusted regimen** (the one usually used): **start high for 3–4 weeks, then reduce by about half according
to response**. ⚠️ **Base the initial adjustment on plasma T4/T3, as TSH may remain suppressed for a few months
after starting** [A1]. **Review every 4–6 weeks, reducing to the lowest effective dose** [A1].

**Block-replace regimen**: maintain the high antithyroid dose and **add levothyroxine after T4/T3 normalise,
about 6 weeks**; **confirm euthyroidism every 3 months**. ⚠️ **No longer indicated in Graves' disease** — it
**may occasionally be used by specialists, eg in unstable disease or inoperable goitre** — and
⚠️ **contraindicated in pregnancy** [A1].

**With radioactive iodine**: ⚠️ **stop the antithyroid drug at least 4 days before and restart no sooner than
3 days after, to allow uptake of iodine into the thyroid** [A1]. **Assess effect by tapering off antithyroid
drugs 2–4 months after treatment** [A1].

**Remission** on long-term antithyroid treatment for Graves' disease **occurs in fewer than 50% of people
after about 1–2 years, and may be temporary** [A1].

## ⚠️ Thyroid storm

> **A medical emergency, also known as thyrotoxic crisis, with exaggerated symptoms of hyperthyroidism — eg
> fever, tachycardia, CNS dysfunction and GI symptoms. There is usually a precipitating event, eg
> infection** [A1].

**Treat according to specialist advice: IV fluids, high-dose antithyroid drugs, propranolol and
corticosteroids are required. Iodide, lithium and paracetamol (for fever) may also be used** [A1].

## Subclinical hyperthyroidism and smoking

**Persistently suppressed TSH with normal T4/T3** is **associated with increased risk of AF in older people
and decreased bone density in postmenopausal women**. ⚠️ **There is no consensus on whether it should be
treated, as evidence to guide clinical decisions is limited.** **Iodine loads should be avoided — IV contrast
media, amiodarone, kelp — as iodine excess may precipitate overt hyperthyroidism** [A1].

⚠️ **Cigarette smoking is associated with increased risk of Graves' disease and its complications, eg
ophthalmopathy; disease may be more severe than in non-smokers, and smokers may have lower response rates and
need higher antithyroid doses (limited data)** [A1].
