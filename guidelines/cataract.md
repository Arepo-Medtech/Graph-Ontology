# Cataract

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-22 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** assessment, referral, prioritisation, surgery and postoperative care for clinically significant cataract.

> **Australian primary source.** Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care (ACSQHC).
> ***Cataract Clinical Care Standard***, **published 2021** [S1]. **Eight quality statements.**

**Ophthalmology's first appearance in this compendium** — and the subject is, by the Standard's own account,
**the most common elective surgery diagnosis in Australia** [S1].

## ⚠️ Having a cataract is not a reason to operate

> **Discuss the possible options to manage the patient's symptoms and advise them that the presence of
> cataract alone is not an indication for surgery. Patients with lens opacities that do not cause visual
> symptoms or limit daily activities can continue to be managed and monitored in the primary care
> setting** [S1].

**That is the first substantive instruction in a standard about the country's commonest elective operation.**
The trigger is not the lens — it is **visual impairment that interferes with their ability to carry out their
usual daily tasks, considering the possible impact on their ability to live independently** [S1].

**And the measurement most people reach for is explicitly weak:**

> ⚠️ **Visual acuity of 6/12 or worse may provide a useful objective measure of visual impairment, but may
> significantly underestimate function — for example, in conditions of high or low light. Glare or contrast
> sensitivity may be disabling without an impact on visual acuity. Some patients will have higher visual
> acuity needs, such as for occupational activities** [S1].

**A number that underestimates the problem, and a disability that the number cannot see at all.** Vision-related
activity limitations include **loss of ability to work, drive, carry out daily tasks, or care for themselves or
others — for example, an increased risk of falls in older people with risk factors** [S1].

## ⚠️ The patients most likely to be disappointed

> **Most patients have an improvement in visual function after surgery. Patients most likely to report a poor
> outcome after surgery include those with good self-assessed preoperative visual function** [S1].

**The better a patient says they were seeing, the more likely they are to regret the operation.** It is the
mirror of the threshold above, and it is the single most useful sentence in the Standard for a consenting
conversation.

**Also consider conditions that increase the risk of complications or a poor outcome (such as diabetes,
diabetic retinopathy or uveitis) or limit the extent of visual gain (such as age-related macular
degeneration)** [S1].

## ⚠️ Indicators that audit the referral system, not the operation

| Indicator [S1] | |
|---|---|
| **3a** | Evidence of a locally approved protocol to allocate appointments |
| **3b** | **Proportion of referrals received that included the required patient information** |
| ⚠️ **3c** | **Proportion of patients referred that *did not meet the criteria* for referral** |
| ⚠️ **3d** | **Proportion of patients referred for cataract surgery who *had* cataract surgery** |
| **5a** | Evidence of a locally approved prioritisation protocol |
| ⚠️ **7a** | **Proportion of patients who received intracameral administration of antibiotics at the end of surgery** |

**Six indicators for eight statements**, and **three of the six measure the referral pathway rather than the
care**. **Indicator 3c counts referrals that should not have been made; 3d is the conversion rate** — how
many referred patients actually proceed.

**Together they measure whether primary care and ophthalmology agree about who needs an operation** — which,
given statement 1's insistence that cataract alone is not an indication, is precisely the question.

**Statements 1, 2, 4, 6 and 8 carry no indicator** — assessment, shared decision making, the surgical
indication itself, second-eye surgery and postoperative care are unmeasured.

## Statement 1 — primary care

**A patient with visual problems due to suspected cataract requires an ocular examination, preferably
performed using a slit lamp biomicroscope or direct ophthalmoscope, and an assessment of how their visual
problems are affecting their life. Optometry referral may be a suitable initial option** [S1].

**If the patient does not want to consider surgery** and there is no other indication for ophthalmology
assessment, **referral may not be appropriate** — instead **provide support to reduce the impact of their
visual problems, including refractive correction, tinted lenses to reduce glare, or use of suitable equipment
to optimise vision** [S1].

⚠️ **Willingness to have surgery is part of the referral criteria**, not an afterthought — the standard treats
an unwilling patient as a reason not to refer, and redirects them to low-vision services rather than a
waiting list.

**A standardised referral template can be effective for improving the appropriateness of referral, as well as
improving the quality of information needed to triage** [S1].

## Statements 2 and 3 — information, and who gets seen first

**Options include surgical or non-surgical options, such as visual aids or watchful waiting** [S1], with
information **both verbally and in a written format that is easy to use — for example, in large font or
electronically** [S1]. **A written format designed for the vision the patient currently has.**

**Prioritisation for assessment** uses **an agreed local protocol… prioritising according to clinical need,
including social and cultural circumstances and barriers to accessing care** [S1]. **Referrals should be
reviewed by a credentialed ophthalmic clinician** [S1], and **processes should be in place to monitor patients
waiting for first ophthalmology appointments in case their clinical needs and priority change** [S1].

⚠️ **On Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander health, this Standard does what the paediatric documents in this
compendium do not** [S1]:

> **The level of comorbidities in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people, their age at diagnosis and
> disparities in their health outcomes means that health care for this population needs to be refocused to
> meet the unique needs of each patient.**

**A stated basis — comorbidity burden, age at diagnosis, outcome disparity — and a required response.** Set
against the five paediatric guidelines here that list ethnicity as a bare risk factor (Finding 4 of
`docs/unresolved-audit.md`), **this is what the framing looks like when it is done.**

## Statement 5 — the harms of waiting

**Prioritisation protocols should enable those patients at greatest risk of harm from delayed surgery and
those who are most likely to benefit from surgery to be treated first** [S1].

> **Potential harms associated with delayed surgery include risks of falls or traffic accidents, or increased
> complexity of later surgery — for example, in patients with densely brunescent or white cataract. Social
> factors that may affect the ability of patients to access care should also be considered, including
> remoteness, language and culture** [S1].

⚠️ **Waiting makes the operation harder.** A cataract left long enough becomes a technically more difficult
case — the waiting list is not neutral. **Comorbid conditions that may increase urgency include acute angle
glaucoma and posterior segment disease, where fundal access is required for monitoring or treatment** [S1].

## Statement 6 — the second eye

**Discuss second-eye surgery and its timing when first-eye surgery is being planned** [S1], considering
**existing or anticipated anisometropia… and its impact on vision and visual function, including stereopsis
(depth perception), stereoacuity and falls risk** [S1].

**Delaying allows complete postoperative recovery… and the opportunity to assess and plan surgery based on the
results of first-eye surgery** [S1].

⚠️ > **There is limited evidence to support second-eye surgery on the same or next day.** It may be
> considered for patients **at low risk of complications during and after surgery, or at risk from delayed
> second eye surgery** · **to avoid a second general anaesthetic, when general anaesthetic is required** ·
> ⚠️ **for whom distance and travel are considerable barriers, such as in remote and very remote areas** [S1]

> **Ensure that patients are informed of the consequences of complications, including the risk of potentially
> blinding complications in both eyes, such as endophthalmitis or toxic anterior segment syndrome** [S1].

**Geography is a stated indication for a procedure with limited evidence, against a risk of bilateral
blindness.** The Standard sets both out and leaves the trade to a shared decision — one of the most explicit
access-versus-risk statements in this compendium.

## ⚠️ Statement 7 — the recommended prophylaxis is off-label

> **Evidence-based guidelines recommend use of intracameral antibiotics for endophthalmitis prophylaxis in
> preference to topical pre- or postoperative antibiotic eye drops. *Therapeutic Guidelines: Antibiotic*
> recommend **cefazolin intracamerally as a single dose at the end of surgery**, and **avoiding use of
> quinolones or vancomycin, which lack evidence**; consider the risk of antibiotic resistance.
> ⚠️ **Prophylactic use of topical antibiotic solutions or eye drops is not recommended, and there is little
> evidence to support their use** [S1].

And then:

> ⚠️ **Intracameral use of cefazolin is off-label, and its addition to the formulary will need to be approved
> by the local Drugs and Therapeutics Committee under routine use of an off-label medicine** [S1].

**The nationally recommended, indicator-audited prophylaxis for the country's commonest elective operation is
an off-label use requiring committee approval at every hospital that wants to comply.** Indicator 7a measures
whether it was given.

**Fallbacks** [S1]: **if no commercially prepared intracameral formulation is available, antibiotic eye drops
may be considered to avoid the risk of dilution errors and contamination**; **seek advice in the case of
patients with hypersensitivity to penicillin or cephalosporins**; and **if postoperative antibiotic eye drops
are used, chloramphenicol 0.5% eye drops are recommended — stipulate the duration (maximum seven days) to
avoid overuse**.

**Anti-inflammatory drops**: **consider corticosteroid eye drops, with or without NSAIDs, for patients at high
risk of developing cystoid macular oedema — such as patients with diabetic retinopathy or other high-risk
ocular comorbidities** [S1]. **Adverse effects include allergic reactions to antibiotics, increased
intraocular pressure with corticosteroids and, rarely, epithelial damage with non-steroidal
anti-inflammatories** [S1].

## Statement 8 — postoperative care

**Postoperative care is provided by the operating ophthalmologist or a designated team member** [S1].
**Determine the exact frequency of postoperative review, taking into account the surgical technique, any
operative or postoperative complications, and the person's ability to access care. Early review (within 24
hours) may be warranted for patients at high risk of complications** [S1].

## Where this connects in the compendium

| Guideline here | Connection |
|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Antimicrobial stewardship** | **prophylactic topical antibiotics are not recommended**; **quinolones and vancomycin are to be avoided for lack of evidence**; and postoperative drops, if used, are capped at **seven days to avoid overuse** |
| **Type 2 diabetes** | **diabetic retinopathy** both raises complication risk and triggers postoperative anti-inflammatory drops |
| **Osteoporosis** · **Hip fracture care** · **Dementia** | ⚠️ **falls** — visual impairment is a falls risk, and **delayed surgery is listed as causing falls and traffic accidents** |
| **Fitness to drive — seizures and epilepsy** | *Assessing Fitness to Drive* carries **its own vision chapter**; loss of ability to drive is a listed activity limitation here |
| **Colonoscopy** · **Peripheral intravenous catheters** | the other ACSQHC standards whose indicators measure **appropriateness of referral and use**, not only process |
| **Stillbirth** · **Stroke** | the Commission's pattern of pairing **cultural safety recommendations with a stated basis** — which this Standard does and five paediatric guidelines here do not |
