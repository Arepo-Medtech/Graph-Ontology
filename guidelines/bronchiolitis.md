# Bronchiolitis

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-22 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** viral lower respiratory tract infection in children, generally under 12 months.

> **Australian primary source.** The Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne. ***Clinical Practice Guidelines:
> Bronchiolitis***. **Last updated November 2023. PIC Endorsed** [S1], with the management diagram read from
> its image [S2].

**The destination the acute asthma guideline sends every wheezing infant to** — *children <12 months of age
presenting with wheeze are likely to have bronchiolitis that does not respond to bronchodilator treatment*.
**This is that guideline, and it says so far more forcefully from its own side.**

## ⚠️ Seven drug classes, prohibited

> **Do not administer**
> - **Beta-2 agonists, including in infants with a personal or family history of atopy**
> - **Corticosteroids (nebulised, oral, intramuscular or intravenous)**
> - **Adrenaline (nebulised, intramuscular or intravenous) except in peri-arrest or arrest situation**
> - **Nebulised hypertonic saline**
> - **Antibiotics, including azithromycin**
> - **Antivirals**
> - **Caffeine** [S1]

**The largest single prohibition list in this compendium**, under its own heading, and each entry closes a
specific loophole:

- ⚠️ **"including in infants with a personal or family history of atopy"** removes the one exception a
  clinician would reach for. The wheezy infant whose sibling has asthma **still does not get salbutamol.**
- **"nebulised, oral, intramuscular or intravenous"** forecloses route-switching.
- **"including azithromycin"** names the antibiotic most often given for its anti-inflammatory claim.
- **Caffeine appears twice** — once in the prohibition list, and once as **"there is insufficient evidence
  for the use of caffeine in prevention of bronchiolitis-associated apnoea"** [S1].

**Two more prohibitions sit outside that list**: **deep nasal suction beyond the nasopharynx is not
recommended**, and **chest physiotherapy is not indicated** [S1].

## ⚠️ And no investigations either

> **In most children with bronchiolitis, no investigations are required or recommended.** Investigations
> should only be undertaken when there is **deterioration or diagnostic uncertainty** (eg cardiac murmur with
> signs of congestive cardiac failure) [S1].

| | [S1] |
|---|---|
| **Chest X-ray** | **not indicated and may lead to unnecessary treatment with antibiotics.** **CXRs cannot discriminate between viral and bacterial infections and are poorly reproducible in this age group** |
| **Blood tests** | **not indicated (including blood gas, FBE, blood cultures)** |
| **Virological testing** (NPS or aspirate) | ⚠️ **not indicated in the management of individual children and should not be used to guide treatment** |

**The CXR entry names the downstream harm, not the test's own cost** — an X-ray leads to antibiotics, which
are themselves on the prohibition list. **The two refusals are one argument.**

**And knowing the virus changes nothing.** In a disease defined by its virology, the guideline states that
identifying the virus **should not be used to guide treatment** — because there is no treatment to guide.

## Key points

> 1. **Bronchiolitis is a clinical diagnosis. Investigations are not indicated**
> 2. **The goal of management is to maintain hydration and oxygenation. Medication beyond simple analgesia is
>    not indicated**
> 3. **Infants with a history of prematurity or cardiopulmonary disease are at higher risk of deterioration
>    and are more likely to require admission**
> 4. **Use nasal prong oxygen for persistent SpO₂ <90%. High flow nasal prong (HFNP) therapy should be
>    reserved for failure of nasal prong supplemental oxygen** [S1]

**Background** [S1]: **a viral lower respiratory tract infection, generally affecting children <12 months**;
**peak severity usually around day 2–3 from the onset of increased work of breathing, with resolution over
7–10 days**; **self-limiting, usually requiring no treatment or intervention**; **a cough may persist for
weeks**.

**Telling parents the cough will last weeks is part of the treatment**, because the alternative is a
re-presentation and a course of azithromycin.

## Assessment

**A coryzal prodrome followed by one or more of**: **cough · fever · difficulty feeding · apnoeic episodes ·
unsettledness/irritability** [S1]. **Examination**: **tachypnoea · use of accessory muscles · bilateral wheeze
and/or fine crepitations · hypoxia** [S1].

**Risk factors for more serious illness** — **consider hospital admission for these children, even if
presenting early in illness with mild symptoms** [S1]:

> **Chronic lung disease** · **Congenital heart disease** · **Chronic neurological conditions** ·
> **Gestational age <37 weeks, or chronological age at presentation <10 weeks** · **Growth restriction** ·
> ⚠️ **Indigenous ethnicity** · **Immunodeficiency** · **Exposure to cigarette smoke** · **Trisomy 21**

⚠️ **Fifth document in this compendium to list Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people as a risk factor
without a stated basis** — and **the bluntest phrasing of the five**, at two words. The paediatric sepsis and
febrile child guidelines use the same construction; **Western Australia's sepsis guideline is the only one
that instead names remoteness, deprivation, delayed access and language.** See *Unresolved* and Finding 4 of
`docs/unresolved-audit.md`.

**Apnoea** is defined as **absence of breathing for >15 seconds, often associated with bradycardia and/or
oxygen desaturation** [S1].

## ⚠️ Oxygen — a guideline arguing against its own monitor

- **Target SpO₂ is 90%**, unless underlying cardiopulmonary disease sets a different target [S1]
- ⚠️ **Infants with bronchiolitis will have brief episodes of mild/moderate desaturations <90%. These brief
  desaturations are not a reason to commence supplemental oxygen** [S1]
- **Nasal prong oxygen should only be instituted when oxygen saturations are persistently <90%** [S1]
- ⚠️ **It is unnecessary to aim for SpO₂ in mid-high 90's** [S1]
- **Trial off supplemental oxygen (nasal prong or HFNP) every 6 hours** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Once no longer requiring supplemental oxygen for 2 hours, discontinue oxygen saturation
  monitoring.** **Continue observations 2–4 hourly and reinstate intermittent oxygen monitoring if clinically
  deteriorating** [S1]

**Turning the saturation probe off is a recommendation.** The guideline treats continuous pulse oximetry as a
driver of unnecessary oxygen and unnecessary admission, and says to stop it once it has stopped being useful.

**HFNP** is for **failure of low-flow**: **if saturations remain persistently <90% and there is no
improvement in respiratory state despite 2 hours of low flow oxygen, consider HFNP**; and **HFNP is not
recommended in children with SpO₂ consistently ≥90%** [S1].

## Hydration

- **Children are often more settled if comfort oral feeds are continued** [S1]
- **When non-oral hydration is required, nasogastric (NG) hydration is the route of choice and can be safely
  used in children on respiratory support** [S1]
- ⚠️ **NG bolus feeding is safe and should be commenced at two-thirds maintenance rate because of potential
  for increased ADH secretion** [S1]
- **IV hydration should be reserved for children in whom NG feeding is not possible. If IV fluid is used it
  should be isotonic with added glucose** [S1]

**The two-thirds rule has its mechanism attached** — SIADH in bronchiolitis, and the hyponatraemia that
follows full-maintenance fluid. **NG before IV** is the other inversion of habit here.

## The one permitted medication

> **Chloral hydrate may be used to reduce agitation in children with worsening respiratory distress,
> regardless of need for respiratory support. It is most effective in children <2 years and <15 kg** [S1]

| | [S1] |
|---|---|
| **Dose 0–2 years** | **10 mg/kg initial dose, every 4–6 hours** |
| **Escalation** | **may increase to 15 mg/kg and then up to 20 mg/kg every 4–6 hourly if required, but recommend increased monitoring and senior clinician involvement is required outside of critical care areas** |
| **Adverse effects** | **excess sedation, respiratory depression, hypotension, paradoxical agitation** |
| **Caution** | **renal or hepatic impairment** |

**A sedative, in a respiratory illness, with respiratory depression on its own adverse effect list.** The
guideline permits it because **agitation increases work of breathing** — and hedges it with escalating
monitoring rather than a hard ceiling.

## The management algorithm, transcribed from the image

**Verification basis differs for this section** — read off the diagram, verdict **`pass_image_transcription`**,
not machine re-checkable [S2].

**Two parallel assessments** [S2]:

**1. Assess respiratory status** — **is the infant persistently hypoxaemic (SpO₂ <90% in room air)?**

- **No** → **consider period of observation if child has moderate to severe WOB, risk factors for severe
  disease or apnoeas. Otherwise discharge home**
- **Yes** → **commence oxygen at 2 L/min by low flow nasal prongs.** **Perform hourly observations for
  moderate disease (continuous observations if severely unwell).** **If patient has severe/life threatening
  illness, seek ICU review and start HFNP or CPAP**
- **Assess treatment response within 2–3 hours (or sooner if unwell)**
- **Ongoing hypoxaemia (SpO₂ <90%), tachycardia, tachypnoea and/or WOB despite 2–3 hours of treatment?** —
  **if hypoxaemia is mild and patient clinically improved, consider a longer trial of nasal prong oxygen**
  - **No** → **titrate and wean oxygen to keep SpO₂ ≥90%. When not requiring oxygen ≥2 hours, cease oxygen
    saturation monitoring**
  - **Yes** → **consider high flow nasal cannula oxygen (HFNP) at 2 L/kg per min and titrate FiO₂ to keep
    SpO₂ ≥90%**
- **Does the infant respond — SpO₂ ≥90%; HR, RR and/or WOB improves; no significant or recurrent apnoeas
  associated with desaturation and/or bradycardia?**
  - **No** → **seek paediatric intensive care input (onsite or via retrieval services). Consider CPAP in
    severe disease**
  - **Yes** → **titrate FiO₂ to room air (to keep SpO₂ ≥90%). Trial off oxygen every 6 hours and recommence
    nasal prong oxygen if SpO₂ <90%. Cease HFNP when stable in room air**

**2. Assess hydration status** — **oral intake <50% over 12 hours, or clinically dehydrated** → **commence NG
feeds at 2/3 maintenance rate. Cease NG feeds when patient able to tolerate 2/3 oral feeds** [S2]

> ⚠️ **NOTE: If infant has risk factors for severe disease, apnoeas, or moderate–severe WOB, consider a
> period of hospital observation regardless of whether they require oxygen or fluid therapy** [S2]

**The flow rates are only in the diagram**: **2 L/min low flow**, **2 L/kg/min HFNP**. Neither appears in the
guideline text.

## Escalation and discharge

**Consult the local paediatric team** for **specific risk factors · requiring supplemental oxygen · less than
half normal oral intake and clinically dehydrated · respiratory status assessed as moderate or severe** [S1].

**Transfer** for **severe bronchiolitis · risk factors for more severe illness · care above the level of
comfort of the local hospital · oxygen requirement on HFNP with FiO₂ >50%** [S1].

**Discharge** when **SpO₂ ≥90% and feeding is adequate** [S1]. ⚠️ **Infants <8 weeks of age are at increased
risk of representation** [S1]. **Transfer to Hospital in the Home on home oxygen can be considered after a
period of observation in selected infants as per local policies** [S1]. **Parents should be educated about
the illness, the expected progression and when and where to seek further advice** [S1].
