# Opioid analgesic stewardship in acute pain

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-21 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults and children, hospital and primary care.

> **Australian primary source.** Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care (ACSQHC).
> ***Opioid Analgesic Stewardship in Acute Pain Clinical Care Standard*** [S1]. **Nine quality statements.**

> **The goal of the Standard is to ensure the appropriate use and review of opioid analgesics for the
> management of acute pain, to optimise patient outcomes and reduce the potential for opioid-related
> harm** [S1].

**This guideline closes a gap named in the low back pain guideline of this compendium**, which
cross-references this standard for opioid use and transfer of care.

**Opioid analgesics are high-risk medicines, widely used in hospitals and primary care to manage pain** [S1].

## ⚠️ The admission the whole standard is built on

> **Patients cannot be reliably assessed for risk of respiratory depression (OIVI)** [S1].

Risk stratification does not work well enough to rely on. So the standard does not try to predict who will
stop breathing — it **monitors sedation in everyone**, continuously, and acts on it.

> **Excess sedation is a reliable indicator of respiratory depression (OIVI)** [S1].

That is the substitution: an unmeasurable risk replaced by a measurable sign.

## ⚠️ Sedation score, not pain score, governs the next dose

> **Patients with excess sedation (such as a score of greater than 1) should be managed by de-escalating the
> opioid analgesic dose and continued monitoring. Withhold all opioids until the patient is awake. If more
> analgesia is needed, a smaller dose should be given regardless of pain score** [S1].

**"Regardless of pain score."** In thirty-six guidelines this is the only instruction that explicitly
**decouples dosing from the patient's reported symptom**, and it does so because the competing signal predicts
death.

**Patient sedation levels should be monitored and the results documented in the monitoring chart in the
patient medical record** [S1]. **Monitoring sedation should always be paired with appropriate opioid analgesic
prescription and dose adjustment.** **Excess sedation may require escalation of care, according to locally
approved protocols.** **Consider the administration of naloxone to reverse respiratory depression (OIVI),
according to a locally approved protocol** [S1].

## ⚠️ Modified-release opioids in acute pain

> **Modified-release opioid analgesics cannot be safely or rapidly titrated and their use in acute pain should
> be exceptional and not routine** *(Quality statement 5)* [S1].

> **There is no evidence to support the use of modified-release opioid analgesics for acute pain. Some emerging
> evidence shows that their use is problematic. For example, modified-release opioid analgesics following
> surgery are associated with increased risk of opioid-related harm and complications** [S1].

> **The Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) states that modified-release products should only be used where
> the pain is opioid-responsive and the patient requires daily, continuous, long-term treatment. Long-term
> treatment does not align with the definition of acute pain** [S1].

A regulatory position and a clinical one arriving at the same conclusion by different routes — the indication
itself excludes acute pain.

## Why the standard exists

> **An opioid analgesic weaning and cessation plan is particularly important for patients prescribed opioid
> analgesics because long-term opioid use often starts with using opioid analgesics for acute pain** [S1].

Every statement below is downstream of that one sentence.

## Prescribing

**Quality statement 5 — Appropriate opioid analgesic prescribing** [S1]:

> If an opioid analgesic is considered appropriate for an **opioid-naïve** patient with acute pain, **use an
> immediate-release formulation at the lowest appropriate dose, for a limited duration**, and prescribe in line
> with best practice guidelines... **The patient is supported to cease any opioid analgesic use as their
> function and pain improve.**

- **Consider Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme requirements for drugs of addiction, state and territory
  regulations, and best practice guidelines when prescribing** [S1].
- **Consider individual characteristics** — **age, weight, hepatic and renal function, allergies, and other
  health conditions such as obstructive sleep apnoea**; the patient's **opioid status** and other medicines [S1].
- > **Use paracetamol and anti-inflammatories to reduce the dose of opioid analgesic for acute pain** [S1].
- **Consider strategies to minimise overall opioid analgesic use** [S1].
- **Consider whether the patient has a life-limiting illness and whether they are in the care of a palliative
  care team** [S1].

## ⚠️ Weaning — and the order of withdrawal

> **Opioid analgesic dose reduction should start as soon as possible, and can usually start one to two days
> after major surgery or trauma** [S1].

> **In general, opioid analgesics should be discontinued before paracetamol and non-steroidal
> anti-inflammatories are discontinued** [S1].

That inverts ordinary practice, where the simple analgesics are stopped first and the opioid is what remains.
Here the opioid goes first and the co-analgesia covers the gap.

**Define a weaning and cessation plan guided by** the patient's **functional activity and pain scores**, **the
amount of opioid analgesic used in each 24-hour period**, and **the duration of therapy** [S1].

> **If discontinuing opioid analgesics that were prescribed for a short duration and used for less than 10
> days, doses can be reduced quickly. This also applies when discontinuing immediate-release opioids prescribed
> for acute pain in patients who are also on long-term opioid analgesic therapy** [S1].

**Discuss, and agree to, the weaning and cessation plan with the patient** [S1].

## Assessment before prescribing

**Quality statement 2 — Acute pain assessment** [S1]:

> Analgesic prescribing for a patient with acute pain is guided by its **expected severity** and assessment of
> **patient-reported pain intensity** and **the impact of pain on the patient's function**.

> **Assess the patient's functional activity using an evidence-based assessment tool before prescribing an
> opioid analgesic. The results of the functional assessment should be considered together with the patient's
> pain scores in discussion with the patient to guide appropriate treatment. The outcome of the assessments
> should be documented in the patient's medical record** [S1].

**Function is assessed before the first dose, not after it** — and it is the pairing with the pain score, not
the pain score alone, that guides treatment.

**Quality statement 1 — Patient information and shared decision making** [S1]:

> The **non-pharmacological and pharmacological options** for managing acute pain are discussed with a patient
> and their carer **in a way that they can understand**, and that leads to **a shared understanding of the
> decision to use an opioid analgesic or other treatment(s)**.

**Discuss the patient's expected recovery, and the potential benefits and harms of acute pain treatment
options.** **Tailor the options in line with a stepwise approach — which may or may not result in prescribing
opioid analgesics** [S1].

## ⚠️ Risk of harm

**Quality statement 3 — Risk-benefit analysis** [S1]:

> Whenever an opioid analgesic is considered, **their risk of opioid-related harm is assessed. An opioid
> analgesic may be prescribed when other analgesics are not clinically feasible or sufficient, and the
> potential benefits outweigh the potential harms.**

**Identify and document avoidable, modifiable risks of harm, using appropriate assessment tools where
possible** [S1].

**Groups at increased risk of opioid analgesic-related harm** [S1]: **older people; infants and children;
pregnant and breastfeeding patients; patients with a disability; patients with unstable adverse social
circumstances; patients with psychological comorbidities; and patients with substance use disorders.**

**Risk factors for respiratory depression** [S1]: **older age**; **chronic opioid analgesic use; chronic
sleep-disordered breathing; chronic obstructive pulmonary disease; diabetes; hypertension; hepatic or renal
impairment; neurological diseases; and obesity**.

### Modifiable, avoidable risk factors for sedation and respiratory depression [S1]

- **use of more than one opioid analgesic at a time**
- **use of modified-release oral and transdermal opioid analgesics**
- **use of continuous opioid analgesic infusions**
- **continued administration of opioid analgesics to treat pain that is not responding to opioid analgesics**
- **co-administration of CNS depressants such as benzodiazepines and other sedative hypnotics, barbiturates,
  gabapentinoids, alcohol and recreational drugs**

That fourth item is the quietest and the most common: **continuing an opioid for pain the opioid is not
treating.**

> **CNS depressants such as benzodiazepines or other sedative hypnotics, barbiturates, and gabapentinoids
> increase the risk of excess sedation. These medicines should be avoided and patients taking these medicines
> should be subject to increased monitoring** [S1].

**Establish the patient's opioid status and existing opioid analgesics in their possession** [S1].

## Adverse effects

**Quality statement 6** [S1]: adverse effects are **monitored and managed**, and **the patient and carer are
made aware of potential adverse effects and signs of overdose, including respiratory depression**.

**Monitoring frequency is influenced by the hospital setting, pain severity, age and comorbidities, and the
dose and route of administration** [S1]. **Develop management strategies including adjustments to the dose,
route of administration, formulation and type of opioid analgesic** [S1].

> **Prescribe appropriate treatments to prevent and manage opioid analgesic–induced adverse effects: laxatives
> to prevent or treat constipation, and appropriate treatments for nausea and vomiting, and itchiness** [S1].

**Prevent**, not only treat — the laxative is part of the opioid prescription.

## Pathways, documentation, review and discharge

**Quality statement 4 — Pathways of care** [S1]: a patient at increased risk **is appropriately managed in
conjunction with a locally approved pathway**. **Manage and refer according to a defined clinical pathway for
appropriate support services**, including **escalation to specialist services for paediatrics, pain
management, drug and alcohol, clinical pharmacy and allied health**. **Inform the patient why they are being
referred.**

**Quality statement 7 — Documentation** [S1]:

> When a patient is prescribed, supplied or administered an opioid analgesic, **the intended duration of
> therapy, and the review and referral plan are documented in the patient's healthcare record. The cause of
> the pain for which the opioid analgesic is prescribed is documented, including on the inpatient
> prescription.**

**Document the indication, intended duration (number of days), the review and referral plan, and the weaning
and cessation plan** [S1]. **Duration in days, written down** — the prescription carries its own end date.

**Quality statement 8 — Review of therapy** [S1]: during hospital care a patient **is assessed regularly to
determine their response to therapy and whether an opioid analgesic is effective and appropriate for their
stage of care.**

> **If the opioid analgesic is continued, decisions about the appropriate daily dose should be based on the
> oral morphine-equivalent daily dose (oMEDD) given in the past 24 hours** [S1].

**Regular assessment of pain and function; due to interpatient variability, the timing of regular assessments
should be individualised** [S1].

**Quality statement 9 — Transfer of care** [S1]:

> **Planning for appropriate analgesic use at the transfer of care begins when a patient is started on an
> opioid analgesic during their hospital visit**, according to an agreed weaning and cessation protocol.
> **The number of days' supply of an opioid analgesic on discharge is based on multiple factors, including the
> expected course of the patient's condition, appropriate arrangements for follow-up and opioid analgesic use
> in the last 24 hours before discharge.**

**Discharge quantity is calculated, not defaulted** — from the last 24 hours of actual use, the expected
course, and whether follow-up is arranged. And the planning starts at the **first** dose, not at discharge.

## Where this connects in the compendium

**The low back pain standard** requires that opioids for low back pain be limited to **immediate-release
formulations for a limited duration** — this standard supplies the reasoning, the weaning schedule and the
monitoring.

**The osteoarthritis guideline** makes both oral and transdermal opioids a **strong recommendation against**.

And the convergence worth noting: **low back pain avoids benzodiazepines and gabapentinoids as ineffective**;
this standard names **co-administration of exactly those drugs** as a **modifiable, avoidable risk factor for
respiratory depression**. Two Australian standards, the same medicines, two independent reasons not to use
them.
