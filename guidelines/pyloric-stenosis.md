# Pyloric stenosis

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** recognition, investigation, pre-operative fluid and electrolyte correction and transfer of infants with pyloric stenosis. No adult counterpart; `vomiting` (children) lists it as the cause of projectile vomiting, and fluid volumes are in `dehydration-children`.

> ✅ **OPEN AND QUOTED.** 17 of 17 claims quoted verbatim from the **RCH Melbourne Clinical Practice Guideline *Pyloric stenosis*** (Last updated March 2024); **20 fragments re-checked by machine. 3 quoted doses.**

> ⚠️ **Correct dehydration, electrolytes and acid-base first — surgery waits, especially for a normal bicarbonate** (post-operative apnoea risk). Transfer confirmed or strongly suspected cases to a paediatric surgical centre early.

---

## Recognise it

- **Hypertrophy of the pyloric muscle causes gastric outlet obstruction.** It usually presents at **2–6 weeks** with **progressive non-bilious vomiting**, is unlikely after 12 weeks, and definitive treatment is **pyloromyotomy**. [S1]
- **Risk factors:** male · first born · parental (especially maternal) history · preterm birth · younger maternal age · **postnatal macrolide exposure** · formula feeding · maternal smoking in pregnancy. [S1]
- **Vomiting is recurrent and increasingly forceful**, may be projectile, often shortly after feeds; the infant may seem hungry afterwards. Non-bilious; blood-stained in under 10%. [S1]
- **Pyloric mass:** olive-shaped, right upper quadrant at the lateral edge of rectus abdominis, **best felt from the left** with the infant settled and supine; may need repeated or prolonged examination. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Early presentations lack the classic signs.** Visible peristalsis and a palpable mass are often absent and **are not required to consider the diagnosis.** [S1]
- **Assess the degree of dehydration, weigh, and plot against previous weights.** [S1]
- **Differentials:** UTI · gastroenteritis · GOR · surgical causes such as volvulus or malrotation · congenital adrenal hyperplasia. [S1]

## Investigate

- **Capillary or venous gas for electrolytes and glucose.** Prolonged vomiting gives a **hypochloraemic hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis**, proportionate to symptom duration. [S1]
- **Abdominal ultrasound is 95% sensitive.** ⚠️ **Repeat it if suspicion persists** after an equivocal first scan. [S1]

## Treat

- **Gain IV access, correct fluids, electrolytes and acid-base, stop oral feeds**; pass a nasogastric tube if profuse vomiting continues despite stopping feeds. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Delay surgery until dehydration, electrolytes and acid-base are corrected — above all, until serum bicarbonate is normal**, because metabolic alkalosis risks post-operative hypoventilation/apnoea. [S1]
- **Resuscitation fluid: 0.9% sodium chloride**, only as required — not every infant needs it. [S1]
- **Deficit: 0.9% sodium chloride + 5% glucose** (volumes per the dehydration and IV fluid guidelines). ⚠️ **Add potassium once urine output is adequate (1–2 mL/kg/hr).** [S1]
- **Maintenance: 0.9% sodium chloride + 5% glucose.** [S1]

## Escalate and discharge

- **Involve the paediatric team whenever pyloric stenosis is suspected.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Confirmed or strongly suspected: transfer early to a paediatric surgical centre**, once an initial management plan is in place. [S1]
- **Discharge when** ultrasound is not consistent with pyloric stenosis, differentials have been considered, the infant is well and tolerating feeds, and follow-up is planned. [S1]
