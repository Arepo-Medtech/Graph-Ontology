# Hypokalaemia in children

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** severity grading, investigation, and oral and IV potassium replacement, administration limits and escalation for hypokalaemia in children. The adult counterpart is `potassium-disturbances` (AMH, which gives no doses). Related RCH pages: `hyperkalaemia-children`, `intravenous-fluids-children` (potassium in maintenance fluids), `diabetic-ketoacidosis-children`, `hypomagnesaemia-children`.

> ⚠️ **IV potassium is a high-risk medication:** rapid IV administration or overdose may cause fatal arrhythmia or arrest. **Oral/enteral is preferred.** Cardiac monitoring if K under 3.0 mmol/L or on IV replacement.

---

## Key points and severity

- **The oral/enteral route is preferred.** ⚠️ **IV potassium is high-risk** — inadvertent hyperkalaemia, fluid overload, extravasation and thrombophlebitis; rapid IV administration or overdose may cause arrhythmia or arrest. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Monitor fluid status and electrolytes in every child on replacement. Cardiac monitoring if initial K is under 3.0 mmol/L or the child is on IV replacement.** Seek specialist advice for critical (K under 2.0 mmol/L) or symptomatic hypokalaemia. [S1]
- **Hypokalaemia = plasma potassium under 3.5 mmol/L.** **Severity (mmol/L):** mild 3.0–3.4 · moderate 2.5–3.0 · severe 2.0–2.4 · **critical under 2.0.** [S1]
- **A 1 mmol/L fall in serum K represents a whole-body deficit of at least 200 mmol** (normal acid–base). Metabolic acidosis may falsely raise serum K and hide the deficit; alkalosis does the opposite. [S1]
- **Hypokalaemia commonly comes with hypomagnesaemia — measure magnesium and replace as required.** [S1]
- **Prevention:** children eating a variety of foods meet their needs (about 2–4 mmol/kg/day); consider potassium-containing maintenance fluids for children nil by mouth for prolonged periods. [S1]

## Assess and investigate

- **Often asymptomatic;** symptoms are more likely with a rapid fall below 3 mmol/L — weakness or paralysis, ileus, constipation, lethargy, confusion. [S1]
- **Repeat K to verify the first result;** haemolysed or finger-prick samples can falsely raise K, so **take a venous sample** if hypokalaemia is suspected. Also UEC, BGL, venous gas, and magnesium (especially if refractory). [S1]
- **ECG if symptomatic, at risk of arrhythmia, or K under 3 mmol/L.** Look for reduced T-wave amplitude (usually earliest), T flattening/inversion, ST depression, U waves, QT prolongation, ectopics. [S1]

## When to replace

- ⚠️ **Make sure the child is passing urine before starting potassium.** **Replace if K under 3.0 mmol/L, or under 3.5 mmol/L with symptoms, signs or ECG changes.** [S1]
- **Mild (3.0–3.5 mmol/L): depends on context** — no acute treatment with monitoring, a higher maintenance dose, or oral/IV replacement if ongoing losses or poor intake. **Stable, no ECG change: correct gradually over 24–48 hours.** [S1]
- **Consider IV** if the child cannot tolerate enteral medication, the initial K is under 2.5 mmol/L, or there are ECG changes. Oral potassium is best taken with or soon after food. [S1]

## Oral / enteral dosing

- **Acute oral dose: 1–2 mmol/kg/dose (max 20 mmol per dose).** Repeat after checking K, **to max 5 mmol/kg/day (max 50 mmol/day).** [S1]
- **Maintenance, if required: 2–5 mmol/kg/day orally in divided doses (max 20 mmol per dose).** [S1]
- **Products:** KCl oral mixture 1.33 mmol/mL (rise after about 2 hours) · effervescent tablet (e.g. Chlorvescent) 14 mmol, dissolve completely (about 2 hours) · controlled-release Slow K 8 mmol, for mild or chronic hypokalaemia, swallow whole (about 4 hours). [S1]

## IV dosing and administration

- ⚠️ **Use an infusion pump with dose-error-reduction software where available; concentrated potassium only in critical care.** **Count every potassium source** (maintenance additives, PN, enteral supplements); other potassium-containing fluids may need pausing. [S1]
- **General ward: 0.2 mmol/kg/hour for 3 hours (max 10 mmol/hour)** — likely needs a fluid rate above maintenance. ECG monitoring only if K under 3 mmol/L or arrhythmia risk. **Repeat K 1 hour after completion**, and before any further dose. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Critical care: 0.4 mmol/kg/hour for 1–2 hours (max 20 mmol/hour)** — likely concentrated infusion and central line; **ECG monitoring required; repeat K 1 hour after starting and 1 hour after completion.** [S1]
- **IV maintenance, if required: 1–4 mmol/kg/day (max 10 mmol/hour).** Discuss doses above 4 mmol/kg/day with a senior clinician or retrieval service. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Give potassium chloride IV by infusion only. Never flush after a potassium chloride infusion.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Peripheral line: usual max 40 mmol/L** (discuss 40–60 mmol/L with a senior clinician). Use premixed bags; if adding KCl, invert at least 10 times; label everything to prevent flushing. The premixed **10 mmol KCl in 100 mL 0.29% sodium chloride** may run peripherally because it is isotonic. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Over 60 mmol/L: central line only, max 0.4 mmol/kg/hour**, in areas with a clear protocol, senior approval and ECG monitoring. [S1]
- **Monitor** vital signs, fluid status and urine output, signs of hyperkalaemia, and the IV site for phlebitis or extravasation. [S1]

## Escalation and discharge

- ⚠️ **Consider transfer:** K under 2.0 mmol/L · symptomatic (especially paralysis) · significant ECG changes · renal impairment including oliguria · arrhythmia risk · fluid overload · neonates · complex medical conditions. [S1]
- **Discharge when** asymptomatic, **K over 3.0 mmol/L and stable**, losses replaced with ongoing replacement possible, and repeat levels are planned. [S1]
