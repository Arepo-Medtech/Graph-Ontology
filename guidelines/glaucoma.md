## ⚠️ The frame: it is not a pressure disease

> **Glaucoma is best understood as a progressive optic neuropathy in which intraocular pressure (IOP) is a
> modifiable risk factor, not the disease itself** [S1].
>
> **Although increased intraocular pressure is not a defining characteristic of glaucoma, IOP is its main
> modifiable risk factor. Treatments that lower IOP reduce the rate of glaucoma progression (whether or not
> IOP is increased)** [A1].

⚠️ **Think progression, not pressure: IOP is a surrogate marker** [S1]. **Normal-tension glaucoma** exists —
**glaucomatous disc and field change with normal IOP** [S1].

**Glaucoma is the second leading cause of irreversible blindness in Australia in people aged over 55 years,
with over 50–60% of cases in Australia remaining undiagnosed** [S1]. ⚠️ **$4.3 billion in 2025, up from
$1.9 billion in 2005** [S1].

## ⚠️ What general practice is and is not for

> **Progression in glaucoma is confirmed with formal visual field testing and optical coherence tomography,
> both of which are outside the scope of general practice** [S1].
>
> ⚠️ **This underscores the key role of general practitioners in care coordination rather than direct
> diagnosis or monitoring** [S1].

⚠️ **Tonometry is not routinely available in primary care and is not required to guide referral
decisions** [S1]. **GPs can work collaboratively with community-based optometrists who can measure IOP when
this is not available to the GP** [S1].

**What the GP does** [S1]:

- **Coordinate and manage** — **ensure continuity of prescribed topical therapy, address systemic
  comorbidities and arrange timely review**
- ⚠️ **Refer rather than defer**
- **Shared records** — **copy referral correspondence to both optometrist and ophthalmologist**

⚠️ **Shared-care pathways should always retain ophthalmologist oversight, particularly for patients with
progression, advanced disease or high-risk profiles. Optometry-performed VF testing serves a screening
function but does not replace specialist evaluation** [S1].

## Classification

| Group | Subtype | Typical presentation [S1] | Urgency [S1] |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Angle-closure** | ⚠️ **Acute angle closure (AAC)** | **painful red eye, haloes, nausea/vomiting, mid-dilated pupil, IOP >30 mmHg** | ⚠️ **same-day ED/ophthalmology** |
| **Angle-closure** | **Primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG)** | **progressive peripheral field loss, shallow anterior chamber** | **routine ophthalmology** |
| **Open-angle** | **Primary open-angle (POAG)** | **insidious, asymptomatic, cupping ± raised IOP** | **routine ophthalmology** |
| **Open-angle** | **Normal-tension (NTG)** | **glaucomatous disc/field change with normal IOP** | **routine ophthalmology** |
| **Secondary** | **steroid-induced, pseudo-exfoliative, uveitic, pigmentary, traumatic, neovascular** | **context-specific** | **as per scenario** |

### ⚠️ Most angle-closure is not a crisis

> ⚠️ **AMH states that up to 75% of patients with angle-closure glaucoma will have intermittent or chronic
> angle closure with asymptomatic progressive loss of visual field rather than an acute crisis** [A1].

⚠️ **This reframes the topic.** S1's emphasis is on **recognising and acting on angle-closure red flags
immediately** [S1] — correct, and it is the presentation that blinds fastest. But **three-quarters of
angle-closure patients never present that way** [A1]; they appear in the **PACG** row above, as quiet field
loss. ⚠️ **Both statements are held here and neither is presented as correcting the other.**

## Risk stratification [S1]

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Age** | ⚠️ **risk rises approximately 7% for each additional year**; **60–69 years is a three-fold higher risk than 40–49** |
| **Family history** | **a first-degree relative confers an estimated two-and-a-half-fold elevation** |
| ⚠️ **Ancestry** | ⚠️ **incidence is disproportionately elevated among individuals of African and Asian ancestry compared with those of European descent** |
| **Refraction** | **high myopia and marked hypermetropia, for both open-angle and angle-closure** |
| **Comorbidity** | **diabetes mellitus and chronic corticosteroid use**; **ocular trauma** for secondary glaucoma |
| **IOP** | **the single strongest modifiable clinical risk factor, with risk rising for each 1 mmHg increment** |
| **Sex** | **male sex is associated with higher risk of POAG; ACG is more frequent in females** |
| ⚠️ **Access** | ⚠️ **hypertension and barriers to accessing care, such as lower socioeconomic status and limited eye screening, might also contribute** |

**For angle-closure specifically** [A1]: **Asian ethnicity, older age, female gender, family history and
long sight** — and ⚠️ **use of topical or systemic drugs that cause pupil dilation may precipitate an acute
angle-closure crisis**.

⚠️ **Screen for steroid exposure (all routes) and history of ocular trauma** [S1].

## ⚠️ When to refer — quoted thresholds

**Any one is sufficient** [S1]:

| Sign | Likely condition | Action |
|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Severe peri-ocular pain, red eye, haloes, blurred vision, nausea/vomiting PLUS one of steamy cornea, ciliary flush, fixed mid-dilated pupil, IOP ≥30 mmHg** | **acute angle-closure** | ⚠️ **same-day ophthalmology/ED** |
| **IOP ≥35 mmHg (even if asymptomatic) OR IOP 25–34 mmHg with suspicious disc or reproducible VF defect** | **sight-threatening ocular hypertension** | ⚠️ **urgent referral ≤48 h** |
| **Transient brow ache or blurred vision in dim light; haloes around light, nausea/vomiting episodes, shallow anterior chamber on oblique flashlight test** | **intermittent angle closure** | **urgent referral ≤1 week** |
| **Sudden drop in VA or new field loss OR fresh optic-disc (Drance) haemorrhage OR vertical CDR ≥0.8 or asymmetry ≥0.3** | **rapidly progressive open-angle** | **urgent referral ≤1 week** |
| **IOP 22–25 mmHg without other risk factors; high-risk patient (age >60 years, FHx, diabetes, steroid use) even if IOP normal** | **possible early POAG/NTG** | **routine referral 1–3 months** |

⚠️ **Do not delay referral in cases presenting with atypical or ambiguous features** [S1].

**Intermittent (prodromal) angle-closure attacks are specifically distinguished by occurrence in dim
light** and **might be accompanied by headaches or periorbital discomfort** [S1].

⚠️ **Bilateral involvement is rare in an acute case** [S1].

**Document visual acuity and pupil reactions in the referral**, and **include any optometry-recorded
information** [S1].

## Examination in general practice [S1]

- **Visual acuity testing** and **a brief history covering family history of glaucoma, corticosteroid
  exposure, ocular trauma and prior surgery**
- **Direct ophthalmoscopy might allow a gross assessment of the optic disc**, ⚠️ **but accurate estimation
  of the cup-to-disc ratio is often challenging in undilated pupils and should be interpreted cautiously**.
  **Marked asymmetry between eyes or a clearly enlarged cup might still warrant referral**
- **The oblique flashlight test** as **a simple screening tool for a shallow anterior chamber and risk of
  angle closure**
- ⚠️ **Low-cost fundus cameras and smartphone adaptors** — **adjuncts rather than standard GP tools**
- ⚠️ **Artificial intelligence–based disc analysis remains investigational and is not recommended for
  routine clinical use**

## Treatment

**Initial treatment of open-angle glaucoma is generally with topical drugs or selective laser
trabeculoplasty; surgery may be considered in some cases, eg advanced glaucoma** [A1].

**Glaucoma is managed in a stepwise fashion, escalating from drops to selective laser trabeculoplasty
(SLT), minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS) and, in advanced cases, incisional procedures
(trabeculectomy or tube shunt)** [S1].

### Before starting

**Establish a target IOP for each patient using factors such as baseline IOP, glaucoma severity and other
risk factors. The target, which is used to assess treatment efficacy, should be reviewed regularly** [A1].

**Management of secondary glaucomas may also include removal or treatment of the underlying cause, eg
stopping corticosteroids or treating inflammatory eye disease** [A1].

### ⚠️ Drug classes — dosing frequency from AMH's table

| Class | Drugs | Doses/day | Notes [A1] |
|---|---|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Prostaglandin analogues** | **bimatoprost, latanoprost, travoprost** | **1** | ⚠️ **the most effective class**; **increase aqueous humour outflow**; **may cause iris hyperpigmentation and eyelash changes** |
| **Beta-blocker** | **timolol** | **1–2** | **decreases aqueous humour production**; ⚠️ **may cause systemic adverse effects, eg bradycardia, bronchospasm** |
| **Alpha2 agonists** | **apraclonidine, brimonidine** | **2–3** | **increase outflow and decrease production**; ⚠️ **apraclonidine can only be used short term** |
| **Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors** | **brinzolamide, dorzolamide** | **2–3** | **decrease aqueous humour production** |
| **Cholinergic** | **pilocarpine** | **3–4** | ⚠️ **less commonly used**; **high incidence of adverse effects, eg blurred vision, headache** |

⚠️ **These five rows are paraphrased from a licensed source and are in the attestation queue. They give
frequency only — no strength, no volume, no drop count.**

**Prostaglandin analogues are generally first-line due to their higher efficacy, once-daily dosing and
minimal systemic adverse effects** [A1].

**The only oral treatment, acetazolamide, has adverse effects that limit its use to refractory glaucoma or
short-term treatment pre- and post-surgery** [A1].

### Choosing and switching [A1]

- ⚠️ **Consider the irritant effects of preservatives, particularly in patients with ocular surface disease,
  eg dry eye syndrome.** **If possible, use products containing less irritant or no preservatives**
- **If response to a prostaglandin analogue is poor, switching to another one may be effective as drugs in
  this class are structurally different**
- **When a single drug is effective but the target IOP is still not reached, consider adding a second drug**
- **Fixed-dose combinations** have **less dose flexibility** but are **generally preferred as they are
  convenient, aid compliance, reduce exposure to preservatives and reduce cost to the patient**
- **In some cases topical treatment may be started in just one eye, using the other as a control**

**Review treatment 2–8 weeks after any change in medication and regularly thereafter** [A1].

### ⚠️ Adherence is the treatment

> ⚠️ **As glaucoma is usually asymptomatic, compliance is a major problem — only about 30–70% of prescribed
> doses are taken** [A1].
>
> **Check compliance and administration technique BEFORE changing drug treatment** [A1].

⚠️ **The disease has no symptoms until the damage is done, and between a third and two-thirds of doses
never reach the eye.** S1 makes this the GP's central contribution: **support drop adherence — prescribe,
teach technique and monitor side-effects** [S1].

**The supply problem:** **intervals for review with an ophthalmologist might be annual, especially as PBS
prescribing now allows up to 12 months' supply for some glaucoma medications under the 60-day dispensing
model** [S1]. ⚠️ **Renewal of scripts every 6 months is critical for stable annual-review patients** [S1].

### ⚠️ Systemic adverse effects of eye drops

| Class | Systemic effects [S1] |
|---|---|
| ⚠️ **Topical beta-blockers** | ⚠️ **bradycardia, hypotension and bronchospasm, particularly in patients with underlying cardiac or respiratory disease** |
| **Alpha agonists** | **fatigue, dry mouth, drowsiness and delayed allergic conjunctivitis** |
| **Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors** | **paraesthesia, gastrointestinal upset, electrolyte disturbance and metabolic acidosis, especially when oral agents are used** |

⚠️ **Awareness of these effects will allow GPs to identify intolerance, manage comorbidities safely and
prompt timely review with an ophthalmologist** [S1].

### Ocular hypertension

**Although patients with ocular hypertension (increased IOP without glaucoma changes) have an increased
risk of glaucoma, not all will develop glaucoma; monitoring alone is often appropriate; treatment may be
considered for those with additional risk factors** [A1].

## Angle-closure

**Initial treatment is laser iridotomy; if unsuccessful, surgical procedures are considered. Topical
treatment may be used, particularly in those with persistent raised IOP despite laser or surgical
procedures** [A1].

### ⚠️ Acute angle-closure crisis

> ⚠️ **An ophthalmic emergency that requires urgent referral to an ophthalmologist** [A1].

**Treatments used to reduce IOP prior to laser iridotomy may include timolol, a topical alpha2 agonist,
topical carbonic anhydrase inhibitor, pilocarpine, oral or IV acetazolamide or IV mannitol** [A1].

> ⚠️ **S1 states that if AAC is suspected and no contraindication exists, a topical β-blocker (eg timolol)
> and oral acetazolamide might be administered while arranging transfer** [S1].

⚠️ **Neither source gives a dose for either drug in this setting** — see *Unresolved*. **This is the one
place in the guideline where a GP might act pharmacologically before transfer, and the dose is absent.**

## After surgery

**Patients remain under close ophthalmology review, but GPs contribute by reinforcing adherence to
prescribed post-operative drops, reviewing systemic medications that might influence IOP, and recognising
warning symptoms such as ocular pain, redness or blurred vision that require urgent re-referral** [S1].

| ⚠️ **Vertigo** | ⚠️ **the same contraindication, from the other side**: angle-closure glaucoma removes cinnarizine/dimenhydrinate as a vertigo option |

| **Psoriasis / eczema / asthma** | ⚠️ **chronic corticosteroid use by ANY route is a glaucoma risk factor** [S1] and a cause of secondary glaucoma [A1] |
| **Allergic rhinitis** | ⚠️ **intranasal and ocular corticosteroids count as steroid exposure** — S1 says screen **all routes** |
