## Mild

**Will respond to oral glucose or sucrose (cane sugar)** [M1].

> ⚠️ **Give glucose — for example glucose tablets — to people treated with acarbose, because of delayed
> absorption of sucrose** [M1]. **This is the one substitution that matters and it is easily missed.**

---

## Severe, in an adult unable to take oral food or fluids

| Option | Detail [M1] |
|---|---|
| **SC or IM glucagon** | ⚠️ **No dose given** |
| **IV infusion of 15–20 g glucose** | = **150–200 mL of glucose 10%**, or **75–100 mL of glucose 20% injection** |
| **Glucose 50% injection** | ⚠️ **Give by slow IV injection into a secure IV cannula in an antecubital vein, to reduce the risk of superficial thrombophlebitis** |

---

## Severe, in children

**Give glucagon or IV glucose 10% injection; follow local protocol** [M1].

> ⚠️ **Monitor closely — continued glucose boluses may render a young child hyperosmolar, with the risk of
> cerebral oedema** [M1].

---

## What to expect, and what to do next

- **Response should occur within 10 minutes for glucagon and within 4–5 minutes for glucose** [M1].
- ⚠️ **When the person has responded, give longer-acting carbohydrates — for example a sandwich — to
  prevent recurrent hypoglycaemia** [M1].

> ### ⚠️ The long-acting sulfonylurea trap
> **Prolonged treatment with glucose infusion may be required for hypoglycaemia due to a long-acting
> sulfonylurea. Octreotide may be used if hypoglycaemia persists despite glucose infusion** [M1].
> **A person who responds and is discharged may relapse.**
