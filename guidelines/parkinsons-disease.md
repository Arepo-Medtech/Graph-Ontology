## ⚠️ The two things to establish before treating

> **No agent has been proven to slow progression of disease** [A1].

Everything here is **symptomatic relief** [A1]. That reframes the timing decision:

> **The decision to begin drug treatment depends on the patient's situation and expectations and may be
> deferred until the symptoms become physically or socially disabling** [A1].

**And confirm it is Parkinson's disease**: **when tremor is the main symptom, consider other conditions such
as hyperthyroidism or essential tremor**, and **distinguish idiopathic Parkinson's disease from other causes
of parkinsonism, eg other neurodegenerative disorders, drug-induced parkinsonism** [A1].

**Mechanism**: **dopaminergic deficiency and relative cholinergic excess in the brain; drugs act by
increasing dopamine or acting as dopamine agonists or as anticholinergics** [A1].

## First-line: levodopa or a dopamine agonist

> **Evidence suggests no long-term advantage for patients initially treated with a dopamine agonist compared
> with levodopa, suggesting that either is appropriate first-line treatment** [A1].

**Choice is individualised on disease severity, comorbidities, adverse effects and patient preference**, and
in every case **start with a low dose and increase gradually to improve mobility while minimising adverse
effects** [A1].

### Levodopa

**The most effective drug for motor symptoms** — **improves bradykinesia, rigidity and, less consistently,
tremor** [A1]. ⚠️ **First-line for most people, especially the elderly and people with cognitive
impairment** [A1].

**Always given with a peripheral dopa decarboxylase inhibitor (carbidopa or benserazide)** to cut peripheral
metabolism, allowing a lower levodopa dose and **fewer peripheral dopamine adverse effects such as nausea,
vomiting and hypotension** [A1].

⚠️ **Long-term use is associated with increased motor fluctuations (end-of-dose failure and on-off effect),
dyskinesias and dystonias; their development may relate to duration of levodopa treatment and/or reflect
disease progression** [A1] — AMH does not settle which.

**Device-assisted therapy** — **levodopa with carbidopa intestinal gel infusion, or foslevodopa with
foscarbidopa SC infusion** — is for **levodopa-responsive patients with severe disabling motor fluctuations
not adequately controlled by oral therapy** [A1].

### Oral dopamine agonists

**Bromocriptine and cabergoline (ergot derivatives) and pramipexole (not ergot-derived)** [A1]. They
**improve bradykinesia and rigidity, but are less effective than levodopa** [A1].

⚠️ **May cause confusion and hallucinations more often than levodopa, especially in the elderly, people with
dementia, or in high doses** [A1].

**As monotherapy in early disease they delay the onset of motor fluctuations and dyskinesias and may be
preferred to levodopa in younger patients** — ⚠️ **however, their long-term use as monotherapy is limited by
adverse effects including impulse control disorders** [A1].

**Added to levodopa, a dopamine agonist improves motor fluctuations and allows the levodopa dose to be
reduced** [A1].

> **Efficacy of the oral dopamine agonists seems broadly similar, however pramipexole is preferred as it is
> not associated with the rare fibrotic complications of the ergot derivatives. Bromocriptine is rarely
> used** [A1].

**Rotigotine** is a **non-ergot agonist usable as monotherapy or with levodopa**; **as a patch it may suit
patients unwilling or unable to take oral therapy, eg those with swallowing difficulties** [A1].

**Apomorphine** is for **people severely disabled by motor fluctuations refractory to conventional
treatment** and **may allow a reduction in levodopa dosage**. ⚠️ **Start treatment in hospital under
specialist supervision**, by **intermittent SC injection or continuous SC infusion with portable pump**, and
note it **is highly emetogenic and requires pretreatment with domperidone** [A1].

### Adjuncts in later disease

**MAO-B inhibitors — rasagiline, safinamide, selegiline** — are **mainly an adjunct to levodopa in later
disease (levodopa dose may need reducing)**. **Indirect comparisons suggest similar efficacy in reducing
'off' time to entacapone but less efficacy than oral dopamine agonists** [A1]. **As initial monotherapy,
rasagiline (and rarely selegiline) may sometimes be used in mild symptoms — less effective than levodopa and
probably than dopamine agonists, but may be better tolerated** [A1].

**COMT inhibitors — entacapone and opicapone** — are an **adjunct to levodopa for end-of-dose motor
fluctuations**. They **increase 'on' time**, ⚠️ **but also increase levodopa-induced dyskinesias and GI
adverse effects**, and **the levodopa dose may need to be reduced** [A1].

**Amantadine** has **dopaminergic and anticholinergic activity**, is **more effective than anticholinergics
for akinesia and rigidity but less effective for tremor**, and ⚠️ **shows some loss of efficacy after 3–6
months**. **Mainly used in later disease to alleviate drug-induced dyskinesias**; **may be used as
monotherapy for tremor in early disease** [A1].

⚠️ **Anticholinergics — benzatropine and trihexyphenidyl** — are **used infrequently because of adverse
effects and relatively poor efficacy**. **Avoid in the elderly and those with cognitive impairment.** **Modest
effect on tremor, little effect on rigidity and bradykinesia.** ⚠️ **Withdraw slowly to avoid precipitating a
cholinergic crisis** [A1].

**Deep brain stimulation** may benefit **selected patients with inadequately controlled motor fluctuations
and/or dyskinesia despite optimised drug therapy, or drug-refractory tremor — seek specialist advice** [A1].

## Dosing

> ⚠️ **Paraphrased from AMH monographs, no stored quote, flagged `dose: true`. Check before prescribing.**

| Agent | Dose [A1] |
|---|---|
| **Levodopa** (with benserazide or carbidopa) | **dosage is expressed as levodopa**. Conventional products: **oral, initially 50–100 mg 2 or 3 times daily**, adjusted gradually; **up to 2 g daily in 3 or more doses may be needed**. ⚠️ **Nausea and vomiting may occur at the low starting doses because dopa decarboxylase inhibition is insufficient** |
| **Pramipexole** (conventional tablets) | **125 micrograms 3 times daily for a week**, then **250 micrograms 3 times daily for a week**, then **500 micrograms 3 times daily**; if needed **increase weekly by 250 micrograms 3 times daily** to a **maximum 1.5 mg 3 times daily** |
| ⚠️ **Pramipexole in renal impairment** | **CrCl 20–50 mL/min: initially 125 micrograms twice daily, maximum 2.25 mg daily.** **CrCl <20 mL/min: initially 125 micrograms once daily, maximum 1.5 mg daily** |
| **Rasagiline** | **oral 1 mg once daily**; as an adjunct, **the levodopa dose may need reducing if dopaminergic adverse effects such as dyskinesia occur** |
| **Entacapone** | **oral 200 mg with each levodopa-carbidopa or levodopa-benserazide dose; maximum 2 g daily**. ⚠️ **The daily levodopa dose may need a 10–30% reduction** to reduce dyskinesia |
| **Amantadine** | **≤65 years: oral 100 mg once daily**, increasing after at least a week to **100 mg twice daily**, and if necessary after another week to **100 mg 3 times daily** — ⚠️ **which increases adverse effect risk**. **>65 years: oral 100 mg once daily** |
| **Rotigotine** | early stage, **initially one 2 mg/24 hours patch** |

## ⚠️ Non-motor symptoms, and drug-induced psychosis

**Associated non-motor symptoms**: **autonomic dysfunction (orthostatic hypotension, constipation,
neurogenic bladder disturbances, sexual dysfunction), sleep disorders including REM-sleep behaviour disorder,
depression, anxiety, cognitive decline and eventually dementia, sensory complaints and pain** [A1].

> ⚠️ **Psychosis is rare in untreated disease and appears to be predominantly drug induced** [A1].

**Levodopa seems less likely to cause psychosis than other antiparkinsonian drugs.** The sequence AMH gives
is: **reduce the dose of antiparkinsonian drugs where possible — especially dopamine agonists and
anticholinergics — and consider monotherapy with levodopa**. **If symptoms persist, an antipsychotic may
help: use low-dose quetiapine or clozapine, as they are less likely than other antipsychotics to worsen
parkinsonism** [A1].

**Treatment of dementia in Parkinson's disease with anticholinesterases may be of some benefit** [A1].

## Practice points

⚠️ **For nausea, use domperidone** — **a dopamine antagonist that does not cross the blood–brain barrier and
is not expected to worsen parkinsonism**. **Avoid prochlorperazine and metoclopramide**, which have **central
dopamine antagonist activity** [A1].

⚠️ **Abrupt withdrawal of some antiparkinsonian drugs has been associated with symptoms resembling the
neuroleptic malignant syndrome — fever, muscle rigidity, rhabdomyolysis, profuse sweating. Reduce dose
gradually** [A1].

⚠️ **Dopaminergic drugs, particularly dopamine agonists, commonly cause impulse control disorders; warn
patients and carers and monitor behaviour** [A1].

## Unanswered questions

**There is no evidence-based strategy for the use of additional agents in later Parkinson's disease, as few
trials directly compare the different classes used as adjuncts** [A1].

> ⚠️ **Somnolence and/or episodes of sudden sleep have been reported with all dopamine agonists, levodopa and
> rasagiline. These may be caused by the disease itself and/or exacerbated by dopaminergic drugs. It is not
> yet clear whether they occur more frequently with some drugs or how to detect patients at risk. Monitor
> patients and advise them not to drive or operate machinery if they are affected** [A1].
