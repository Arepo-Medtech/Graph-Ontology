# Cerebral palsy — increased seizures

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** the CP-specific assessment of increased seizures in a child with cerebral palsy — precipitants, medication history, mimics and disposition. **The acute seizure itself is managed as an afebrile seizure: that algorithm and its benzodiazepine doses are in `seizures-acute-management-children` and are not repeated here.** See also `cerebral-palsy-children`.

> ✅ **OPEN AND QUOTED.** 17 of 17 claims quoted verbatim from the **RCH Melbourne Clinical Practice Guideline *Cerebral palsy - increased seizures*** (Last updated July 2023); **23 fragments re-checked by machine. 0 quoted doses.**

> ⚠️ **Conscious state is hard to judge in these children — listen to the parents.** Failure to recover after a seizure needs re-evaluation and senior advice; think of non-convulsive status and intrathecal baclofen withdrawal.

## Key points

- **Conscious state and responsiveness can be difficult to assess. Pay attention to parental concern.** [S1]
- **Manage the acute seizure as an afebrile seizure**, assessing and treating at the same time if the child is still seizing. [S1]
- **Seizure disorders affect up to half of children with CP**, are linked with worse motor function, and may be hard to control. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Stopping intrathecal baclofen causes withdrawal** — dystonia and dyskinesia that can resemble status epilepticus. [S1]

## Recognise

- **Consider seizures in:** changed behaviour · deteriorating motor function · sudden falls · cognitive decline · sleep disturbance. [S1]
- **Precipitants:** intercurrent illness · changed or missed anti-epileptic doses · CNS infection · VP shunt malfunction. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Missing any AED can cause breakthrough seizures; abrupt withdrawal of carbamazepine or oxcarbazepine causes more severe seizures.** [S1]

## History and examination

- **Take a medication history focused on AEDs, benzodiazepines and baclofen**, and the child's seizure types and emergency plan. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Count benzodiazepines already given — cumulative dosing and tolerance** — and include the pre-hospital seizure time. [S1]
- **Vomiting, diarrhoea or feed intolerance reduces AED absorption. Ask about drugs that lower the seizure threshold** — eg tramadol, tricyclics, antihistamines. [S1]
- **Ask about a VP shunt or intrathecal baclofen pump**, examine the shunt, and look for subtle ongoing seizure signs such as eye deviation. [S1]

## Manage

- **Airway:** optimise head position — **left lateral if aspiration risk**; head-tilt chin-lift or jaw thrust; oro/nasopharyngeal airway for obstruction; suction. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Conscious state not improving, or worsening, after a seizure: re-evaluate, consider LP and neuroimaging, and seek senior advice.** [S1]
- ⚠️ **Consider non-convulsive status**, especially with multiple seizure types; it may need EEG. Seek senior advice. [S1]
- **Maintenance AED changes go through the responsible consultant.** Treat any intercurrent illness. [S1]

## Disposition

- **Involve the paediatric team** for prolonged seizures, incomplete recovery or status, or rising seizure frequency. **Consider transfer** for status epilepticus or anticipated ICU-level care. [S1]
- **Discharge when** back to usual consciousness and function, tolerating AEDs, **with an updated emergency seizure management plan** and follow-up in place. [S1]
