# Sepsis

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-21 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** all ages — neonates, children, adults, pregnant patients. Community, primary care and hospital.

> **Australian primary source.** Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care (ACSQHC).
> ***Sepsis Clinical Care Standard*** [S1]. **Seven quality statements.**

**This closes the gap named in the antimicrobial stewardship guideline of this compendium**, whose first
quality statement turns on suspected sepsis. The relationship is explicit in the other direction too:

> **Antimicrobial therapy is managed in line with the Antimicrobial Stewardship Clinical Care Standard,
> including a review within 48 hours from the first dose** *(Quality statement 3)* [S1].

## ⚠️ The first quality statement is a question

> **Quality statement 1 — *Could it be sepsis?*** [S1]

Forty-two guidelines in, this is the only quality statement titled as a **question a clinician asks
themselves**. The content explains why: sepsis is missed because it is not considered.

> **A diagnosis of sepsis is considered in any patient with an acute illness or clinical deterioration that
> may be due to infection. A clinical support tool that includes assessment of vital signs and lactate is
> used to help recognise sepsis early and escalate care when required** [S1].

> **Recognising a patient with sepsis can be challenging. Consider sepsis in all patients with acute illness
> or deterioration who may have an infection** [S1].

## ⚠️ It looks different at the two ends of life

> **The presentation of sepsis in neonates and children may differ to adults. In children, hypotension is not
> necessary to diagnose septic shock** [S1].

> **In older people, commonly recognised sepsis signs and symptoms are often absent. Older people are more
> likely to have relative immunosuppression. The presentation is less likely to include a fever, raised white
> cell count or raised C-reactive protein. In older people, hypothermia, delirium and falls (in frail people)
> are more likely** [S1].

**The three markers a clinician reaches for — fever, white cells, CRP — are the three the standard says may
be absent in exactly the population most likely to die.** And the substitutes it names are **hypothermia,
delirium and falls**, which lands directly on the **delirium** and **hip fracture** guidelines in this set:
a fall in a frail person and new delirium are both, here, possible presentations of sepsis.

**Clinical support tools are available to help detect sepsis early... although these tools do not replace
clinical judgement** [S1]. **Tools will vary according to the patient age, cultural needs and healthcare
setting — no single tool will apply to everyone** [S1]. **Where a decision support tool is not available — for
example, for neonatal and maternity patients — early assessment and escalation to a clinician with expertise
in recognising and managing sepsis are the priority** [S1].

## Observations

**To determine whether a patient has signs or symptoms of organ dysfunction, take a full set of
observations** [S1]:

- **respiratory rate**
- **oxygen saturation**
- **heart rate**
- **systolic blood pressure**
- **temperature**
- **altered mentation, behaviour change or delirium**
- **poor peripheral perfusion, cool peripheries, delayed capillary refill time or mottled skin**
- **blood lactate concentration**
- **in pregnant women, the vital signs of the fetus measured using cardiotocography (CTG)**

**Where this will not delay urgent care, test venous blood lactate levels. Capillary lactates may be useful in
children** [S1].

> **An increased lactate level may indicate a protective or a maladaptive response to shock and can play an
> important role in screening. Include blood lactate routinely in decision-making for an acutely deteriorating
> patient or for suspected sepsis. Although assessment of lactate levels is not sufficient for the purpose of
> diagnosis, it is a relatively simple** [test] [S1].

**Lactate is the subject of the standard's first indicator** — *proportion of patients with suspected sepsis
who had blood lactate levels taken as part of screening* [S1] — while the text is careful that it **is not
diagnostic on its own**.

## ⚠️ The antimicrobial clock has two settings

**Quality statement 3** [S1]: blood cultures **immediately, ensuring that this does not delay the
administration of appropriate antimicrobial therapy**. And then the timing rule, which is **tiered rather than
a single number**:

| Situation | Timing [S1] |
|---|---|
| **Signs of infection-related organ dysfunction present** | **Start appropriate antimicrobials within 60 minutes** |
| **Shock present** | **As soon as possible** |
| **Adult without shock, uncertainty about the likelihood of an infectious cause** | **Rapidly investigate alternative diagnoses.** If concern for infection persists after appropriate evaluation, **start antimicrobial therapy as soon as possible, no later than three hours from initial clinical review** |

That middle path is the clinically hard one, and the standard writes it explicitly: **where the diagnosis is
genuinely uncertain and the patient is not shocked, look for something else first — but the clock still runs,
and it stops at three hours.**

> **Do not wait for blood test results if someone is clearly unwell. Urgent antimicrobial therapy should start
> as soon as possible. Do not withhold antimicrobials while awaiting the results of microbiological (for
> example, blood, urine, pus, sputum microscopy or culture) or other tests** [S1].

**Cultures** [S1]: **for adults, two sets of blood cultures; for children, one set.** **Other relevant
microbiological cultures should also be collected, ideally before administering antimicrobials.**

> **In the community setting, if this is outside the scope of clinical practice, refer the patient to hospital
> immediately** [S1].

**Escalate care to a clinician with experience in recognising and managing sepsis, if required**, and **closely
monitor all patients who may have sepsis**, escalating on **evolving or worsening signs of organ
dysfunction** [S1].

### Two prescribing facts that are easy to miss

> **As patients with sepsis often require higher doses of antimicrobials, the patient's other medicines should
> be reviewed by an experienced clinician, preferably a pharmacist, to avoid interactions and enable the safe
> and appropriate administration of antimicrobial therapy** [S1].

> **When prescribing antimicrobials for Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander peoples, consider the higher
> prevalence of multidrug-resistant organisms. Refer to the Central Australian Rural Practitioners Association
> guidelines, or other guidelines based on local resistance data, where appropriate** [S1].

**Arrange consultation with an infectious diseases physician or clinical microbiologist if there is
uncertainty about the appropriate antimicrobial therapy or local sepsis guidelines — ideally when initiating
therapy, or else on review** [S1].

## Time-critical management

**Quality statement 2** [S1]:

> **Sepsis is a time-critical medical emergency.** Assessment and treatment are **started urgently according to
> a locally approved clinical pathway**, and the response to treatment is **monitored and reviewed**. The
> patient is **reviewed by a clinician experienced in recognising and managing sepsis**, and is **escalated to
> a higher level of care when required**.

> **Any clinician can activate the sepsis clinical pathway at an early stage. The principles of recognise,
> resuscitate, refer and review should guide care** [S1].

**Recognise the signs of clinically significant organ dysfunction and, if sepsis is suspected, escalate
immediately and activate the rapid response system** [S1].

> **In smaller hospitals that do not always have doctors on site, follow relevant local procedures related to
> care of the deteriorating patient for the healthcare setting. On-call clinicians will need to be called into
> the hospital** [S1].

### Box 1 — essential elements of a sepsis clinical pathway [S1]

- **Criteria to support clinical decision-making to enable recognition of sepsis**, including **a clinical
  decision support tool with parameter thresholds for vital signs and blood lactate measurement**, and
  **guidance on recognising clinically significant organ dysfunction that warrants starting time-sensitive
  interventions — such as fluid resuscitation, administration of appropriate antimicrobials, and timely
  surgical source control when required**
- **Triggers and timeframes for escalation of care**, including **methods to communicate with a clinician
  experienced in sepsis**; **processes to enable escalation 24 hours a day, 7 days a week**; **escalation to
  higher levels of care**; **the ability for emergency transfer of patients to or from other health
  services**; and **a way for the appropriate investigations and treatments to start before transfer**
- **Guidance on the availability of appropriate interventions and timing of their use**, covering **fluids and
  other time-critical treatments, blood cultures, antimicrobial therapy, and source control**
- **Timeframes for clinical review**, including monitoring, review of investigation results, the patient's
  response to treatment and **the antimicrobial plan**
- **Ways to consider the patient's age, cultural needs, goals of care and advance care plans in
  decision-making**
- **Consideration of alternate diagnoses**

**Pathways are designed to assist clinical judgement using the best available clinical evidence** [S1] — and
the last element is a safeguard against the pathway itself: **keep considering what else this could be.**

## Coordination

**Quality statement 4** [S1]: **sepsis is a complex, multisystem disease requiring a multidisciplinary
approach**, with treatment in hospital **coordinated by a clinician with expertise in managing patients with
sepsis**.

**The patient and family are at the centre of the multidisciplinary team**; the coordinating clinician should
**ensure that the patient, carer or family participate in decision-making whenever possible, and that the
patient voice is being respected, heard and responded to**, and should **attend regular multidisciplinary
meetings** [S1].

## Information

**Quality statement 5** [S1]: the patient, family or carer **is informed about sepsis from the time that it is
suspected**, in a way they can understand — **verbally and in writing**.

**Provide verbal information early; written information while in hospital, appropriate to the stage of
care** [S1], covering:

- **their suspected or probable sepsis diagnosis**
- **treatment options and the benefits and risks associated with them, including the rationale behind the
  treatment**
- **the signs of deterioration and what patients should do if they deteriorate**
- **systems to escalate care when the patient, their family or carer is concerned**
- **what they can do to best support their health after discharge**

**Information begins when sepsis is *suspected*, not when it is confirmed** — and it includes telling the
patient and family **how to escalate care themselves**.

## Handover

**Quality statement 6** [S1]: a **documented clinical handover at transitions of care**, including **the
provisional sepsis diagnosis, comorbidities, and the management plan for medicines and medical conditions**,
provided to the patient, family and carer as appropriate.

**Where the transition involves transfer to a higher level of care — such as from primary care or a smaller
hospital to a larger hospital — the initial plan may be limited to relevant history, examinations, initial
management and reason for transfer** [S1]. **The documentation should identify the patient's carer where
relevant** [S1].

A deliberately **lower bar for the transfer document**, so that producing it never delays the transfer.

## ⚠️ Survivorship — and bereavement

**Quality statement 7** [S1]:

> A patient who has survived sepsis receives **individualised follow-up care to optimise functional outcomes,
> minimise recurrence, reduce rehospitalisation and manage the ongoing health effects of sepsis**. This
> requires **structured, holistic and coordinated post-discharge care and education** involving the patient,
> family, carer, GP and other clinicians. **Support and information are provided to the family or carer of a
> patient who has died from sepsis.**

**A quality statement that includes the bereaved.** In forty-two guidelines, this is the only statement whose
scope extends to the family of a patient the care did not save.

**When discharging, communicate the ongoing care plan to the patient's GP. Ensure the patient is aware of any
follow-up appointments required and communicate these to the GP as part of their discharge
documentation** [S1].
