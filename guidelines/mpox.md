# Mpox

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-21 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** adults, primary care.

> **Australian primary source.** ASHM *Australian STI Management Guidelines for use in primary care* —
> *mpox* [S1], modified 30 June 2026.

## The first action is a phone call, not a prescription

> Upon clinical suspicion of mpox, **immediately phone the local public health unit** for advice on
> exclusions and restrictions during the infectious period **before the patient leaves the clinic** [S1].

The only auditable outcome for the topic is that outcome:

> **All cases of mpox are immediately discussed with the local Public Health Unit** [S1].

Twenty guidelines in, this is the first whose single quality measure is a **phone call** rather than a test,
a drug or a follow-up. It is also the only one that specifies *before the patient leaves the clinic* —
because the decisions being made are about isolation, and they cannot be made after the patient has gone.

Patients needing admission should be **discussed with the receiving infectious diseases team before
transport**, so biocontainment can be arranged [S1].

## ⚠️ A treatment whose trials failed, still stocked

> **Tecovirimat was made available through the National Medical Stockpile based on in vitro data, however
> two subsequent randomised controlled trials did not demonstrate clinical benefit.** Nonetheless people at
> significantly increased risk of severe disease should be discussed with an expert, as treatment may still
> be advised for some people [S1].

This is the clearest evidence reversal stated in any of the twenty guidelines. The source does not quietly
drop the drug and it does not pretend the trials went the other way — it records the failure, keeps the
pathway open for the highest-risk patients, and moves the decision to a specialist. Adverse effects are
given as headache, nausea, abdominal pain and vomiting, and it **otherwise appears safe** [S1].

**Vaccinia immunoglobulin (VIG)** is held in the National Medical Stockpile **in limited quantities** [S1].
In pregnancy, **VIG may be preferred over tecovirimat** [S1].

## Treatment

| Situation | Recommended [S1] |
|---|---|
| **Uncomplicated infection** | **Supportive treatment** — analgesia, **antibiotics for secondary cellulitis**, and **stool softeners for anal lesions** |
| **Severe infection, or at risk of severe infection (e.g. CD4 < 200 cells/μL), or with complications (e.g. eye infection, severe secondary cellulitis)** | **Discuss with an infectious diseases or sexual health specialist** whether antiviral and/or immunoglobulin treatment is indicated |

**Most cases are mild and self-limited and do not require specific antiviral therapy** [S1].

Urgent specialist discussion is indicated for [S1]:

1. **Severe disease** — sepsis, encephalitis, eye infection;
2. **One or more complications** — secondary cellulitis, dehydration, pneumonia;
3. **Those at risk of severe disease** — immunocompromise such as CD4 < 200 cells/μL, **children, pregnancy,
   breastfeeding**.

Supportive care, spelled out because it is the actual treatment for most people [S1]:

- **Early empiric antibiotics if secondary cellulitis is suspected** — it is the **common** complication.
- **Strong analgesia** for lesions in troublesome areas such as the **oropharynx and anorectum**.
- **Topical lignocaine** for painful lesions.
- **Stool softeners** for anal lesions.
- **Best-practice wound care** to reduce the risk of cellulitis.

**Anorectal pain may require management in hospital** [S1].

## ⚠️ Do not test asymptomatic people

> **Testing is only indicated when patients present with symptoms suspected of being due to mpox virus.
> Asymptomatic screening, or testing asymptomatic contacts for mpox, is not recommended, as asymptomatic
> carriage appears rare** [S1].

The fifth explicit recommendation *against* in the set. The anorectal swab carries the same restriction:
**not recommended for use without symptoms** [S1].

## Diagnosis

| Site/specimen | Test | Note [S1] |
|---|---|---|
| **Lesion fluid** | NAAT of **dry swab** | Ideally collected when a vesicle or pustule is **de-roofed** |
| **Lesion tissue or crust** | NAAT of material in **dry container** | **Best test if no fluid-filled vesicles or pustules are available** |
| **Skin biopsy** | NAAT in dry container | Alternative if no vesicles, pustules or crusts available |
| **Anorectal swab** | NAAT of dry swab | Alternative if the patient has anal symptoms and **cannot tolerate anoscopy**. **Not recommended without symptoms** |

Collection detail that determines whether the test works at all [S1]:

- **Contact your local laboratory before collecting**, to check mpox NAAT availability — or the nearest
  hospital laboratory to arrange transport.
- **Dry swab transport tubes are preferred** (leakage risk, and correct processing). If VTM or liquid Amies
  is used, secure the lid.
- **Bacterial culture swabs containing gel media are not acceptable for NAAT** — a swab that would be
  correct for any other infection in this set destroys this test.
- Where lesions are anatomically or morphologically distinct, **swab at least 2 lesions with separate
  swabs** and **write the lesion site on the container**.
- **PPE: gown, gloves, surgical mask and eye protection.** Avoid contact with contaminated materials such
  as bedding.

**Public laboratories have implemented mpox-specific PCR/NAAT assays; some laboratories may still use
Orthopox assays, and some commercial laboratories do not have a specific assay** [S1] — check before you
collect, not after.

**Always send differentials:** swab suspected mpox lesions for **HSV PCR, syphilis PCR and LGV PCR**;
consider **M/C/S** because secondary bacterial cellulitis is common; and **perform a full HIV/STI screen at
presentation, as co-infection is common** [S1].

## Vaccination

**Mpox is vaccine-preventable** using **JYNNEOS** (a brand name of replication-deficient modified vaccinia
Ankara — Bavarian Nordic, or **MVA-BN**) [S1]. Available from sexual health clinics and some general
practitioners and pharmacies; further advice from the local public health service; vaccine information from
the **Australian Immunisation Handbook** [S1].

> **A standard primary course requires 2 subcutaneous doses, each of 0.5 mL, given 4 weeks apart.
> Subcutaneous injection is the preferred route** [S1].

**Recommended for groups at risk of exposure** [S1]:

- **gay, bisexual or other men who have sex with men (GBMSM), including transgender and gender-diverse
  people**
- **sex workers**, particularly those whose clients are at risk of exposure
- **people with HIV, if at risk of exposure**
- **other individuals whose sexual networks might include GBMSM**
- **laboratory personnel working with orthopoxviruses**
- **may also be considered for healthcare workers** at risk of exposure to patients with mpox
- **post-exposure preventive vaccination** for anyone categorised by public health authorities as a
  **high-risk contact within the past 14 days**

## Contact tracing

**Seek immediate local Public Health Unit assistance** — tracing is not run from the clinic [S1]. Contacts
are stratified **low-, medium- or high-risk** per the CDNA National Guidelines [S1].

> **Medium- and high-risk contacts should be offered post-exposure vaccination with a subcutaneous dose of
> 3rd-generation vaccinia vaccine (MVA), ideally within 4 days of last contact, but up to 14 days.
> Post-exposure vaccination more than 4 days after last contact may not prevent infection, but it may
> attenuate disease severity** [S1].

A second subcutaneous dose follows **at least 28 days** after the first, unless they develop mpox in the
interim [S1].

The 4-day/14-day structure is worth noting: unlike HIV PEP's single 72-hour cliff, this window has a
**preventive** phase and an **attenuating** phase, and the source says so rather than presenting one cutoff.

## Infectious period, and what follows recovery

- **Cases are infectious until scabs have fallen off and a fresh layer of skin has formed** [S1].
- **Cases with anal pain or bleeding are infectious until their anal symptoms have completely resolved** [S1]
  — a separate, symptom-based endpoint.
- **Mpox viral DNA has been found in the semen of men who have recovered.** This raises the possibility of
  transmission via semen for some weeks after clinical infection, **but to date there have been no
  definitive transmissions via semen from someone recently recovered.** For that reason people with a penis
  who have recently recovered **may wish to** use condoms for some time afterwards, to reduce risk to
  partners not vaccinated or previously infected [S1].
- **Pets are at risk of infection**; this should be considered during isolation, and pets **may need to be
  re-housed temporarily** [S1]. The only veterinary consideration in the entire set.

**Laboratory-confirmed cases are considered immune from re-infection, at least in the short to medium term,
and hence do not need immunisation** [S1]. **Test of cure is not necessary** (self-limiting), and
**re-testing is not necessary** [S1].

**Arrange regular clinical reviews by telehealth while the patient is isolating**, depending on severity —
to assess recovery, emergence of complications, and **need for assistance with isolation** [S1].

> **Offer HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis to anyone who has recovered from mpox**, after appropriate
> assessment [S1].

## Epidemiology as the source states it

- **Clade II global outbreak from 2022**, with increased Australian cases in **2022 and 2024**. Within this
  outbreak mpox has been **almost exclusively diagnosed among men who have sex with men**, transmitted
  through sexual contact and other similarly close contact. **Ongoing risk within Australia** [S1].
- **A separate clade I outbreak commenced in 2023 in some African countries. Clade I infections are
  associated with greater disease severity. There have been very few clade I cases detected in Australia,
  and no evidence of sustained transmission to date** [S1].

**Asymptomatic infection is rare** [S1]. Lesions evolve **macule → papule → vesicle → pustule → crusted
scab** and **typically last 3 weeks, but can be longer**; prodromal symptoms **typically last up to 5
days** [S1].
