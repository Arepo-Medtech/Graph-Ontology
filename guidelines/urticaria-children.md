# Urticaria in children

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-23 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** causes, assessment, antihistamine treatment and disposition for acute and chronic urticaria in children. No adult urticaria guideline exists in this compendium; for the systemic reaction see `anaphylaxis-children` and `acute-anaphylaxis`.

> ✅ **OPEN AND QUOTED.** 18 of 18 claims quoted verbatim from the **RCH Melbourne Clinical Practice Guideline *Urticaria*** (Last updated March 2023); **29 fragments re-checked by machine. 3 quoted doses.**

> ⚠️ **Urticaria plus airway, breathing, circulation or gut involvement is anaphylaxis until proven otherwise — give adrenaline.** Isolated urticaria: a non-sedating antihistamine.

---

## Recognise it

- **Most urticaria is idiopathic or viral**; viral infection is a common cause in children, during or after the illness. [S1]
- ⚠️ **Urticaria with airway, respiratory, cardiovascular or gastrointestinal involvement: suspect anaphylaxis — urgent adrenaline and medical assessment.** [S1]
- **Acute: under 6 weeks. Chronic: most days for more than 6 weeks** — usually idiopathic, sometimes inducible or autoimmune. [S1]
- **Suspect allergy when episodes are rare, short-lived and circumstantial**: only with exercise, always within 1–2 hours of a meal, or with abdominal pain, vomiting, breathing difficulty or dizziness. [S1]
- **Allergic urticaria appears within 1–2 hours of exposure and fades within 6–8 hours. Urticaria lasting days is almost never allergy** (except some medication allergies). [S1]
- **Urticaria during an antibiotic course is often the infection, not antibiotic allergy.** Take a detailed drug allergy history; consider drug allergy clinic referral. [S1]
- **Wheals are well circumscribed, often coalescent, usually gone within 24 hours, and migrate** from site to site within minutes to hours. [S1]
- **Angioedema** is larger, painful rather than itchy, and may take up to 72 hours to resolve. [S1]

## Differentials

- **Urticaria: non-target lesions with central pallor. Erythema multiforme: target lesions with central purpura.** [S1]
- **Urticarial vasculitis (including HSP):** painful, lasts over 48 hours, leaves discolouration. [S1]
- **Recurrent angioedema without wheals: think C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency** — urticaria is not a feature of it. [S1]

## Investigate

- **Investigations are almost never indicated for acute urticaria; routine allergy tests are not required** (a common misconception worth discussing). They may be indicated in chronic urticaria with features of an underlying cause. [S1]

## Treat

- **Most acute cases resolve untreated in days to weeks.** Remove triggers, avoid heat and spicy food, **avoid NSAIDs**, use a cool compress. [S1]
- **Non-sedating antihistamine, e.g. cetirizine orally:** 6–11 months 0.25 mg/kg (max 2.5 mg) daily · 1–2 years 2.5 mg twice daily · 2–6 years 5 mg daily or 2.5 mg twice daily · 6–12 years 10 mg daily or 5 mg twice daily · 12–18 years 10 mg daily. [S1]
- **Severe and not responding to antihistamines:** consider a single dose of prednisolone 0.5–1 mg/kg (max 60 mg) orally. **Steroid creams are not effective.** [S1]
- **Chronic urticaria:** start as for acute; if ongoing, **cetirizine can go up to 4 times the recommended daily dose** (6 years and older: max 40 mg total daily), and a sedating antihistamine may be added. Still persisting: specialist advice; immunomodulators only with an allergy specialist. [S1]

## Disposition

- **Discuss with the paediatric team:** fever, bruising, joint pain or systemic features · anaphylaxis · angioedema · age under 6 months · chronic urticaria over 6 weeks. [S1]
- **Discharge once anaphylaxis is excluded — most children do not need admission.** [S1]
