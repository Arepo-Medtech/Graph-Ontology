# Psychotropic medicines in cognitive disability or impairment

**Edition:** 1.0 · 2026-09-21 · **Status:** drafted and source-verified; awaiting clinical attestation
**Scope:** aged care, disability services, general practice and hospital — people with **cognitive disability,
dementia or delirium**.

> **Australian primary source.** Macfarlane S, Trollor JN, Koncz R, Sukkar M, Hullick C. *Supporting the
> appropriate use of psychotropic medicines in aged and disability care.* **Aust Prescr** 2026;49(1),
> **published 10 February 2026**, finalised 4 December 2025 [S1] — an overview of the ACSQHC
> ***Psychotropic Medicines in Cognitive Disability or Impairment Clinical Care Standard*** (2024) [S2].
>
> **The most recently published source in this compendium.** Four of the five authors declare involvement in
> developing the Standard, and one chaired it; conflicts of interest are declared in the source.

**This extends a thread running through four guidelines already here** — dementia (antipsychotics in BPSD),
delirium (a whole quality statement on avoiding them), insomnia (benzodiazepines) and major depressive
disorder (antidepressants). Those describe when to use the drugs. **This one describes what using them
*is*.**

## ⚠️ It is a legal category, not only a clinical one

> **Use of psychotropic medicines in this context is regarded by legislation as chemical restraint, which is a
> type of restrictive practice defined as the use of a medicine or chemical substance for the primary purpose
> of influencing a person's (or care recipient's) behaviour** [S1].

**Five restrictive practices are subject to regulation and oversight** under the NDIS (Restrictive Practices
and Behaviour Support) Rules 2018, the ***Aged Care Act 2024*** and the **Aged Care Rules 2025** [S1]:

**chemical restraint · environmental restraint · mechanical restraint · physical restraint · seclusion.**

**Use of restrictive practices in Australia is also subject to state and territory legislation** [S1].

### What is *not* chemical restraint

The definition has exclusions, and the source is emphatic that they matter [S1]:

> **This definition specifically excludes the use of medicines prescribed for the treatment of a mental or
> physical illness or condition, including end-of-life care. It also excludes medicines used to support access
> to necessary medical appointments or investigations, such as when a benzodiazepine is administered before a
> blood test or imaging procedure. These are important exclusions, as psychotropic medicines are used for a
> broad range of therapeutic purposes, and clearly not all psychotropic use equates to chemical restraint.**

**The trigger is the *primary purpose*, not the drug.** A benzodiazepine before an MRI is not restraint; the
same drug given to settle a resident is.

**The Standard focuses predominantly on three groups — antidepressants, antipsychotics, and
anxiolytics/hypnotics** — but **use of other medicines, e.g. anticonvulsants, for the primary purpose of
influencing behaviour would also be considered chemical restraint** [S1].

## Why the Standard exists

> **Royal Commissions into the aged-care and disability sectors revealed significant concerns about the
> inappropriate use of psychotropic medicines as chemical restraint in people with cognitive disability or
> impairment** [S1].

The **Royal Commission into Aged Care Quality and Safety** and the **Royal Commission into Violence, Abuse,
Neglect and Exploitation of People with Disability** highlighted the extent of inappropriate prescribing,
**especially antipsychotic medicines (e.g. risperidone, quetiapine) and anxiolytic or hypnotic medicines
(mostly benzodiazepines such as diazepam, oxazepam)** [S1].

**In aged care the issue primarily related to overuse for behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia;
in the disability sector, to psychotropic management of behaviours of concern largely in non-progressive
cognitive disability** [S1]. **Concerns had existed for many years, with high rates and considerable variation
in prescribing reported** [S1].

The Standard was developed **in consultation with the NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission and the Aged Care
Quality and Safety Commission** [S1].

## ⚠️ The regulatory gap

> **While regulatory frameworks are legally binding on aged-care and disability *service providers*, they are
> not binding upon individual *prescribers* who provide medical services either within service providers'
> facilities (such as general practitioners and nurse practitioners) or in general practice, other specialist
> clinics and hospitals. Service providers and prescribers may therefore approach behaviour management from
> different perspectives, leading to challenges in coordinating inputs into approval processes** [S1].

> **In practice, this places prescribers in a situation of needing to support service providers with
> authorisation and document the reason for chemical restraint** [S1].

**The facility is bound; the prescriber is not.** This is the clearest statement of a structural accountability
gap anywhere in forty-four guidelines, and it is stated by the people who wrote the Standard.

**Informed consent** [S1]:

> **Medical practitioners need to independently obtain informed consent from either their patient or their
> patient's legally appointed medical decision-maker prior to the use of restricted practice, including the
> prescription of a medicine. Where there is no person who is authorised under relevant jurisdictional
> legislation to provide consent, escalation through the relevant state- or territory-specific authorising
> body is required.**

**Providers have additional requirements** for authorisation, **including ensuring informed consent has been
obtained by the prescriber**, and for **monitoring and reporting the use of chemical restraint** to the Aged
Care Quality and Safety Commission and the NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission respectively [S1].

## The order of operations

> **Best-practice management of behaviours of concern in both aged and disability care is broadly agreed to
> comprise an initial thorough assessment to enable identification and management of any underlying causes of
> behaviour change. This is followed by discussion with the patient, their family and caregivers about the
> issues, and providing them with non-medication management (e.g. behavioural strategies) and problem-solving
> approaches. Environmental approaches and adaptations, in conjunction with a tailored activity program, are
> also important. **Last in the process, if the above approaches are not adequate, is a stepped trial of
> medicines**** [S1].

**Behaviour support plans** are **good practice and a regulatory requirement** in both sectors [S1]:

> **Psychotropic medicines and other types of restrictive practice for behaviours of concern must be a last
> resort following consideration and use of alternative strategies which must be detailed in the behaviour
> support plan** [S1].

A **behaviour support plan** is **a written plan, prepared with the person (and their family or support people
as appropriate), that helps those involved in the person's daily life and care to improve the person's care,
reduce or prevent behaviours of concern, and potentially eliminate the need for restrictive practices to
control behaviour** [S1].

## ⚠️ Not all behaviours respond to the drug

> **It is important to be aware that not all behaviours of concern respond to psychotropic medicines (e.g.
> antipsychotic drugs are usually not effective for reducing calling out, disinhibition, wandering and
> exit-seeking, but they may reduce agitation and aggression)** [S1].

**Four named behaviours that an antipsychotic will not fix** — and they are among the most common reasons one
is requested. Prescribing for them delivers the harms without the effect.

**Appropriate reasons for prescribing** [S1]: **only if the behaviours either pose a substantial risk to the
person or to others, or are significantly reducing the person's quality of life.** **Emergency use is also
justified if such use can facilitate necessary clinical assessments or interventions.**

**How behaviours of concern are defined** [S1]:

> **Behaviours that indicate a risk to the safety or wellbeing of the person who exhibits them, or the safety
> or wellbeing of those around them. Behaviours may challenge the person, their supporters and the care
> services they are in, but may serve a purpose for the person, such as communicating unmet needs or responding
> to their environment. Behaviours that cause concern to others may not be concerning to the person who
> exhibits them.**

That last sentence is the whole problem in one line: **the person distressed by the behaviour may not be the
person receiving the drug.**

## ⚠️ Review, and the drift into permanence

> **Despite fears that behaviours of concern may re-emerge if psychotropics are stopped, many behaviours such
> as agitation and irritability are relatively transient and self-limiting** [S1].

> **It is common for psychotropic medicines to be continued long term (e.g. one Australian study reported
> usage for an average of 2.2 years), despite the risks from longer term use being well known** [S1].

> **Many older people who have had antipsychotics initiated in hospital for short-term management of behaviours
> of concern have them continued upon discharge** [S1].

> **Among people discharged from hospital with a psychotropic medicine for behaviours associated with dementia
> or other cognitive disturbance, very few are provided with a psychotropic management plan. Providing a plan
> for reviewing and stopping the psychotropic medicine is an important step to prevent long-term inappropriate
> use** [S1].

**Agitation and irritability are the behaviours most commonly leading to referral to Dementia Support
Australia, and account for much of the inappropriate prescribing that occurs in people with dementia** [S1] —
and they are exactly the behaviours the source describes as **transient and self-limiting**.

### The review rules

- **Frequency guided by the context of use, the medicine, and clinical need — typically more frequently during
  initiation and dose changes, and whenever there are new behaviours, emerging adverse effects, or changes in
  health or circumstances** [S1].
- **Consider the potential for dose reduction or stopping (deprescribing) at *each* review**, taking into
  account **whether behaviours have stabilised and adverse effects have emerged** [S1].
- > **If behaviours have been settled for 3 months or more at review, the need for ongoing use should be
  > assessed** [S1].
- **Involve the person's family or support network** to facilitate shared decision-making, and **document review
  outcomes clearly — including the rationale for continuing, changing or stopping** [S1].
- > **Stopping a psychotropic medicine should usually be undertaken slowly using an individualised tapering
  > plan to reduce the risk of discontinuation symptoms** [S1].
- > **Ideally, a plan for deprescribing should be made when the medicine is started; as a minimum, the expected
  > duration of therapy should be communicated to the patient and those involved in their care** [S1].

**A deprescribing plan written at initiation** — the same principle the opioid stewardship standard applies to
analgesia, here applied to psychotropics.

**The Australian Deprescribing Network is named as a gateway to local and international deprescribing
resources** [S1].

**Psychotropic polypharmacy is common** in people with cognitive disabilities **and should be rationalised and
minimised wherever possible**; and **people with intellectual and developmental disabilities have varying
abilities to recognise and communicate adverse effects, necessitating proactive and vigilant
monitoring** [S1].

## Harms

**Common harms associated with most psychotropic medicines include, but are not limited to, cognitive
impairment, sedation and increased risk of falls** [S1].

**Adverse effects associated with antipsychotic medicines include extrapyramidal side effects, weight gain,
increased cardiovascular risk, and, in people with dementia, increased risk of cerebrovascular events and
death** [S1].

**The falls line connects to the hip fracture and delirium guidelines here; the cerebrovascular and death line
is the same warning the dementia guideline carries at Moderate evidence.**

## Transitions of care

**Transitions of care are associated with risk of medication errors and adverse events** [S1]. **Aged-care and
disability providers must ensure that up-to-date behavioural data, medication lists, medical histories and
contact details of key people in the person's support network are available at clinical appointments and
transfers to hospital.** **Clinicians, in turn, must clearly communicate their clinical findings and
recommendations to all those involved in the person's care and support, subject to appropriate consent** [S1].

> **Whenever psychotropic medicines are prescribed, clinical communications should include the rationale for
> medication initiation, target symptoms, evidence of effectiveness, potential and observed adverse effects,
> reasons for any medication changes, and recommendations for duration of therapy and ongoing monitoring** [S1].

## The eight quality statements

| # | Quality statement [S2, as reproduced in S1] |
|---|---|
| **1** | **Person-centred care** — care driven by the person's individual preferences, needs and values, upholding their **personal dignity, human rights and legal rights**, with the person supported to be an **active participant** in informed choices |
| **2** | **Informed consent** — the person and their family, support people or nominated decision-maker are informed about **the reason, intended duration, and potential benefits and harms**. If agreed, **informed consent is documented before use**. In an emergency, or without capacity, **processes follow relevant legislation** |
| **3** | **Assessing behaviours** — initially assessed for **immediate risks**, then further assessed to identify **clinical, psychosocial and environmental causes** and the **context**. Assessment by **suitably trained individuals**, considering existing plans and **information from others who know the person well** |
| **4** | **Non-medication strategies** — used **first line and as the mainstay of care**. Individualised, **documented and communicated to all those involved** |
| **5** | **Behaviour support plans** — used to inform and support care; the person's response, **including any use of psychotropic medicines**, is **continually assessed, documented and communicated** to inform updates to the plan and prescribing decisions |
| **6** | **Appropriate reasons for prescribing** — considered **only when there is a significant risk of harm** or the behaviours have a **major impact on quality of life** and a **reasonable trial of non-medication strategies has been ineffective**; or where a **mental health condition has been diagnosed or is reasonably suspected** following documented assessment. **The reason is clearly documented at the time of prescribing** |
| **7** | **Monitoring, reviewing and ceasing** — response **regularly monitored and reviewed**; **benefits, harms and the potential for dose adjustment or cessation considered at each review**; **outcome documented and communicated, along with the timing of the next review** |
| **8** | **Information sharing at transitions of care** — including **behaviour support plans**, and if psychotropics are prescribed, **the reason for use, intended duration, timing of last administration, and plans for monitoring and review** |

**Each quality statement is accompanied by one or more indicators** [S1].

## Disability care — a thinner evidence base

> **Guidance on prescribing psychotropic medicines is more limited for people with intellectual and
> developmental disabilities due to a paucity of high-quality evidence in this population. Some evidence
> supports the use of risperidone and aripiprazole in reducing behaviours of concern in children with
> intellectual disability, and the use of atypical antipsychotics and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder
> medicines in reducing irritability in autism, although side effects are problematic** [S1].

**For people with cognitive disabilities, psychotropic medicines are frequently prescribed to manage behaviours
of concern *in the absence of a diagnosed mental illness*, and their ongoing use may no longer be appropriate
as circumstances evolve, or once behavioural and environmental strategies have taken effect** [S1].
